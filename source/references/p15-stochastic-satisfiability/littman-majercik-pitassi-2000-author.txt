Abstract. Satis ability problems and probabilistic models are core topics of ar-
ti cial intelligence and computer science. This paper looks at the rich intersection
between these two areas, opening the door for the use of satis ability approaches in
probabilistic domains. The paper examines a generic stochastic satis ability prob-
lem, SSat, which can function for probabilistic domains as Sat does for deterministic
domains. It shows the connection between SSat and well studied problems in belief
network inference and planning under uncertainty, and de nes algorithms, both
systematic and stochastic, for solving SSat instances. These algorithms are validated
on random SSat formulae generated under the xed-clause model. In spite of the
large complexity gap between SSat (PSPACE) and Sat (NP), the paper suggests
that much of what we have learned about Sat transfers to the probabilistic domain.


                               1. Introduction
There has been a recent focus in arti cial intelligence (AI) on solving
problems exhibiting various forms of uncertainty. In parallel, there is
a great deal of work in AI and computer science on solving determin-
istic problems using techniques for testing Boolean satis ability. Some
recent work has looked at combinations of these ideas, viewing plan-
ning under uncertainty as stochastic Boolean satis ability (Majercik &
Littman, 1998). This paper extends these ideas, and provides a general
approach for combining reasoning about uncertainty and satis ability.
    The remainder of this section reviews deterministic satis ability,
introduces the stochastic satis ability (SSat) framework, describes the
relationship between special cases of SSat and planning and reasoning
under uncertainty, and discusses the family of random SSat instances.

1.1. Deterministic Satisfiability
In the deterministic satis ability problem, or Sat, we are given a
Boolean formula and wish to determine whether there is some as-
signment to the variables in the formula that results in the formula
evaluating to True. This problem is connected to problems throughout
computer science, from circuit design and complexity theory to AI.
The last several years has seen tremendous progress in our ability to
solve Sat problems. In part due to the success of solving satis ability
problems, researchers are nding ecient ways of modeling diverse
problems within the satis ability framework, such as planning (Kautz
& Selman, 1996).

1.3. Satisfiability and Uncertainty
As AI techniques are used more and more to attack real-world prob-
lems, many researchers have embraced probability theory as a way of
representing the pervasive uncertainty they nd. Two speci c examples
of this trend are the increasing use of Markov decision process models in
planning and learning, and belief networks in reasoning and knowledge
representation; however, the in uence of probabilistic models in AI is
felt quite broadly.
    Whereas many basic deterministic problems are complete for the
well-known complexity class NP, and are therefore formally equivalent
to Sat, many planning and reasoning problems in probabilistic models
lie in other complexity classes, such as #P or PP (Roth, 1996; Littman,
Goldsmith, & Mundhenk, 1998). This means that, with respect to the
current state of complexity theory, these problems cannot be polyno-

mially reduced to Sat. However, many standard uncertain reasoning
problems can be reduced to special cases of SSat, as described next.
The probabilistic problems discussed are complete for NP, PP, NPPP ,
and PSPACE.
    The NP-complete problem Sat is the SSat problem obtained by
using only existential quanti ers and setting  = 1. The problem of nd-
ing the most probable explanation (MPE) in a belief network (Dechter,
1996) is (polynomially) equivalent to Sat. A related problem from
planning under uncertainty is determining whether there is some choice
of actions such that the probability of the most likely trajectory through
state space to the goal exceeds a given probability threshold.
    The class PP, or probabilistic polynomial time, can be de ned in
several ways. For the purposes of this paper, it is useful to de ne it
by one of its complete problems, Majsat. In its standard formulation,
Majsat asks whether, given a Boolean formula (x) in CNF, at least
half of its assignments are satisfying. Thus, Majsat is concerned, not
just with the existence of a satisfying assignment, but the number of
satisfying assignments.
    The Majsat problem connects satis ability to probability in the
sense that, if we imagine that all assignments are equally likely, Majsat
asks whether the probability of a satisfying assignment is at least 1=2.
Thus, Majsat can be expressed as an instance of SSat:

1.4. Random Formulae
To empirically evaluate algorithms for satis ability and stochastic sat-
is ability, it is useful to have a good testbed of hard formulae. This is a
nontrivial problem: speci c hand-coded instances of Sat are known to
be satis able or not, and usually for a clear (easily computable) reason.
What is sought is a natural distribution over Sat instances that is hard
on average. This has led many researchers to study randomly generated
formulae (although this distribution is not known to be average-case
hard). The usual xed-clause random k-CNF model is used in this
paper, which is de ned in terms of three integer parameters n; k and
m; a formula is generated by selecting m clauses of size k independently.
A clause is generated by randomly selecting one of n variables k times,
rejecting duplicate selections, and randomly negating it with probabil-
ity 1=2. This distribution is denoted Fmk;n. In the experiments described
in Section 3, a program called makewff, available from AT&T Labs {
Research, was used to generate random formulae from this distribution.
   The random k-CNF model has been widely studied for several good
reasons. First, it is an intrinsically natural model, analogous to the ran-
dom graph model, that sheds light on fundamental structural properties
of the satis ability problem. As with Sat, it is possible to gain insight
into the mathematical structure of SSat by looking at the behavior
of randomly generated formulae. Second, using an appropriate choice
of parameters, randomly chosen formulae are empirically dicult for
satis ability algorithms, and are a commonly used benchmark (Gu
et al., 1997). The next section brie y reviews some analytical results
concerning random Sat and Majsat instances.
1.4.1. Random Sat
An interesting property of random k-CNF formulae is that when m
is small relative to n, almost all formulae are satis able, and most
algorithms nd it easy to show this satis ability. When m is large

relative to n, almost all formulae are unsatis able and unsatis ability
is relatively easy to show. For intermediate values of m (around 4:2n

very dicult to show satis ability. Thus, with respect to m, random
Sat instances exhibit an \easy-hardest-hard" pattern. This pattern is
consistent for various values of n but becomes more pronounced with
larger n. In addition, the hard region is very large for large n.

that there exists a constant k such that a random k-CNF formula
from Fmk;n is almost certainly satis able for m=n < k (as n gets large),
and almost certainly unsatis able if m=n > k . As mentioned above,
empirical evidence (Selman, Mitchell, & Levesque, 1996) suggests that

of bounding the threshold value from above and below appear to be
asymmetric. At slightly below the 0{1 threshold, there are simple linear-

above the 0{1 threshold, there are no known algorithms that quickly
produce proofs of unsatis ability. Moreover, it has been proven that no
Resolution-based method (including the DPLL procedure that forms
the basis of several algorithms in this paper) will succeed in sub-
exponential time on a broad range of randomly generated formulae
(Chvatal & Reed, 1992; Beame & Pitassi, 1996; Beame et al., 1998).
In particular, Beame et al. (1998) show that for k = 3 and m 

a superpolynomial-size decision tree. This suggests that DPLL will per-

the 0{1 threshold (up to n6=5 ), and that the easy-hardest-hard pattern
observed empirically may actually be misleading|for large enough n,
the expected pattern is more like easy-hardest-...-hardest-easy. This
points out that when analyzing algorithms for random instances of
Sat it is important to know whether the algorithm is applied above,
below, or at the threshold.

          2. Algorithms for Stochastic Satis ability
The SSat problem is PSPACE complete, and therefore would seem to
require a di erent set of algorithmic approaches from those developed
for the NP-complete Sat. In fact, this section shows that the funda-
mental algorithmic ideas developed in the Sat community provide an
excellent starting point for SSat algorithms|the gap between NP and
PSPACE may not be as large as it seems.
   This section describes three algorithms for SSat. Section 2.1 devel-
ops a DPLL-based algorithm. It shows that the basic structure of the
Sat DPLL algorithm is applicable to Extended SSat.
   Section 2.2 describes another algorithm that is similar to the DPLL-
based algorithm, but that has a guarantee of being ecient with respect
to other decision-tree-based algorithms. This type of algorithm can be
viewed as a \universal" search algorithm for DPLL-based procedures.
The algorithm is guaranteed to solve the Extended SSat instance in
time that is ecient in the size of the smallest decision tree. As dis-
cussed in Section 4, although the theoretical performance of the uni-
versal algorithm is superior in the worst case, it does not perform as
well on random formulae as the DPLL algorithm.
   The algorithms in Section 2.1 and Section 2.2 are both sound and
complete algorithms: given an arbitrary Extended SSat instance (; ; Q),
both of these algorithms are guaranteed to return the correct answer,
although the running time can be exponential. As with Sat, incomplete
or approximate algorithms can often solve large problems more quickly
than complete algorithms like DPLL. For Sat, randomized local search
procedures like walksat have been shown to be successful for large
problem instances. Similarly, Majsat instances are very naturally ap-
proximated using random sampling. Section 2.3 describes a new SSat
algorithm that combines local search with random sampling to compute
approximate answers to SSat problems (existential and randomized
quanti ers only).
2.1. A Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland Algorithm
The Davis-Putnam-Logemann-Loveland (DPLL) algorithm for Boolean
satis ability (Davis, Logemann, & Loveland, 1962) works by enumer-

the formula. These simpli cations, or pruning rules, make it possible

Cadoli, Giovanardi, & Schaerf (1998) provide a set of pruning rules for
universal quanti ers (for solving QBF problems); the puri cation and
unit propagation pruning rules described here are introduced and jus-

to DPLL for counting satisfying assignments.

2.1.2. Puri cation and Thresholding
The puri cation pruning rules apply when there is a variable xi that
appears only with one sign b in . If Q(xi ) = 9, the algorithm assigns
xi = b and recurses. This is valid because any clause satis ed by an
assignment with xi = 1 b will also be satis ed by assigning xi = b. By
similar reasoning, if Q(xi ) = 8, the algorithm recurses after assigning
xi = 1 b. Interestingly, puri cation pruning does not appear possible
for randomized variables. Both assignments to a randomized variable
give some contribution to the value of the Extended SSat formula, and
must be considered independently.
     Another useful class of pruning rules concerns the threshold param-
eters l and h. While some care must be taken to pass meaningful
thresholds when applying unit resolution, threshold pruning mainly
comes into play when variables are split to try to prevent recursively
computing both assignments to x1 , the outermost quanti ed variable.

of the current formula with x1 set to False), it is possible that h has
already been exceeded. In this case, the algorithm can simply return
v0 , without ever computing v1 (the value of the current formula with
x1 set to True). In particular, it is possible that v1 > v0 , but all that
is signi cant is whether the larger of the two exceeds h . If v0 exceeds
l but falls short of h, this can be used to increase the lower threshold
for the recursive computation of v1 ; since the algorithm must take the
larger of v0 and v1 , the precise value of v1 is not needed if it less than
v0 .

for universal quanti ers.

though it can be done. There are two types of threshold pruning that

2.1.3. Complexity of evalssat
Not surprisingly, the worst-case running time of evalssat is exponential
in the size of the formula. The reason is that on even simple instances,
often the underlying decision tree is always large, and in addition,
pruning does not help. A particular example of this is applying evalssat
to unsatis able Sat formulae that are known to require exponential size
Resolution proofs. Since the algorithm (in this special case) produces a
Resolution refutation, the Resolution lower bounds for speci c formulae
(such as the propositional pigeonhole principle) imply exponential time
lower bounds on the running time of evalssat on these examples.
   It is also interesting to understand the average-case running time
of evalssat and its performance on more general formulae, such as in-
stances of Majsat or Alternating SSat. This question can be partially
answered in the context of random formulae, yielding results on the
complexity of the DPLL algorithm assuming no pruning rules, that is,
assuming that the algorithm needs to generate the entire decision tree
before terminating. This is interesting, since for many hard formulae
the entire decision tree must be generated in order to determine truth or
falsity of the formula, and, thus, lower bounds on the decision-tree size
yield lower bounds on the running time of the algorithm. The following
theorem follows from the results of Beame et al. (1998).
THEOREM 1. Let  be a 3-CNF formula chosen uniformly at random
from Fmk;n. For all n suciently large, for all m, n  m  n5=4 , with
high probability any decision tree for  requires exponential size in n.
    This theorem shows that for random formulae in a large range, the
decision-tree complexity of the underlying 3-CNF formula will be huge,
and therefore the running time of any decision-tree-based algorithm
without pruning will be exponential. To make matters worse, the DPLL
algorithm above searches for a decision tree in a very speci c way; that
is, it is not guaranteed to even search for the smallest tree. This leads to
the following interesting question: Given a CNF formula , is it possible
to eciently produce a decision tree for  that is close to an optimal
one? An armative answer to this question suggests a more ecient
algorithm for Sat, and moreover suggests that if one is using a decision-
tree-based method, that an \optimal" algorithm within this class exists.
The next section describes such an algorithm: it is guaranteed to nd
a close-to-optimal size tree eciently.

2.2. A Universal Search Algorithm for Extended SSat
As indicated earlier, it may be possible to determine whether a Majsat
instance (; ; Q) is positive without actually constructing the entire
decision tree, via pruning. On the other hand, to determine the exact
value of , the entire decision tree must be examined, and therefore
a lower bound on the best possible running time of the algorithm de-
scribed here will be a linear function of the size of the smallest decision
tree for . Thus, it is useful to understand how hard it is to search for
the smallest such decision tree.
    Let (; ; Q) be an instance of Extended SSat. A block is a maximal
set of variables that appear in the formula with the same quanti-
  er and are adjacent in the quanti er ordering. Note that, within a
block, quanti ers commute. In what follows, assume that (; ; Q) has
 consecutive blocks of variables X 1 ; : : : ; X  .

2.3. A Stochastic Sampling Algorithm
Randomized local search algorithms, like walksat (Kautz & Selman,
1996), have been extremely successful in solving dicult satis abil-
ity problems. walksat initially makes a random assignment to the
variables in the Sat formula. If this is not a satisfying assignment, it
randomly selects an unsatis ed clause. If it can satisfy this clause by
  ipping a variable without unsatisfying any other clauses, it does so.
If not, with probability 1/2, it randomly ips that variable that would
unsatisfy the fewest clauses. This process continues until a satisfying
assignment is found or until a speci ed maximum number of variables
have been ipped. This entire process can also be repeated a speci-
  ed number of times starting with a new random assignment. Clearly,
walksat is not complete; it may not nd a satisfying assignment when
one exists. Further, it cannot determine when no satisfying assignment
exists. But, compared to existing systematic solvers, randomized local
search algorithms can solve random satis ability problems that are
orders of magnitude larger (Selman, Kautz, & Cohen, 1996).
    The presence of randomly quanti ed variables in SSat problems,
however, makes it impossible to apply local search techniques directly
to SSat problems. A possible approach is suggested by an approxi-
mation technique for Majsat problems: stochastic sampling. In this
approach, some number of assignments to the random variables are
generated according to their probabilities, and then the probability that
the formula is satis able is estimated from that sample. This section
develops an approximation algorithm for SSat problems that combines
stochastic sampling to reduce the size of the problem (Section 2.3.2)
and randomized local search to solve the reduced problem eciently
(Section 2.3.3).

2.3.3. Randomized Local Search
The basic approach described in the previous section is to randomly
sample a set of assignments to be used to evaluate policies, then sys-
tematically search the space of policies to nd one with the best ap-
proximate value. Although, for many problems, the number of sam-
ples required is not too large, the search for the best policy will be
exponential in the number of existential variables in the formula.
   Randomized local search can be used to substantially reduce the
search time for a good policy, at the cost of nding one that is only
locally optimal. As described in Section 2.3.2, stochastic sampling can
be used to construct a partial policy tree and, given a policy, the partial




                                           paper.tex; 1/09/1999; 0:14; p.26
                        Stochastic Boolean Satis ability                  27
policy tree can be evaluated (its value is the proportion of the sample
corresponding to satis ed leaves). The goal is to search the space of
policies to nd one with high value.
    The randevalssat algorithm in Figure 6 takes SSat formula  and re-
turns an approximation of its value, along with the policy that approxi-
mately produces this value. The algorithm's local search component op-
erates by rst converting the partial policy tree to a two-level Boolean
formula, called a treeSAT formula (function construct treesat formula
in Figure 6). As illustrated in Figure 3, the e ect of a policy on a
leaf can be summarized by a Boolean formula, called a path formula,
for that leaf. Each random sample can potentially produce a di erent
path formula, and the union of all the path formulae produces the
treeSAT formula. Thus, on one level the treeSAT formula is a collection
of clauses, while on another level it is a collection of path formulae.
    A satisfying assignment to the treeSAT formula corresponds to a
setting of the decision variables that satis es the original SSat formula
along all paths in the partial policy tree. In general, it will not be possi-
ble to nd such a setting, so the challenge here is to nd an assignment
to the treeSAT formula that maximizes the number of path formulae
that are satis ed. This can be accomplished by randomized local search.
In the experiments in this paper, this is done by hillclimbing on an
objective function that counts both clauses satis ed and path formulae
satis ed. A satis ed path formula is weighted as heavily as the number
of clauses in the original SSat formula, so that satisfying all the clauses
in a path formula contributes more to the treeSAT formula's value than
satisfying the same number of clauses scattered over more than one
path formula. The function eval treesat formula calculates the value of
the treeSAT formula using this objective function.
    The operation of randevalssat is similar to that of walksat (Kautz
& Selman, 1996). The main di erence is in the objective function.
walksat searches for an assignment that maximizes the number of
satis ed clauses. As described above, the two-level structure of the
treeSAT formula requires randevalssat to use an objective function that
counts both clauses satis ed and path formulae satis ed. (In fact, in
the tests conducted, counting satis ed clauses did not seem to be as
important as counting satis ed path formulae; randevalssat performed
as well with an objective function that counted only satis ed path
formulae. Larger problems might exhibit di erent behavior.)
    Essentially, randevalssat randomly sets the values of the treeSAT
variables, then hillclimbs on the objective function described above.
On each iteration (up to a maximum of IterationLimit iterations), the
algorithm randomly chooses an unsatis ed clause. If it is possible to
improve the value of the objective function by ipping the truth value

of a variable in that clause, it does so. Otherwise, it calculates which
variable ip in that clause will lead to the least decrease in the value
of the objective function, and accepts that ip with a probability of
0.5. The algorithm returns the treeSAT assignment that produced the
highest value of the objective function over all iterations. If the algo-
rithm fails to make progress on a prespeci ed number of consecutive
iterations (FailureLimit) it restarts with a new random setting of the
treeSAT variables. A prespeci ed number of restarts (RestartLimit) is
allowed.

2.3.4. Related Work
Many other researchers have explored approximations for probability-
based problems. Roth (1996) studied the complexity of inference in
belief networks. He showed that computing the probability of a query
node (akin to evaluating a Majsat formula) is #P-complete. In ad-
dition, approximating the probability of a query node to within a
multiplicative factor of its true value is just as hard as computing the
exact value. In contrast, Lemma 4 shows that the complexity of getting
within an additive factor for Majsat is polynomial.
   Kearns, Mansour, & Ng (1999) show how approximately optimal ac-
tions can be chosen with high probability in in nite-horizon discounted
Markov decision processes. Their strategy is to show that it is sucient
to consider the rst H actions in the sequence, for an appropriate choice
of H . The space of all H -step action sequences is then evaluated using
random sampling. The sampleevalssat algorithm in Section 2.3.2 was
directly inspired by this work.
2.3.5. Summary
In summary, the stochastic sampling algorithm randevalssat appears to
be a promising approximation method for SSat problems. Its primary
strength is the use of sampling to convert the problem to a lower
complexity problem and its use of randomized local search to solve
that problem eciently. A feature of this process is that it does not
necessarily completely discard the probabilities of the original problem
(as would, say, a conversion that merely rounded o the probabilities of
the random variables to 0 and 1 and set their truth values accordingly).
It would, for example, be possible to modify the algorithm such that the
probabilities of the random variables are used to direct the construction
of the partial policy tree. A more substantial modi cation would be to
iteratively build the partial policy tree by using the solution of the
partial policy tree in a given iteration to construct a better partial
policy tree (and solution) in the next iteration.

threshold is m  4:2(n t). This relation makes sense for t = 0, since it
says that the 0{1 threshold for Sat occurs at m  4:2n. It also makes
sense for t = n, since only a single clause is needed to prevent there

bound on this threshold with this form, but no upper bound of this

3.3. An Evaluation of Heuristics for evalssat
In all but the most trivial Sat problems, the DPLL algorithm will
exhaust opportunities to apply unit resolution and puri cation before
a satisfying assignment has been discovered. When this occurs, the algo-
rithm must select an unassigned variable x from the current simpli ed
formula to split on, i.e. check for satisfying assignments both when x is
assigned True and when x is assigned False. For peak performance, the
order in which variables are selected is critical. For example, suppose a
Sat problem is presented to DPLL with n variables and, for the sake
of this example, that no pruning is done. Suppose further that if the
variable xa is set to True the formula becomes unsatis able. If xa is
the last variable the algorithm chooses to split on, it will potentially
generate 2n 1 assignments, discovering as many times that setting xa




                                                    paper.tex; 1/09/1999; 0:14; p.36
                       Stochastic Boolean Satis ability                 37
to True makes the formula unsatis able. If, however, it selects xa as the
  rst variable to split on, it will discover that setting that variable to
True leads to unsatis ability before generating and checking those 2n 1
assignments. Unit resolution, as described in Section 2.1.1, handles this
extreme case; other ideas are needed to address this more generally.
    Considerations such as this have prompted a great deal of research
into ecient splitting heuristics for Sat (Hooker & Vinay, 1994; Ba-
yardo & Schrag, 1997; Gallo & Urbani, 1989; Harche, Hooker, & Thomp-
son, 1994; Jeroslow & Wang, 1990; Li & Anbulagan, 1997; Crawford
& Auton, 1996; Freeman, 1995). An appropriate splitting heuristic can
reduce the running time of the DPLL algorithm by several orders of
magnitude. But, do these splitting heuristics improve eciency in SSat
problems?
    Relative to its counterpart in Sat problems, splitting in SSat is
restricted to selection from among a speci c class of variables: To ensure
that the value of the formula is computed correctly, splitting heuristics
for SSat must choose a variable from the rst (leftmost or outermost)
block, as described in Section 1.2.1.
    The reasoning behind this restriction is because adjacent quanti ers
commute if they are of the same type, but not if they are of di erent
types. Returning to the example from Section 1.2, consider the formula
               R R
                  x; z; 9y; E [(x _ y)(y _ z)(x _ y)]  3=5:
The value of the formula is 3=4, and this can be determined by splitting
either on x or z rst (both are in the rst block). On the other hand,
if y is used as the variable in the rst split, the value is calculated
incorrectly as 1=2.
    A hypothesis is that splitting heuristics would have the greatest
impact when there are large blocks of interchangeable variables (the
extremes being Sat and Majsat, both of which consist of a single
block of size n), and would not be as useful when there was a great deal
of quanti er alternation (since, barring unit clauses and pure variables,
DPLL must split on all variables in the rst block before splitting on
a variable from another block). In the case where blocks are single
variables, the splitting heuristic can be expected to have no impact on
performance.
    To test this hypothesis, tests were conducted using six splitting
heuristics (where, in all cases, it is understood that the choice is made
from the outermost block of variables):
      RAND chooses a variable randomly,
      SATS MOST nds the literal that satis es the most clauses in the
      current formula and chooses that variable,

With the exception of RAND, these heuristics try to select the variable
that will most simplify the formula and establish its satis ability (or

the current partial assignment is insucient to establish satisfaction or

the variable to True and calling itself recursively, and then setting the
variable to False and calling itself recursively. Whenever possible, as
in the case of a variable in a unit clause, one of these recursive calls is
eliminated. Measuring the number of calls to this function is equivalent
to measuring the size of the DPLL tree generated by the algorithm
and is thus a more direct measure of the eciency of the heuristic
than measuring, for example, CPU time. For perspective, however,
the running time, measured in CPU seconds, for a non-Majsat 24-
variable, 48-clause problem was typically less than a second. Running
times for Majsat problems varied from 1 to 5 seconds. A connection
between these two ways of measuring performance is provided by the
fact that for the results reported below (problems with 24 variables,
48 clauses, 3 literals per clause) the algorithm performs approximately
20,000 recursive calls per cpu second.

SATS MOST and MOMS were particularly e ective, reducing the num-

heuristic which might have demonstrated improved performance, was

on type E problems; both heuristics degraded performance, and this

2S JW performed no better than RAND agrees with results reported
by Hooker & Vinay (1994); on a set of hard, random Sat instances,
they reported that the DPLL trees constructed using the 2S JW heuris-

   For the heuristics that improved performance, the block size did
not have to be very large to produce signi cant reductions in the size
of the DPLL tree generated. For the four successful heuristics, tree
size was reduced as soon as blocks were larger than a single variable
and, in some cases, the reduction became signi cant even for relatively
small block sizes. MAX UNIT, SATS MOST, and MOMS, for example,
provided average reductions in tree size of approximately 23%, 28%,
and 32%, respectively, for a block size of six.
   The results suggest that a successful Sat heuristic is likely to be
a successful heuristic in Majsat and SSat problems, but block size
is a limiting factor. In the case where blocks are single variables, the
splitting heuristic has no impact on performance. It is important to
note, however, that the tests were conducted on small, random prob-
lems. For problems with more structure, such as SSat formulae that
encode planning problems, these results may not hold. In addition,
there may be other good splitting heuristics that take advantage of this
structure. For example, Majercik & Littman (1998) report improved
performance on such SSat problems using a time-ordered splitting
heuristic in which variables with a lower time index are chosen rst.
The success of SATS MOST, which is computationally much simpler
than MAX UNIT or MOMS, was somewhat surprising and warrants
further study. It is possible that performance disparities might appear
in these three splitting heuristics as the problem size increases beyond
that of the problems in these tests. Finally, it is possible that split-
ting heuristics unique to Extended SSat problems could be developed.
Such heuristics might use the probabilities associated with the random
variables to select variables in a way that provides potentially greater
simpli cation of the problem.

                 4. Conclusion and Future Work
This paper described a stochastic satis ability framework, relating it
to existing problems in reasoning and planning under uncertainty. It
described several algorithms, inspired by work in deterministic satis -
ability: a DPLL-based algorithm, a \universal" search algorithm, and
a stochastic sampling algorithm. The paper also explored the behavior
of the algorithms on randomly generated satis ability problems.
   This paper takes a rst stab at developing practical algorithms for
SSat, with particular emphasis on understanding which algorithms,
intuitions and ideas developed for the satis ability problem extend
to more general satis ability-type problems. In spite of the gap in
computational complexity between the general form of stochastic sat-
is ability (SSat, PSPACE-complete) and deterministic satis ability
(Sat, NP-complete), this work shows that many insights obtained from
studying Sat carry over to the more general probabilistic setting. Many
more questions remain unanswered; some directions for future work are
sketched next.




                                           paper.tex; 1/09/1999; 0:14; p.45
46                      Littman, Majercik, and Pitassi
   A primary motivation for studying Extended SSat problems is to
gain a better understanding of algorithms for decision making under
uncertainty. These problems are modeled by Extended SSat in that
existential quanti ers act like \maximizing" decisions, universal quan-
ti ers act like a \minimizing" decisions, and randomized quanti ers
act like an oblivious random in uence. Condon (1992) studied a simple
stochastic game model with precisely these three types of nodes. There
are two fundamental di erences between simple stochastic games and
stochastic satis ability. Stochastic games have no prede ned \horizon";
games can continue inde nitely. Extended SSat problems, in contrast,
model domains with a xed number of decisions or events (one for
each quanti er), at which point the \game" ends. On the other hand,
Extended SSat problems model very complex decisions, in that a value
for an existential quanti er can be conditioned on all the preceding ran-
domized quanti ers|possibly an exponential-sized set of conditions.
This allows Extended SSat problems to model compactly represented
AI planning domains, an insight that has led to an implemented state-
of-the-art planning algorithm (Majercik & Littman, 1999). Represent-
ing a planning problem via a simple stochastic game, however, leads
to a state-space explosion, rendering it an inecient formalism for
complex domains. An important direction for future work involves com-
bining the core propositional representation of Extended SSat with the
unbounded horizon of simple stochastic games.
   The study of randomized satis ability algorithms has helped spur
the development of algorithms and applications of deterministic satis-
  ability. While this paper has proposed an initial stochastic sampling
algorithm for SSat, more experiments and more algorithmic insights
are needed. One area in need of more research is how the approximation
algorithm can be applied to solve planning problems. In particular, the
algorithm returns a partial assignment strategy, or policy, that speci es
how each existential variable should be set given the settings of the vari-
ables that precede it in the quanti er ordering, but this only happens
for those situations represented by paths in the random sample. The
algorithm implicitly assumes that performance on missing branches
is the same as the average performance over all paths in the partial
tree, but doesn't actually nd a plan that achieves this performance. A
topic for future work is extending the stochastic sampling algorithm to
return successful plans, perhaps in an iterative approach that integrates
planning and execution.
   In addition, it is not clear how to apply the proposed algorithm
to formulae with intermixed existential, randomized, and universal
quanti ers. Such problems arise, for example, in applications in which
two competitive agents interact with a stochastic environment, and a




                                           paper.tex; 1/09/1999; 0:14; p.46
                       Stochastic Boolean Satis ability                 47
randomized algorithm for this broader class of SSat problems would
be useful.
   The proposed universal search algorithm is a promising approach to
solving Extended SSat problems exactly and eciently. Future work
will be to implement and test the algorithm, comparing it to the split-
ting heuristics examined in Section 3.3. Although they are not well
understood theoretically, the threshold pruning rules described in Sec-
tion 2.1.2 could be helpful in a practical implementation of the universal
search algorithm.
   There has been a recent urry of activity in analyzing common
Davis-Putnam algorithms, and proving that these algorithms are guar-
anteed to solve any k-SAT instance in subexponential-time (Monien &
Speckenmeyer, 1985; Paturi, Pudlak, & Zane, 1997; Paturi et al., 1998).
(For example, a very simple randomized DPLL-type algorithm solves
3-CNF Sat in worst-case time 2(2=3)n .) It would be interesting to show
that these analyses carry over to the SSat setting.
   In addition to Resolution-based methods, there are several alterna-
tive methods for solving satis ability instances. In particular, algebraic
approaches based on discrete versions of the Grobner basis algorithm
are looking promising for solving satis ability problems (Clegg, Ed-
monds, & Impagliazzo, 1996). Future work will examine to what extent
these methods carry over to the SSat domain.
   A number of recent studies have shown that the satis ability frame-
work is useful for examining a variety of search problems. An exciting
element of the present work is that it illustrates how the satis ability
approach applies outside of Sat's complexity class, greatly expanding
the realm of problems that can be attacked.
