\section{Readability and valuations over an arbitrary set of prime formulas}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-prime-readability}

Barwise sets up propositional logic over an arbitrary set \(P\) of
\emph{prime formulas} and extends each truth assignment
\(v:P\to\{\mathbf t,\mathbf f\}\) to a function \(\bar v\) on all
propositional formulas of \(P\) ``by induction on length of formulas''
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, pp.~23--24]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}; his definitions are
reproduced in \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-propformula} and
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-truth-assignment}.  That recursion defines a
function only if every propositional formula of \(P\) is built in exactly
one way, and the chapter neither states nor proves such a property.  The
handbook's readability theorem \cref{thm:prop-unique-readability} does not
settle the point either: it concerns the countable alphabet of
\cref{sec:prop-syntax-semantics-01}, with variables \(p_n\) and the constant
\(\bot\), whereas Barwise allows \(P\) to be any set, for example a set of
sentences of a natural language, and in his application takes \(P\) to be a
set of first-order formulas, which are themselves strings containing
connectives and parentheses \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, pp.~23, 28]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.

\emph{Purpose.}  This section states the assumption under which Barwise's
recursion is sound, proves readability under it, shows by an example that
it can fail without the assumption, and proves readability in Barwise's own
application to first-order formulas.  It then derives the recursion theorem
that makes \(\bar v\) a function, the coincidence property behind truth
tables, and the correctness of the truth-table test for tautologies and
consistency that Barwise asserts without proof
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~25]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
\emph{Inputs.}  Barwise's definitions of propositional formula, truth
assignment, tautology and consistency
(\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-propformula,def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-truth-assignment,def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-tautology})
and, for the application, his first-order syntax
(\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-syntax}).
\emph{Conclusions.}
\Cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-readability,thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton,thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-recursion,thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-coincidence,thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-truth-table}.
Throughout, \(\circ\) ranges over the binary connectives
\(\land,\lor,\to\), and the length of an expression is its number of
entries.

\subsection{Prime formulas as single symbols}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-letters}

Barwise calls a propositional formula of \(P\) an \emph{expression} and
takes the propositional formulas to be the smallest set of expressions that
contains the members of \(P\) and is closed under forming \(\neg A\),
\((A\land B)\), \((A\lor B)\) and \((A\to B)\)
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~23]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  When the members of
\(P\) are arbitrary objects, the natural reading treats each of them as one
symbol.

\begin{hbconvention}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-reading}{Expressions over a set of prime formulas}
Let \(P\) be a set with no member among the six symbols
\(\neg,\land,\lor,\to,(,)\).  An \emph{expression of \(P\)} is a finite
sequence each of whose entries is a member of \(P\) or one of these six
symbols.  A member \(p\in P\) is identified with the expression of length
\(1\) whose only entry is \(p\), and \(\neg A\), \((A\circ B)\) denote
concatenations.  The propositional formulas of \(P\) are then those of
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-propformula}.  No restriction is placed on the
cardinality of \(P\).
\end{hbconvention}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-readability}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-readability}{Unique readability over an arbitrary set of prime formulas}
Under \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-reading}, every propositional
formula \(A\) of \(P\) is exactly one of the following: a member of \(P\);
\(\neg B\) for a propositional formula \(B\); \((B\circ D)\) for a binary
connective \(\circ\) and propositional formulas \(B,D\).  In the second case
\(B\) is uniquely determined by \(A\), and in the third case \(\circ\),
\(B\) and \(D\) are.  Moreover no propositional formula is a proper initial
segment of another.
\end{hbtheorem}

The proof in the annex compares two formulas symbol by symbol from the
left, using induction on the length of the shorter one; it does not use the
parenthesis count of \cref{lem:paren-count}, and it needs no enumeration of
\(P\), so it applies to uncountable sets of prime formulas, the case used in
the second proof of compactness and in the reduction of first-order logic
(\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-prop-compactness,thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-main-lemma}).

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-string-primes}{Compound strings as prime formulas}
The disjointness assumption cannot simply be dropped when the members of
\(P\) are strings over the same symbols.  Let \(p,q\) be distinct letters
and let \(P=\{p,q,(p\land q)\}\), where \((p\land q)\) is the string of
five symbols.  If expressions are formed by concatenating strings, the
string \((p\land q)\) is a propositional formula of \(P\) in two ways: as a
member of \(P\), and as the conjunction of the members \(p\) and \(q\).
Take the truth assignment with \(v(p)=\mathbf f\), \(v(q)=\mathbf t\) and
\(v((p\land q))=\mathbf t\).  The clause for prime formulas demands
\(\bar v((p\land q))=\mathbf t\); the clause for \(\land\) demands
\(\bar v((p\land q))=\mathbf f\), because \(\bar v(p)=\mathbf f\).  So no
function satisfies all of Barwise's clauses for this \(v\), and the
recursion ``on length of formulas'' has no solution.  Readability is
therefore a hypothesis on \(P\), not a consequence of the definition.
Barwise's remark that prime formulas might be sentences of a natural
language \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~23]{Barwise1977FirstOrder} is covered by
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-reading} when each sentence is treated as
one symbol; the case where the prime formulas are first-order formulas is
settled by \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton}.
\end{hbwarning}

\subsection{Barwise's application: first-order formulas as propositional formulas}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-skeleton}

In the application, \(P\) is the set of formulas of a first-order language
\(L\) that are atomic or begin with a quantifier, and a \emph{tautology of
first-order logic} is a formula of \(L\) that is true under every truth
assignment to this \(P\) \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~28]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
In Barwise's official notation the quantified formulas are
\((\exists v\varphi)\) and \((\forall v\varphi)\), with an outer pair of
parentheses, an equation is \((t_1=t_2)\), and a term built by an \(n\)-ary
function symbol is \(f(t_1\cdots t_n)\), without separating commas
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, Definitions~3.2--3.4, pp.~18--19]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
So three kinds of formula begin with a left parenthesis, and a prime
formula is a string that contains connectives.  The next theorem shows
that the propositional structure is nevertheless unambiguous.

Barwise lets \(L\) be a set of function, relation and constant symbols,
each function and relation symbol carrying a positive integer arity
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, p.~17]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  The theorem assumes in
addition what his formation rules tacitly require and the chapter does not
state: the logical symbols \(\land,\lor,\neg,\to,=,\forall,\exists\), the
variables, the two parentheses and the members of \(L\) are pairwise
distinct symbols, and no member of \(L\) is of two kinds.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-read}{Readability of first-order formulas over Barwise's prime formulas}
Let \(L\) be a language as just described, with terms, atomic formulas and
formulas as in \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-syntax}, and let \(P\) be the
set of formulas of \(L\) that are atomic or of the form \((\exists v\varphi)\)
or \((\forall v\varphi)\).  Form propositional formulas of \(P\) as in
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-propformula}, with concatenation of strings of
symbols.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The propositional formulas of \(P\) are exactly the formulas of \(L\).
\item Every formula of \(L\) is exactly one of: a member of \(P\);
      \(\neg\varphi\) for a formula \(\varphi\); \((\varphi\circ\psi)\) for a
      binary connective \(\circ\) and formulas \(\varphi,\psi\).  In the
      second case \(\varphi\) is uniquely determined, and in the third case
      \(\circ\), \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are.
\item No term of \(L\) is a proper initial segment of another term, and no
      formula of \(L\) is a proper initial segment of another formula.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Clause~2 is the property that the warning
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-string-primes} shows can fail for strings in
general.  Here it holds because the second symbol separates the three
kinds of formula that begin with a left parenthesis: after the left
parenthesis an equation continues with the first symbol of a term (a
variable, a constant symbol or a function symbol), a quantified formula
with \(\exists\) or \(\forall\), and a binary compound with the first symbol
of a formula (a left parenthesis, a relation symbol or \(\neg\)).

\subsection{The recursion, prime constituents and truth tables}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-recursion}

Say that a set of propositional formulas of \(P\) \emph{reads uniquely} if
each member is exactly one of a member of \(P\), \(\neg B\), or \((B\circ D)\),
with the components uniquely determined.  By
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-readability,thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton},
this holds under \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-reading} and in Barwise's
application.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-recursion}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-recursion}{Recursion on propositional formulas}
Suppose the propositional formulas of \(P\) read uniquely.  Let \(Y\) be a
set and let \(g_0:P\to Y\), \(g_\neg:Y\to Y\) and
\(g_\circ:Y\times Y\to Y\) for each binary connective \(\circ\) be
functions.  There is exactly one function \(h\) on the propositional
formulas of \(P\) such that \(h(p)=g_0(p)\) for \(p\in P\),
\(h(\neg B)=g_\neg(h(B))\), and \(h((B\circ D))=g_\circ(h(B),h(D))\).
In particular each truth assignment \(v:P\to\{\mathbf t,\mathbf f\}\) has
exactly one extension \(\bar v\) satisfying Barwise's clauses of
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-truth-assignment}.
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-constituents}{Prime constituents, recursively}
Barwise describes the prime constituents of \(A\) as the prime formulas out
of which \(A\) is built \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~23]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
When the propositional formulas of \(P\) read uniquely,
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-recursion} with \(Y\) the set of finite subsets
of \(P\) makes this precise: the prime constituents of \(p\in P\) form the
set \(\{p\}\); those of \(\neg B\) are those of \(B\); those of \((B\circ D)\)
are those of \(B\) together with those of \(D\).  Every formula has finitely
many prime constituents.
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-coincidence}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-coincidence}{Coincidence on prime constituents}
Suppose the propositional formulas of \(P\) read uniquely.  If two truth
assignments \(v,w:P\to\{\mathbf t,\mathbf f\}\) agree on the prime
constituents of \(A\), then \(\bar v(A)=\bar w(A)\).
\end{hblemma}

This is Barwise's statement that truth tables show ``how the truth or
falsity of a propositional formula depends on the truth or falsity of its
prime constituents'' \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~24]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, in
the form needed when \(P\) is infinite; for the handbook's countable
alphabet it is \cref{lem:prop-coincidence}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-truth-table}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-tables}{The truth-table test}
Suppose the propositional formulas of \(P\) read uniquely, and let the prime
constituents of \(A\) be the distinct \(q_1,\ldots,q_k\).  Call a function
\(r:\{q_1,\ldots,q_k\}\to\{\mathbf t,\mathbf f\}\) a \emph{row}, and let the
value of \(A\) at \(r\) be \(\bar v(A)\) for any truth assignment \(v\)
extending \(r\).  This value is well defined; there are \(2^k\) rows; \(A\)
is a tautology in the sense of \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-tautology} if
and only if its value at every row is \(\mathbf t\); and \(A\) is
consistent if and only if its value at some row is \(\mathbf t\).
\end{hbtheorem}

The theorem justifies Barwise's claim that the method of truth tables makes
it ``a trivial matter'' to decide whether a propositional formula is a
tautology or consistent \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~25]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}:
the test inspects \(2^k\) rows, and computes each row's value from the
clauses of \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-truth-assignment} applied to the
subformulas, which is what the columns of his tables record
(\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-table-two,def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-table-three}).
It decides tautologyhood of a single formula; it does not decide whether an
infinite set of formulas is consistent, which is the question
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-prop-compactness} reduces to finite subsets.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-sample-tautologies}{Barwise's two sample tautologies}
Barwise introduces tautologies by two formulas that come out true whatever
the truth values of their prime constituents, \((p\lor\neg p)\) and
\((((p\to q)\land\neg q)\to\neg p)\), true ``by virtue of their syntactic
form alone'' \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~24]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
\emph{Hypotheses.}  The prime formulas are letters, as in his example
\(P=\{p,q,r\}\) \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~23]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, with
\(p\neq q\); so \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-reading} holds, the
formulas of \(P\) read uniquely by
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-letter-readability}, and
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-truth-table} applies.  The parentheses are
Barwise's: each binary compound carries its own pair, which is what makes
the decomposition below the only one.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(A_1=(p\lor\neg p)\) is \((B\lor D)\) with \(B=p\) and
      \(D=\neg p\).  By \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-constituents} its prime
      constituents form the set \(\{p\}\), so there are \(2^1=2\) rows.
      At \(p\mapsto\mathbf t\), \(\bar v(p)=\mathbf t\) already makes the
      disjunction \(\mathbf t\).  At \(p\mapsto\mathbf f\), the clause for
      \(\neg\) gives \(\bar v(\neg p)=\mathbf t\), and the disjunction is
      \(\mathbf t\).  Both rows give \(\mathbf t\), so \(A_1\) is a
      tautology.  It is the excluded middle of
      \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-tautology} with \(A=p\).
\item \(A_2=(((p\to q)\land\neg q)\to\neg p)\) is \((C\to\neg p)\) with
      \(C=((p\to q)\land\neg q)\), and \(C\) is \((E\land\neg q)\) with
      \(E=(p\to q)\).  Its prime constituents form the set \(\{p,q\}\), so
      there are \(2^2=4\) rows.  The clauses for \(\to\), \(\neg\) and
      \(\land\) give the columns
      \begin{center}
      \begin{tabular}{cc|ccccc}
      \(p\) & \(q\) & \((p\to q)\) & \(\neg q\) & \(C\) & \(\neg p\) & \(A_2\)\\
      \hline
      \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf t\)\\
      \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf t\)\\
      \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\)\\
      \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf f\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\) & \(\mathbf t\)
      \end{tabular}
      \end{center}
      and the last column has no \(\mathbf f\), so \(A_2\) is a tautology.
      Read backward, as in \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-table-three}: a
      row giving \(\mathbf f\) would need \(\bar v(C)=\mathbf t\) and
      \(\bar v(\neg p)=\mathbf f\), so \(p\mapsto\mathbf t\); \(C\) true
      needs \(\bar v(\neg q)=\mathbf t\), so \(q\mapsto\mathbf f\); then
      \(\bar v(p\to q)=\mathbf f\) and \(C\) is false after all.  The
      formula expresses the inference from \(p\to q\) and \(\neg q\) to
      \(\neg p\).
\end{enumerate}
Both checks use only the clauses of
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-truth-assignment} for the connectives that
occur, and the values at the rows do not depend on how \(v\) is defined
off \(\{p\}\) or \(\{p,q\}\), by \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-coincidence}.
The first formula of \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-tautologies} has the
form of \(A_1\) with the prime formula \(\forall x\,R(x)\) in place of
\(p\); its table is checked there directly.
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-tautologies}{Prime constituents of first-order formulas}
Barwise gives five sentences to separate the two notions.  He lists
\(\forall x\,R(x)\lor\neg\forall x\,R(x)\) and
\(\neg(\forall x\,R(x)\land\exists x\,S(x))\leftrightarrow
(\neg\forall x\,R(x)\lor\neg\exists x\,S(x))\) as tautologies, and
\((c=c)\), \(\forall x\,(R(x)\lor\neg R(x))\) and
\(\neg\exists x\,S(x)\to\forall x\,\neg S(x)\) as sentences that are not
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~28]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}; here
\(A\leftrightarrow B\) abbreviates \((A\to B)\land(B\to A)\)
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~25]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, and the formulas are
written in the informal notation, outer parentheses of quantified formulas
dropped.  By \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton}, each has a unique
decomposition into members of \(P\), so
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-truth-table} applies.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The first has the single prime constituent \(a=\forall x\,R(x)\) and
      the form \(a\lor\neg a\); both rows give \(\mathbf t\).
\item The second has the two prime constituents \(a=\forall x\,R(x)\) and
      \(b=\exists x\,S(x)\) and the form
      \(\neg(a\land b)\leftrightarrow(\neg a\lor\neg b)\), the first de
      Morgan law of \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-tautology}; all four rows
      give \(\mathbf t\).
\item \((c=c)\) is atomic and \(\forall x\,(R(x)\lor\neg R(x))\) begins with a
      quantifier, so each is a member of \(P\) and is its own only prime
      constituent; the row assigning \(\mathbf f\) to it gives
      \(\mathbf f\).  The connectives inside \(\forall x\,(R(x)\lor\neg R(x))\)
      are invisible to the test, because by clause~2 of
      \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton} the formula is not of the
      form \(\neg\varphi\) or \((\varphi\circ\psi)\).
\item \(\neg\exists x\,S(x)\to\forall x\,\neg S(x)\) has the two distinct
      prime constituents \(b=\exists x\,S(x)\) and
      \(c'=\forall x\,\neg S(x)\) and the form \(\neg b\to c'\); the row
      \(b\mapsto\mathbf f\), \(c'\mapsto\mathbf f\) gives \(\mathbf f\).
\end{enumerate}
The three non-tautologies are nevertheless true in every structure under
every assignment: a tautology of first-order logic is a much narrower
notion than validity, which is the point Barwise draws from these examples
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~28]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
\end{hbexample}

\emph{Relationships.}  For the countable alphabet with \(\bot\), the
corresponding facts are \cref{thm:prop-unique-readability} and
\cref{lem:prop-coincidence}; the present results extend them to Barwise's
arbitrary \(P\) and to his strings of first-order symbols.  The handbook's
own first-order grammar, with its readability theorem
\cref{thm:fol-unique-readability}, uses a different notation; clause~3 of
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton} is proved directly for Barwise's
notation.  \Cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-fo-skeleton} is used implicitly
wherever Barwise's reduction applies truth assignments to first-order
formulas, as in \cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-lemma-43} and
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-main-lemma}.

\section{Completeness of the cut-free system G and Barwise's Cut-Elimination Theorem}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness}

Barwise proves the Completeness Theorem for \(\mathbf G^{+}\)
(\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-completeness-g}) through the Main Lemma, and
that proof uses the cut rule heavily.  He then states Gentzen's
Cut-Elimination Theorem, his 4.14: every sequent derivable with cut is
derivable without it, so \(\mathbf G^{+}\) and \(\mathbf G\) derive the same
sequents.  As one way to see this he points to a direct proof that the
cut-free system \(\mathbf G\) is complete, obtained by working with
\(\mathbf G\) itself and expanding his proof of the propositional case 4.12,
with Smullyan's book as reference \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~40]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.

\emph{Purpose.}  This section carries out that route for \(\mathbf G\) as
Barwise defines it: sequents of finite sets, his equality axioms and
equality rule, and his four quantifier rules with the condition on the
variable \(v\) in \((\vdash\forall)\) and \((\exists\vdash)\)
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, pp.~37, 39]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
\emph{Inputs.}  The rules of \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-sequents} and
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-system-g} (collected in the calculus sheet of
\cref{sec:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-gentzen}), Barwise's syntax, satisfaction and
substitution
(\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-syntax,def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-satisfaction,def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-substitution}),
and the soundness of \(\mathbf G^{+}\) established in the proof of
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-completeness-g}.
\emph{Conclusions.}  \Cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness} (every valid
sequent that separates its variables has a cut-free derivation) and
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-cut-elimination} (Barwise's 4.14 for those
sequents, and the Completeness Theorem with \(\mathbf G\) in place of
\(\mathbf G^{+}\)).  The hypothesis on variables is discussed in
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-limit}.  Without it the answer depends on
whether bound variables may be renamed:
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-clean-failure} shows that 4.14 fails when they
may not, and \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant-cut-elimination} proves
4.14 for every sequent when they may.

\subsection{Validity of sequents and the reading of the rules}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-setup}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-valid}{Valid sequents; sequents that separate their variables}
Let \(L\) be a language and \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) a sequent of formulas of
\(L\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) is \emph{valid} if, for every structure
      \(\mathfrak M\) for \(L\) and every assignment \(s\) in \(\mathfrak M\)
      that satisfies every member of \(\Gamma\), some member of \(\Delta\) is
      satisfied by \(s\).  This makes exact, by
      \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-satisfaction}, Barwise's informal reading
      of a sequent: the conjunction of the left side yields the disjunction
      of the right side \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~37]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
      For a finite set \(\Gamma\) of sentences and a sentence \(\varphi\),
      \(\Gamma\vdash\{\varphi\}\) is valid iff \(\Gamma\models\varphi\).
\item A variable is \emph{quantified in} a set of formulas if
      \(\forall v\) or \(\exists v\) occurs in one of its members.  The
      \emph{scope} of the displayed quantifier in \((\forall v\theta)\) or
      \((\exists v\theta)\) is \(\theta\).
\item \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) \emph{separates its variables} if (a) no
      variable quantified in \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\) occurs free in a member of
      \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\), and (b) in no member of \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\) does
      a quantifier on a variable \(y\) occur inside the scope of another
      quantifier on the same variable \(y\).
\end{enumerate}
A sequent of sentences satisfies (a) automatically; it separates its
variables iff no sentence in it nests a quantifier on \(y\) inside the scope
of a quantifier on \(y\).  For example \(\forall x\,R(x)\vdash\forall y\,R(y)\)
and \(\emptyset\vdash(\forall y\,R(y)\lor\exists y\,\neg R(y))\) separate
their variables; \(R(y)\vdash\forall y\,R(y)\) violates (a), and
\(\emptyset\vdash\forall y\,(R(y)\lor\neg\forall y\,R(y))\) violates (b).
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbconvention}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-reading}{How instances of the rules of G are read}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Sets.}  \(\Gamma,\varphi\) is \(\Gamma\cup\{\varphi\}\)
      \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~37]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, and nothing
      prevents \(\varphi\in\Gamma\).  So an instance of a rule may have its
      principal formula already in the side set: from
      \(\Gamma,\varphi,\psi\vdash\Delta\) with \((\varphi\land\psi)\in\Gamma\),
      rule \((\land\vdash)\) gives \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\).  The proofs below
      use only such instances, so they need neither weakening nor
      contraction.
\item \emph{Substitution.}  \(\varphi(t)\) is \(\varphi(t/v)\), the result
      of replacing the free occurrences of \(v\) by \(t\), and is written
      only when no variable of \(t\) occurs bound in \(\varphi(v)\)
      (\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-substitution};
      \cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, p.~22]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}).  Call such a
      substitution \emph{clean}.  Every substitution used below is clean,
      so no bound variable is ever renamed.  Concretely,
      \(v(t/v)=t\), \(w(t/v)=w\) for a variable \(w\neq v\), \(c(t/v)=c\) for
      a constant symbol \(c\), and
      \(f(t_1\cdots t_n)(t/v)=f(t_1(t/v)\cdots t_n(t/v))\); on formulas the
      operation acts on every term of an atomic formula, commutes with the
      connectives, leaves \((\forall v\sigma)\) and \((\exists v\sigma)\)
      unchanged, and sends \((Qw\sigma)\) to \((Qw\,\sigma(t/v))\) for a
      quantifier \(Q\) and a variable \(w\neq v\).
\item \emph{Parameters.}  In the quantifier rules and the equality rule the
      displayed variable is the one substituted for; the formulas
      \(\varphi(v)\), \(\psi(x)\) may contain other free variables, as do
      the quantifier axioms \(\forall v\,\varphi(v)\to\varphi(t)\) of
      \(\mathbf H\) (\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-system-h}).  In the
      equality rule the formula \(\varphi(x)\) may also lack \(x\); then
      \(\varphi(t_1)\) and \(\varphi(t_2)\) are both \(\varphi(x)\).
\item \emph{Generalized variable.}  In \((\vdash\forall)\) and
      \((\exists\vdash)\), with premise formula \(\varphi(v)\) and conclusion
      formula \(\forall y\,\varphi(y)\) or \(\exists y\,\varphi(y)\), the
      variable \(y\) is \(v\) itself or does not occur in \(\varphi(v)\), as
      in \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-system-h}; the variable \(v\) may not
      occur free in \(\Gamma\cup\Delta\)
      \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~39]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbconvention}

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-substitution}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-substitution}{Clean substitution}
Let \(\theta\) be a formula, \(v\) a variable and \(t\) a term such that no
variable of \(t\) occurs bound in \(\theta\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\theta(t/v)\) has the same quantifier occurrences as \(\theta\), on
      the same variables, and the same number of occurrences of connectives
      and quantifiers; a quantifier occurrence lies in the scope of another
      in \(\theta(t/v)\) iff it does in \(\theta\).  The free variables of
      \(\theta(t/v)\) are those of \(\theta\) if \(v\notin\FV(\theta)\), and
      \((\FV(\theta)-\{v\})\cup\operatorname{Var}(t)\) otherwise, where
      \(\operatorname{Var}(t)\) is the set of variables of \(t\).
\item If the variable \(u\) does not occur in \(\theta\) and the variable
      \(y\) does not occur bound in \(\theta\), then
      \(\theta(u/y)(y/u)=\theta\), both substitutions being clean.
\item If the variable \(z\) does not occur in the term \(\tau\), then
      \(\tau(w/x)(r/z)=\tau(w(r/z)/x)\) for all terms \(w,r\) and every
      variable \(x\); the same holds with an atomic formula in place of
      \(\tau\).
\item For every structure \(\mathfrak M\) and assignment \(s\),
      \(\mathfrak M\models\theta(t/v)[s]\) iff
      \(\mathfrak M\models\theta[s(\tfrac{b}{v})]\), where
      \(b=t^{\mathfrak M}(s)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hblemma}

\subsection{The construction and the completeness theorem}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-theorem}

\begin{hbmethod}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-search}{Saturating a sequent that has no cut-free derivation}
\emph{Input:} a sequent \(\Gamma_0\vdash\Delta_0\) of \(L\) that separates
its variables and has no derivation in \(\mathbf G\).  Let \(B\) be the set
of variables quantified in \(\Gamma_0\cup\Delta_0\), \(L_0\) the finite set
of symbols of \(L\) occurring in \(\Gamma_0\cup\Delta_0\), and \(U\) the
set of terms of \(L_0\) none of whose variables lies in \(B\).  Fix a list
of all \emph{tasks}: pairs \((\chi,t)\) of a formula of \(L_0\) and a term in
\(U\), and quadruples \((a,b,\psi,x)\) of terms \(a,b\in U\), an atomic
formula \(\psi\) of \(L_0\) with terms in \(U\), and a variable \(x\notin B\);
and a schedule that visits each task at infinitely many stages.

\emph{Stage \(n\).}  Given \(\Gamma_n\vdash\Delta_n\) without a derivation in
\(\mathbf G\), take the task scheduled at stage \(n\).
\begin{itemize}
\item For \((\chi,t)\) with \(\chi\in\Gamma_n\): apply the left rule for the
      main symbol of \(\chi\) backwards with side sets \(\Gamma_n,\Delta_n\).
      A rule with two premises leaves a choice; take a premise without a
      derivation in \(\mathbf G\), which exists because derivations of both
      premises would give one of \(\Gamma_n\vdash\Delta_n\).  For
      \(\chi=\forall v\theta\) the new formula is \(\theta(t/v)\); for
      \(\chi=\exists y\theta\) it is \(\theta(u/y)\) with \(u\) the first
      variable outside \(B\) that occurs in no member of
      \(\Gamma_n\cup\Delta_n\).  For \(\chi\in\Delta_n\) use the right rules
      in the same way.  An atomic \(\chi\), or a \(\chi\) in neither set,
      changes nothing.
\item For \((a,b,\psi,x)\): if \((a=b)\) or \((b=a)\) is in \(\Gamma_n\) and
      \(\psi(b/x)\in\Delta_n\), add \(\psi(a/x)\) to the right side, by the
      equality rule read backwards; otherwise change nothing.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Output:} the unions \(\Gamma_\omega\), \(\Delta_\omega\) of the
increasing sequences; the relation \(a\approx b\) on \(U\), which holds when
\(a\) and \(b\) can replace each other in every atomic member of
\(\Delta_\omega\); and the structure on \(U/{\approx}\) in which a relation
symbol \(R\) holds of the classes of \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) iff
\(R(a_1\cdots a_n)\in\Gamma_\omega\), with each variable outside \(B\)
assigned its own class.
\emph{Side conditions:} the fresh variable \(u\) is the variable required
by \((\exists\vdash)\) and \((\vdash\forall)\) not to occur free in
\(\Gamma\cup\Delta\); every substitution is clean because terms of \(U\)
avoid \(B\).  The choice of premise depends on whether a sequent has a
derivation, so the construction is a proof device, not an algorithm.
\end{hbmethod}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness}{Completeness of the cut-free system G}
Let \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) be a sequent of a language \(L\) that separates
its variables.  If \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) is valid, then it has a derivation
in \(\mathbf G\), that is, one without the cut rule.  Conversely every
sequent with a derivation in \(\mathbf G\) is valid.
\end{hbtheorem}

The proof in the annex runs the construction of
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-search}: if \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) has no
derivation in \(\mathbf G\), the structure built from \(\Gamma_\omega\) and
\(\Delta_\omega\), with the assignment sending each variable outside \(B\) to
its class, satisfies every member of \(\Gamma\) and no member of \(\Delta\).
The propositional stages are the backward rule applications of Barwise's
proof of 4.12, and the final step generalizes his truth assignment that is
\(\mathbf t\) on the left and \(\mathbf f\) on the right of an end node
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~38]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  The quantifier rules
need the schedule, because \(\forall v\,\theta\) on the left must be
instantiated by every term of \(U\).  The equality rule enters only through
its backward use on the right side, which is enough to make \(\approx\) a
congruence that identifies \(a\) and \(b\) whenever \((a=b)\) is on the left;
the equality axioms ensure that no \((a=a)\) is on the right.

\begin{hbcorollary}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-cut-elimination}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-cutfree}{Cut-Elimination for sequents that separate their variables (Barwise, 4.14)}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A sequent that separates its variables has a derivation in
      \(\mathbf G^{+}\) iff it has a derivation in \(\mathbf G\).
\item Let \(T\) be a set of sentences and \(\varphi\) a sentence of \(L\),
      none of which nests a quantifier on a variable inside the scope of a
      quantifier on the same variable.  Then \(T\models\varphi\) iff
      \(\Gamma\vdash\{\varphi\}\) has a derivation in \(\mathbf G\) for some
      finite \(\Gamma\subseteq T\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbcorollary}

Part~1 is Barwise's 4.14 \cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~40]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}
for every sequent that separates its variables.  Part~2 is the
Completeness Theorem 4.13 with \(\mathbf G\) in place of \(\mathbf G^{+}\),
for theories whose sentences meet the nesting condition; its proof uses
4.13 itself to pass from \(T\) to a finite subset.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-example}{A cut-free derivation using the equality rule}
Let \(c,d\) be constant symbols and \(P\) a binary relation symbol, and
write \(\Gamma=\{(c=d),\forall x\,P(xc)\}\).  The sequent
\(\Gamma\vdash\{\exists y\,P(yd)\}\) is valid: if \(c\) and \(d\) denote the
same element \(e\) and every element bears \(P\) to \(e\), then \(e\) bears
\(P\) to the value of \(d\).  The construction finds the derivation below,
read from the top.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\Gamma,P(cc)\vdash P(cd),P(cc)\) is an axiom, with common formula
      \(P(cc)\).
\item Rule \((\forall\vdash)\) with \(\varphi(x)=P(xc)\) and \(t=c\), so
      \(\varphi(t)=P(cc)\), gives \(\Gamma\vdash P(cd),P(cc)\); the
      principal formula \(\forall x\,P(xc)\) is already in \(\Gamma\).
\item The equality rule with \(E=(c=d)\), \(t_1=c\), \(t_2=d\),
      \(\varphi(z)=(c=d)\) (no \(z\)) and \(\psi(z)=P(cz)\) passes from
      \(\Gamma,(c=d)\vdash P(cd),P(cc)\), which is line~2, to
      \(\Gamma,(c=d),(c=d)\vdash P(cd)\), which is \(\Gamma\vdash P(cd)\).
\item Rule \((\vdash\exists)\) with \(\varphi(y)=P(yd)\) and \(t=c\) gives
      \(\Gamma\vdash\exists y\,P(yd)\).
\end{enumerate}
No cut is used.  In line~3 the formula \(P(cc)\) on the right is the
backward image of \(P(cd)\) under the equation; this is the right-side use
of the equality rule on which the proof of
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness} relies.  The eigenvariable
condition excludes the following step: \(R(u)\vdash R(u)\) is an axiom, but
\((\vdash\forall)\) may not pass to \(R(u)\vdash\forall y\,R(y)\), since
\(u\) is free on the left, and that sequent is not valid.
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-limit}{Sequents that do not separate their variables}
Two results here assume that the sequent separates its variables, namely
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness} and part~1 of
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-cut-elimination}; the proof uses the
hypothesis at the steps for
\((\vdash\forall)\) and \((\exists\vdash)\).  Under the reading of
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-reading} the backward step can be unavailable
without it.  Take \(\Gamma=\{\forall z\,R(z),\,S(y)\}\) and
\(\chi=\forall y\,(R(y)\land\forall y\,R(y))\).  The sequent
\(\Gamma\vdash\{\chi\}\) is valid, and it violates both (a) and (b) of
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-valid}.  No instance of \((\vdash\forall)\) has
conclusion \(\Gamma\vdash\{\chi\}\) with principal formula \(\chi\): such
an instance needs \(\varphi(v)\) with
\(\varphi(y/v)=(R(y)\land\forall y\,R(y))\); replacing free occurrences of
\(v\) leaves a subformula \(\forall y\,R(y)\) unchanged, so \(\varphi(v)\)
contains \(y\); the reading then requires \(v=y\), and \(y\) is free in
\(S(y)\in\Gamma\).  Barwise notes that clashing bound variables can always
be renamed \cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, p.~22]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, but
\(\mathbf G\) has no rule that renames bound variables inside a sequent.
\Cref{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variants} settles the questions this raises.
Under the reading of \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-reading}, some valid
sequents that violate (a) have no derivation in \(\mathbf G\) although they
have one in \(\mathbf G^{+}\), so 4.14 fails for them
(\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-clean-failure}).  Under the reading that lets
bound variables be renamed, as Barwise's remark permits, 4.14 holds for
every sequent (\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant-cut-elimination}).
\end{hbwarning}

\subsection{Sequents that do not separate their variables}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variants}

Barwise's convention for \(\varphi(t)\) assumes that no variable of \(t\) is
bound in \(\varphi\), and adds that the bound variables could be renamed if
one were \cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, p.~22]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  The cut-free
completeness proof above never needs that renaming, because a sequent that
separates its variables gives no occasion for it.  For other sequents the
renaming matters, and the two ways of reading the remark give opposite
answers to 4.14.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-clean-failure}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-clean-failure}{Cut cannot be eliminated under the reading without renaming}
Read the rules as in \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-reading}.  Let \(P\) be a
binary relation symbol of \(L\) and \(x,y,z\) distinct variables.  The
sequent
\[
\{\forall x\,\forall y\,P(xy)\}\vdash\{P(yy)\}
\]
is valid and has a derivation in \(\mathbf G^{+}\), but has none in
\(\mathbf G\).  It violates condition (a) of
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-valid} and satisfies (b).
\end{hbtheorem}

So under that reading \(\mathbf G\) is not complete for sequents that
violate (a), and condition (a) cannot be removed from part~1 of
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-cut-elimination}.  The failure comes from the
side condition on \(\varphi(t)\) alone: the only way to instantiate
\(\forall x\,\forall y\,P(xy)\) at \(y\) is to substitute \(y\) into
\(\forall y\,P(xy)\), where \(y\) is bound.  The derivation in
\(\mathbf G^{+}\) goes round this by a cut on the renamed formula
\(\forall x\,\forall z\,P(xz)\).

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant}{Renaming bound variables; variants}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{renaming step} replaces one occurrence of a subformula
      \((Qx\theta)\) of a formula, where \(Q\) is \(\forall\) or
      \(\exists\), by \((Qz\,\theta(z/x))\) for a variable \(z\) that does
      not occur in \((Qx\theta)\).  The substitution \(\theta(z/x)\) is
      clean, since \(z\) does not occur in \(\theta\).
\item \(\varphi'\) is a \emph{variant} of \(\varphi\) if a finite sequence
      of renaming steps, each taken forwards or backwards, leads from
      \(\varphi\) to \(\varphi'\).  Every formula is a variant of itself,
      by the empty sequence, and the relation is symmetric and transitive.
\item A sequent \(\Gamma'\vdash\Delta'\) is a \emph{variant} of
      \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) if every member of \(\Gamma\) is a variant of a
      member of \(\Gamma'\), every member of \(\Gamma'\) is a variant of a
      member of \(\Gamma\), and the same holds for \(\Delta\) and
      \(\Delta'\).
\end{enumerate}
For example \(\forall x\,\forall z\,P(xz)\) is a variant of
\(\forall x\,\forall y\,P(xy)\), by one renaming step on the subformula
\(\forall y\,P(xy)\), and \(\forall y\,(R(y)\land\forall z\,R(z))\) is a
variant of \(\forall y\,(R(y)\land\forall y\,R(y))\).
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbconvention}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant-reading}{Reading the rules with renaming}
Under the \emph{renaming reading}, a derivation in \(\mathbf G\) (or
\(\mathbf G^{+}\)) is a finite tree of sequents in which each node either
is a variant of an axiom, or there is an instance of a rule of \(\mathbf G\)
(or \(\mathbf G^{+}\)), read as in \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-reading},
whose conclusion is a variant of the node and whose premises are variants
of the sequents immediately above the node, one for each.  This makes
exact Barwise's remark that bound variables could always be renamed
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, p.~22]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}: renaming is allowed at
every step, and in nothing else does the reading differ from
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-reading}.  Every derivation in the earlier
sense is one under the renaming reading, each sequent being a variant of
itself.
\end{hbconvention}

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variants}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variants}{Variants}
\begin{enumerate}
\item If \(\varphi'\) is a variant of \(\varphi\), then for every structure
      \(\mathfrak M\) and assignment \(s\),
      \(\mathfrak M\models\varphi[s]\) iff \(\mathfrak M\models\varphi'[s]\).
      Hence a sequent is valid iff any variant of it is valid.
\item For every formula \(\varphi\) and every finite set \(W\) of variables
      there is a variant \(\varphi^{*}\) of \(\varphi\) with
      \(\FV(\varphi^{*})\subseteq\FV(\varphi)\), whose quantifier
      occurrences are on pairwise distinct variables, none of them in
      \(W\).
\item Every sequent has a variant that separates its variables.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hblemma}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant-cut-elimination}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant-cutfree}{Cut-Elimination under the renaming reading (Barwise, 4.14)}
Under the renaming reading of \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-variant-reading},
for every sequent \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) of \(L\) the following are
equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) has a derivation in \(\mathbf G^{+}\);
\item[(ii)] \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) has a derivation in \(\mathbf G\);
\item[(iii)] \(\Gamma\vdash\Delta\) is valid.
\end{enumerate}
In particular \(\mathbf G^{+}\) and \(\mathbf G\) have the same derivable
sequents, and for every set \(T\) of sentences and every sentence
\(\varphi\), \(T\models\varphi\) iff \(\Gamma\vdash\{\varphi\}\) has a
derivation in \(\mathbf G\) for some finite \(\Gamma\subseteq T\).
\end{hbtheorem}

This is Barwise's 4.14 for every sequent
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~40]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, and the Completeness
Theorem 4.13 for \(\mathbf G\) with no condition on \(T\) or \(\varphi\).
The proof renames the given sequent into one that separates its variables,
applies \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness} to it, and undoes the
renaming at the last node.  For the sequent of
\cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-clean-failure} the renaming reading gives a
cut-free derivation directly: \((\forall\vdash)\) applied to the variant
\(\forall x\,\forall z\,P(xz)\) of the left member with \(t=y\), and then to
\(\forall z\,P(yz)\) with \(t=y\), reaches the axiom
\(\forall x\,\forall z\,P(xz),\forall z\,P(yz),P(yy)\vdash P(yy)\); the
lowest node \(\{\forall x\,\forall y\,P(xy)\}\vdash\{P(yy)\}\) is a variant
of the conclusion \(\{\forall x\,\forall z\,P(xz)\}\vdash\{P(yy)\}\) of the
last rule instance.  For the sequent of
\cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-limit}, two renaming steps turn the right
member \(\forall y\,(R(y)\land\forall y\,R(y))\) into
\(\forall x\,(R(x)\land\forall z\,R(z))\); the sequent
\(\{\forall z\,R(z),S(y)\}\vdash\{\forall x\,(R(x)\land\forall z\,R(z))\}\)
separates its variables and is valid, so it has a derivation in
\(\mathbf G\) by \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness}, and the
renaming reading lets that derivation end at the original sequent.

\emph{Relationships.}  \Cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-g-completeness} extends
Barwise's propositional result
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-g-prop-completeness} to the full system and
gives, with the soundness half of
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-completeness-g}, the semantic proof of
Cut-Elimination along the direct route Barwise indicates.  Gentzen's own
proof transforms derivations by a double induction; Barwise refers it to
the chapter on cut-elimination of the same handbook
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S4, p.~40]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}, and the handbook's
syntactic cut-elimination theorem \cref{thm:p07-cut-elimination} concerns
the calculus LK of Part~7, not \(\mathbf G\).  The semantic proof gives no
bound on the size of the cut-free derivation, since it obtains the
derivation only from the impossibility of a countermodel.

\section{Lindstr\"om's theorem in Barwise's formulation}
\label{sec:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom}

Barwise closes his chapter with Lindstr\"om's theorem, 5.7: first-order
logic is the only logic closed under \(\land\), \(\neg\), \(\exists\) that
satisfies the Compactness Theorem and the L\"owenheim--Skolem theorem
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S5, 5.7, p.~45]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  The chapter gives
no proof.  Its notion of a logic is described only informally, as a syntax
and a semantics that fit together so that renaming symbols can be carried
out and keeps its intended meaning, and both the precise definition and the
proof are referred to Barwise's paper \cite{Barwise1974Axioms}
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S5, 5.6, p.~45]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  The chapter's
formulation is hosted in \cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-abstract-logic}.

\emph{Purpose.}  This section fixes a precise reading of 5.7 under which
it follows from the handbook's proved form of Lindstr\"om's theorem,
\cref{thm:p03-lindstrom}, and records what the reading adds to Barwise's
words.  \emph{Inputs.}  A regular abstract logic in the relational sense
of \cref{sec:p02-fol-metatheorem-summary} and \cref{sec:p03-lindstrom};
Barwise's Compactness Theorem 2.4 and L\"owenheim--Skolem theorem 2.5,
hosted as \cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-compactness} and
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-ls}.  \emph{Conclusion.}  Under the reading,
every sentence of such a logic satisfying both theorems is equivalent to a
first-order sentence.  The section also shows that each hypothesis is
needed, and that Barwise's remark that \(L(Q)\) satisfies the Compactness
Theorem holds for countable languages but not for 2.4 over an uncountable
language.

\begin{hbconvention}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-reading}{A precise reading of Barwise's 5.7}
Let \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\) be a regular abstract logic in the relational
sense of \cref{sec:p02-fol-metatheorem-summary}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\) \emph{satisfies the Compactness Theorem} if
      2.4 holds with \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\)-sentences in place of
      first-order sentences: for every relational signature \(\tau\) and
      every set \(T\) of \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\)-sentences of \(\tau\), if
      every finite \(T_0\subseteq T\) has a model, then \(T\) has a model.
\item \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\) \emph{satisfies the L\"owenheim--Skolem
      theorem} if 2.5 holds with \(\kappa=\aleph_0\) and
      \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\)-sentences in place of first-order sentences:
      for every \(\tau\) and every set \(T\) of at most \(\aleph_0\)
      \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\)-sentences of \(\tau\), if \(T\) has a model,
      then \(T\) has a model whose domain has cardinality at most
      \(\aleph_0\).  This is the value of \(\kappa\) that Barwise fixes when
      he applies 2.5 to other logics
      \cite[ch.~A.1, \S5, 5.6, p.~45]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
\item \emph{First-order logic is the only such logic} means: every
      \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\)-sentence is equivalent, over its finite
      occurrence signature, to a first-order sentence.  Since
      \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\) contains relational first-order logic, the two
      logics then have the same sentences up to equivalence.
\end{enumerate}
A regular abstract logic is closed under \(\neg\) and \(\land\), and
abstraction of a fresh constant gives \(\exists\), so it meets Barwise's
closure condition.  Regularity also requires isomorphism invariance, finite
occurrence signatures, transport under renaming, containment of
first-order logic and closure under relativization; the chapter leaves the
precise conditions to \cite{Barwise1974Axioms}.
\end{hbconvention}

\begin{hbcorollary}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-barwise-lindstrom}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-barwise-lindstrom}{Barwise's 5.7 under the precise reading}
Let \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\) be a regular abstract logic in the relational
sense that satisfies the Compactness Theorem and the L\"owenheim--Skolem
theorem in the sense of \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-reading}.
Then every \(\mathcal L^{\ast}\)-sentence is equivalent, over its finite
occurrence signature, to a relational first-order sentence.
\end{hbcorollary}

The proof checks that the two hypotheses of \cref{thm:p03-lindstrom},
countable compactness and a countable model for each satisfiable sentence,
are special cases of the two theorems in Barwise's form, and then applies
that theorem.  The proof of \cref{thm:p03-lindstrom} codes a
back-and-forth argument into a single sentence of the logic; it is not
Barwise's route, which the chapter does not give.

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-sharp}{proved=annex:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-sharp}{The two hypotheses in the case of \(L(Q)\)}
Let \(L(Q)\) be first-order logic with the quantifier \(Q\) meaning
``there exist uncountably many'', as in
\cref{def:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-infinitary}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The sentence \(Qx\,(x=x)\) of the empty signature is not equivalent
      to any first-order sentence of the empty signature.
\item Let \(L\) consist of a unary relation symbol \(P\) and constant
      symbols \(c_\alpha\) for \(\alpha<\omega_1\).  The set
      \[
       T=\{\neg Qx\,P(x)\}\cup\{P(c_\alpha):\alpha<\omega_1\}
         \cup\{\neg\, c_\alpha=c_\beta:\alpha<\beta<\omega_1\}
      \]
      of sentences of \(L(Q)\) has no model, although every finite subset
      of \(T\) has one.  Thus 2.4 fails for \(L(Q)\) over this language.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hblemma}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-example}{Each hypothesis of 5.7 is needed}
Barwise lists three logics that satisfy the L\"owenheim--Skolem theorem
with \(\kappa=\aleph_0\) but not the Compactness Theorem, namely weak
second-order logic, \(L_{\omega_1\omega}\) and \(L(Q_0)\), and one, \(L(Q)\),
that he describes as satisfying the Compactness Theorem but not the
L\"owenheim--Skolem theorem
\cite[ch.~A.1, \S5, 5.6, p.~45]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.  The failures for
\(L_{\omega_1\omega}\), \(L(Q_0)\) and \(L(Q)\) are parts~3 and~4 of
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-extended-ls}; weak second-order logic is
treated in \cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-weak-so-ls}.

For \(L(Q)\), part~1 of \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-sharp} shows
that the logic is strictly stronger than first-order logic, while Keisler's
theorem \cref{thm:p03-keisler-q-one} gives it the countable form of
compactness, which is hypothesis~1 of \cref{thm:p03-lindstrom}.  So the
L\"owenheim--Skolem hypothesis cannot be removed from
\cref{thm:p03-lindstrom}.  In the other direction, \(L_{\omega_1\omega}\)
over a language of any cardinality satisfies the L\"owenheim--Skolem
theorem for sets of at most \(\aleph_0\) sentences (part~1 of
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-extended-ls}) and is strictly stronger than
first-order logic: by part~3 of that theorem it has a countable set of
sentences whose finite subsets all have models while the set has none, and
a set of first-order sentences with that property does not exist by
\cref{thm:p02-w02-p02-005-bw-compactness}.  So the compactness hypothesis
cannot be removed either.
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-limit}{What the precise reading adds to Barwise's statement}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The regularity conditions of
      \cref{def:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-reading} go beyond closure under
      \(\land\), \(\neg\), \(\exists\), which is all that 5.7 names; closure
      under relativization, for instance, is used in the proof of
      \cref{thm:p03-lindstrom}.  \Cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-barwise-lindstrom}
      is therefore not a proof of 5.7 for a logic that has only the closure
      properties Barwise names.  The conditions of
      \cite{Barwise1974Axioms}, to which the chapter refers, are stated in
      \cref{def:p02-w02c-p02-003-b74-logic} and compared with these in
      \cref{def:p02-w02c-p02-003-b74-comparison}; 5.7 under them is
      \cref{thm:p02-w02c-p02-003-b74-five-seven}.
\item The handbook's notion of a regular abstract logic is relational.
      Barwise's languages also contain function symbols, with no
      restriction on the size of the language
      \cite[ch.~A.1, \S3, p.~17]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}.
      \Cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-barwise-lindstrom} covers his formulation
      only for relational signatures.  The passage to languages with
      function and constant symbols, of any size, is
      \cref{thm:p02-w02c-p02-003-general-lindstrom}, under the occurrence
      and replacement conditions of
      \cref{def:p02-w02c-p02-003-general-reading}; for logics in
      Barwise's own sense it is \cref{thm:p02-w02c-p02-003-b74-lindstrom},
      without the replacement condition.
\item Barwise's remark that \(L(Q)\) satisfies the Compactness Theorem is
      exact when 2.4 is read for finite or countable languages, the only
      ones the chapter has used before 2.5
      \cite[ch.~A.1, \S2, p.~10]{Barwise1977FirstOrder}; in that form it is
      Keisler's theorem \cref{thm:p03-keisler-q-one}, since such a language
      has only countably many sentences.  Read for arbitrary languages, 2.4
      fails for \(L(Q)\) by part~2 of
      \cref{thm:p02-w02b-p02-001-lindstrom-sharp}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbwarning}
