\chapter{Probability Logic and Conditional-Event Coherence}
\label{sec:p15-rationality-4-4}

Pfeifer's 2021 chapter presents probability logic as a rationality
framework for reasoning under uncertainty, and compares its formal
standards with experimental response patterns. Its subject is uncertain
arguments and conditional events, not probabilities of temporal paths
in a transition system \cite[pp.~277--284]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
The treatment below preserves the chapter's three claims: the framework
generalizes bivalent propositional reasoning, admits a specified connexive
interpretation, and blocks unrestricted strengthening of a default's
conditioning context. Mathematical validity and empirical support for
using this framework are distinct claims.

\section{The objects and the consequence question}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-rat44-events}{Propositions, conditional events, and their possible cases}
Ordinary propositions have classical truth conditions. Following the
chapter, write \(A\mathbin{\&}B\) for conjunction, \(A\lor B\) for
disjunction, \(\sim A\) for negation, and \(\top,\bot\) for a
tautology and contradiction. A finite nonempty constituent space \(\Omega\)
contains the logically possible truth assignments to the propositions
under consideration; an event is a subset of this space.

For \(A\ne\varnothing\), the conditional event \(B\mid A\) is
true on \(A\mathbin{\&}B\), false on
\(A\mathbin{\&}\sim B\), and void on \(\sim A\). A bet on it is
won, lost, or called off, respectively. Its probability is a primitive
assessment, not a third truth value or the probability of the material
conditional \(\sim A\lor B\). The chapter's simple conditional events
have ordinary, nonconditional operands: neither operand contains
another occurrence of \(\mid\).

The event \(A\) may be possible while having probability zero.
This differs from \(A=\bot\), which is excluded as a conditioning
event in this coherence framework. The chapter notes that Popper
functions use a different convention allowing contradictory conditions;
that convention is not inserted here.
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-rat44-coherence}{Two-sided conditional betting coherence}
For a finite family \(E_i\mid H_i\), with every \(H_i\ne\varnothing\),
and prices \(p_i\in[0,1]\), define the gain from stakes \(s_i\in\mathbb R\)
by
\[
 G_s(\omega)=\sum_i s_i\mathbf1_{H_i}(\omega)
                            (\mathbf1_{E_i}(\omega)-p_i).
\]
It is zero for an individual called-off bet. On the active event
\(H_s=\bigcup_{i:s_i\ne0}H_i\), coherence requires
\[
             \min_{H_s}G_s\leq0\leq\max_{H_s}G_s
\]
for every nonzero portfolio. An arbitrary assessment family is coherent
when every finite subfamily has this property. Positive and negative
stakes represent buying and selling at the same precise price. This is
a formal no-sure-gain/no-sure-loss condition, not a claim that every
person actually accepts every monetary gamble. Its relation to the
Dutch-book criterion, and the objections to identifying such prices
with credences, are in \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book}.

Unconditional events are \(E\mid\top\), so
\(p(E)\defeq p(E\mid\top)\). For specified premise values or
interval constraints, the probability-logical conclusion is the set of
values extendible to a coherent assessment satisfying those constraints.
Its tight lower and upper bounds are the infimum and supremum of this
set, when it is nonempty. An inconsistent premise assessment has no
coherent extension and is reported as such, not as a vacuous interval.
``Noninformative'' means this set is the entire \([0,1]\), not
that the conclusion is false or that the premises lack practical relevance.
\end{hbdefinition}

The constituent constructions and proofs below are finite semantic
reconstructions of the chapter's stated coherence conditions. They do
not claim to transcribe the full external algorithms or books cited
there. Unless a different logical relation is specified, the named
atomic propositions are logically independent: all their truth patterns
are possible. Known logical dependencies can narrow the quoted bounds.
No probabilistic independence assumption is made.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-coherence-method}{proved=annex:p15.rat44.coherence}{Convex geometry and the finite conditional method}
For unconditional events \(E_1,\ldots,E_n\), let
\(Q=\{(\mathbf1_{E_i}(\omega))_{i=1}^n:\omega\in\Omega\}\).
The assessment vector \(p\) is coherent iff \(p\in\operatorname{conv}Q\),
equivalently iff nonnegative constituent weights sum to one and give
the specified event probabilities.

An empty assessment is accepted. For a nonempty finite conditional
assessment family, the procedure is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item On the union of the remaining conditioning events, solve
      \(\lambda_\omega\geq0\), \(\sum\lambda_\omega=1\), and
      \(\sum_\omega\lambda_\omega\mathbf1_{H_i}
                  (\mathbf1_{E_i}-p_i)=0\) for all remaining \(i\).
\item If infeasible, reject the assessment. Otherwise identify the
      conditioning events whose maximum attainable weight over this
      solution set is zero, and repeat on just those assessments.
\item Accept when none remain.
\end{enumerate}
This terminates in at most the number of assessed conditionals and is
equivalent to finite coherence. The repeated zero-weight stages are
essential; one unconditional distribution need not decide a primitive
conditional on a null but possible event.
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-basic-laws}{proved=annex:p15.rat44.laws}{The basic identities and the positive-probability bridge}
For every admissible \(A\), coherence implies
\[
 p(A\mid A)=1,\quad p(\sim A\mid A)=0,\quad
 p(B\mid A)+p(\sim B\mid A)=1.
\]
On unconditional events it implies the ordinary finite probability
identities. When the joint assessment includes \(p(A)>0\), it implies
\[
              p(B\mid A)=\frac{p(A\mathbin{\&}B)}{p(A)}.
\]
At \(p(A)=0\), the equation
\(p(A\mathbin{\&}B)=p(A)p(B\mid A)\) does not determine the
conditional price. Its admissible values still depend on coherence and
logical relationships on \(A\), rather than on an assigned value for \(0/0\).
\end{hbtheorem}

An unconditional probability function takes values in \([0,1]\), but
is not an arbitrary assignment of graded truth values. The constituent
constraints preserve the underlying Boolean events. For example knowing
\(p(B),p(C)\) generally leaves an interval for
\(p(B\mathbin{\&}C)\). This is the chapter's contrast with
truth-functional connectives: exact marginal beliefs do not determine
the joint belief. Zero-one ordinary assessments recover classical
valuations; void conditional events remain a further, distinct extension.

\section{Coherence and the Dutch-book argument}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book}

The chapter defines coherence by betting. An assessment on a family of
conditional events is coherent when no finite combination of bets on
its members has a random gain that, over the cases in which at least
one bet is not called off, is everywhere positive or everywhere
negative. It labels this the absence of a Dutch book and refers the
reader to H\'ajek and Staffel's chapter for that notion
\cite[p.~278]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}. Its note on betting terms
fixes the stakes: for each real \(s\), the bettor pays \(ps\) and
receives \(s\) or \(0\) according to whether the event occurs, and the
gain of an assessment is the sum of the gains of its bets
\cite[p.~282, note~3]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}. The handbook's
account of H\'ajek and Staffel's chapter is
\cref{sec:p15-rationality-4-1}; its Dutch-book discussion relies on the
finite equivalence of \cref{thm:p17-finite-price-coherence} for event
contracts quoted on a whole finite algebra
(\cref{def:p17-finite-price-coherence}). The result below states exactly
how the called-off condition of \cref{def:p15-rat44-coherence} relates
to that criterion.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book-bridge}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book-bridge}{Called-off coherence and the finite sure-loss criterion}
Let \(E_i\mid H_i\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\), be a finite family on the finite
constituent space \(\Omega\) with prices \(p_i\in[0,1]\), and let
\(G_s\) and \(H_s\) be as in \cref{def:p15-rat44-coherence}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The family is coherent if and only if no nonzero stake vector
      \(s\) has \(G_s(\omega)<0\) for every \(\omega\in H_s\).
      Excluding a uniform gain adds nothing once every stake may be
      reversed.
\item If every \(H_i=\Omega\), then \(H_s=\Omega\) for every nonzero
      \(s\), and \(G_s=\sum_is_ib_{E_i}\) with
      \(b_{E_i}=\mathbf1_{E_i}-p_i\mathbf1\) the contracts of
      \cref{def:p17-finite-price-coherence} on the state set
      \(\Omega\). The family is coherent if and only if no portfolio
      of these contracts is negative in every state. When the family
      consists of one assessment \(E\mid\Omega\) for every
      \(E\subseteq\Omega\), it is coherent if and only if \(E\mapsto
      p(E\mid\Omega)\) is a probability measure on \(2^\Omega\).
\item If \(U=\bigcup_iH_i\ne\Omega\), every \(G_s\) vanishes on
      \(\Omega\setminus U\), so no portfolio is negative in every
      state of \(\Omega\), whatever the prices. In particular, for
      \(\varnothing\ne A\ne\Omega\) and any \(B\), the assessment
      \(p(B\mid A)=p(\sim B\mid A)=0.6\) has no portfolio negative on
      all of \(\Omega\), yet it is not coherent: the stakes \((1,1)\)
      give \(G_s=-0.2\) throughout \(H_s=A\).
\item If \(\varnothing\ne E\ne\Omega\), the single assessment
      \(p(E\mid\Omega)=0\) is coherent, although the stake \(-1\)
      gives a gain that is nonpositive everywhere and negative on
      \(E\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Item~2 places the unconditional part of the present framework inside
the handbook's finite Dutch-book theorem; item~1 shows that the
chapter's two-sided wording (``all positive or all negative'') is the
one-sided sure-loss exclusion of that theorem, given reversible stakes.
The remaining differences are material.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Which outcomes count.} H\'ajek and Staffel describe a Dutch
      book as bets guaranteeing a net loss
      \cite[p.~240]{HajekStaffel2021Dynamics}; for event contracts the
      loss is assessed in every possible case. The present test looks
      only at the cases in which some bet is live. Item~3 shows that
      the restriction carries weight: tested on all of \(\Omega\), any
      assessment whose conditioning events miss some case would pass,
      including one that violates the complement identity of
      \cref{thm:p15-rat44-basic-laws}.
\item \emph{Conditional probability.} The calculus of
      \cref{def:p15-rat41-probabilism} defines \(P(X\mid Y)\) as a
      ratio only when \(P(Y)>0\), and mentions approaches with primitive
      conditional probability only in notes. Here \(p(B\mid A)\) is the
      price of a called-off bet and stays constrained when \(p(A)=0\)
      (\cref{thm:p15-rat44-paradox}). When the joint assessment
      contains \(p(A)>0\), the two agree by
      \cref{thm:p15-rat44-basic-laws}.
\item \emph{Domain.} \Cref{thm:p17-finite-price-coherence} assumes a
      quote for every event of the algebra. The present definition
      applies to an arbitrary family, and coherence then amounts to
      extendibility (\cref{thm:p15-rat44-coherence-method}).
\item \emph{Weak loss.} Item~4 is the conditional-event counterpart of
      the last clause of \cref{thm:p17-finite-price-coherence}: a zero
      price on a possible event permits a portfolio that never gains
      and sometimes loses. A criterion excluding such portfolios would
      make every assessment with \(p(A)=0\) for a possible
      \(A\ne\Omega\) incoherent, and with it the null antecedents on
      which the chapter's endpoint argument depends
      (\cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-endpoint-argument}).
\item \emph{Synchronic and diachronic.} Called-off bets are priced at
      one time. A coherent \(p(B\mid A)\) does not by itself commit
      anyone to adopt it as the new value of \(p(B)\) after learning
      \(A\); that is the separate updating norm, whose diachronic
      Dutch-book defences and objections are surveyed in
      \cref{sec:p15-rat41-dynamics}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-038-two-books}{The election book and a called-off book}
Write \(D\) and \(R\) for incompatible victories of two parties and
price \(D\mid\Omega\), \(R\mid\Omega\), \((D\lor R)\mid\Omega\) at
\(1/2\), \(1/2\), \(9/10\), the schedule of
\cref{def:p15-rat41-election-book}. The stakes \((1,1,-1)\) give
\(G_s=\mathbf1_D+\mathbf1_R-\mathbf1_{D\lor R}-0.1=-0.1\) at every
case, since \(D\) and \(R\) never hold together. The schedule is
incoherent under both definitions; this is the ten-cent book.

Now price \(B\mid A\) and \(\sim B\mid A\) at \(0.6\) each, with
\(A\) possible but not certain. Buying both bets costs \(1.2\) units
and returns \(1\) when \(A\) holds, so the loss is \(0.2\) in every
case where the bets are live, and the money is returned when \(A\)
fails. Tested on all cases, this is no Dutch book; tested on the live
cases, it is one. Raising the two prices to sum to one removes this
loss.
\end{hbexample}

\paragraph{Objections to the betting identification.}
H\'ajek and Staffel list objections to identifying a credence with a
two-sided betting price \cite[p.~240]{HajekStaffel2021Dynamics}. Each
bears on the assumption in \cref{def:p15-rat44-coherence} that positive
and negative stakes are taken at one precise price.
\begin{itemize}
\item There may be no such price, or more than one. If an agent's
      buying and selling prices differ, the two-sided assumption fails
      at the start; the formal model for one-sided acceptable prices
      is the lower-prevision coherence of \cref{def:p15-ip-coherence},
      not an interval constraint of this chapter.
\item Betting behaviour is sensitive to the stakes, while credences
      are not, and the account is implausible for events under the
      agent's control. The chapter's betting terms require one price
      \(p\) for every real stake \(s\)
      \cite[p.~282, note~3]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}; stake
      sensitivity is a failure of exactly this linearity. Measuring
      credences by bets may also change them, and a high-stakes offer
      can suggest that the other party has inside information.
\item A betting price can be jointly determined by the value of money,
      the thrill of gambling and public bravado, of which credence is
      only one component. The gain \(G_s\) adds monetary amounts
      linearly; \cref{thm:p17-nonlinear-bet-example} shows that
      beliefs alone do not yield such additive monetary evaluation.
\item The Dutch-book argument is criticized for exhibiting a pragmatic
      rather than an epistemic defect, and an agent can decline the
      sure-loss package. Skyrms, following Ramsey, reads the argument
      as dramatizing an inconsistency in the agent's evaluations
      \cite[p.~241]{HajekStaffel2021Dynamics}.
\item If credences come apart from betting prices, a defect in the
      prices does not entail one in the credences
      \cite[p.~241]{HajekStaffel2021Dynamics}.
\end{itemize}
These objections leave the mathematics of this chapter intact:
\cref{thm:p15-rat44-coherence-method}, the bounds, and
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book-bridge} are statements about price
schedules. They bear on a different step, the one that reads a
participant's probability judgment as a degree of belief whose
incoherence or noninformative bounds settle a rationality verdict. That
step is used in the endpoint argument and in the comparisons with
experimental data below. Pfeifer's chapter does not discuss these
objections; it takes the betting interpretation as given and refers to
H\'ajek and Staffel's chapter for Dutch books.

\section{Zero-probability antecedents and the conditional paradox}

The chapter contrasts ``\(B\); therefore, if \(A\), then \(B\)''
under two representations. As a material conditional the conclusion is
classically entailed. Substituting a green-cheese claim about the moon
for \(A\) and a weather claim about Vienna for \(B\) makes the
pragmatic oddity evident without invalidating the classical argument.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-paradox}{proved=annex:p15.rat44.paradox}{The two tight bounds, including both endpoints}
For logically independent \(A,B\) and every \(x\in[0,1]\),
\[
 \begin{array}{ll}
 p(B)=x:& p(B\mid A)\text{ can be any value in }[0,1];\\
 p(B)=x:& p(\sim A\lor B)\text{ can be any value in }[x,1].
 \end{array}
\]
The first claim includes \(x=0\) and \(x=1\), using primitive
conditional assessments on a possible null antecedent. If instead one
requires \(p(A)>0\) and uses only the quotient definition, then
\(x=0\) forces conditional value zero and \(x=1\) forces value one.
For \(0<x<1\), with no further restriction on positive \(p(A)\),
every conditional value remains attainable.
\end{hbtheorem}

Assigning value one to every conditional on a null antecedent would
assign one to both \(B\mid A\) and \(\sim B\mid A\), violating
the complement identity. The chapter therefore rejects that default
within its coherent-price semantics. This is not an assertion that
every alternative formalism uses its same domain and complement laws.
Nor does \(p(B)=1\) make \(B\) a tautology: if \(B\) is logically
true, coherence does force \(p(B\mid A)=1\) on every admissible \(A\).

\subsection{The chapter's argument from the endpoint \(p(B)=1\)}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-endpoint-argument}

The second section of the chapter argues from this endpoint to a claim
about how rationality verdicts are reached. Its steps are of different
kinds, and they are kept apart here
\cite[pp.~278--279]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Formal step, quotient conditioning.} With the conditional
      defined as the quotient \(p(A\mathbin{\&}B)/p(A)\) for
      \(p(A)>0\), the premise \(p(B)=1\) gives
      \(p(A\mathbin{\&}B)=p(A)\), so \(p(B\mid A)=1\) whenever
      \(p(A)>0\), and \(p(B\mid A)\) is undefined when \(p(A)=0\).
      This is the endpoint clause of
      \cref{thm:p15-rat44-paradox}.
\item \emph{Empirical step, a reported finding.} The chapter states
      that this verdict is counterintuitive and conflicts with data:
      people interpret argument~(3), ``\(B\); therefore, if \(A\), then
      \(B\)'' with the conclusion read as \(p(B\mid A)\), as
      probabilistically noninformative even when \(p(B)=1\), citing
      Pfeifer and Kleiter (2011). The boundaries of this report are
      stated in \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-evidence-boundaries}.
\item \emph{Formal step, coherence.} With \(p(B\mid A)\) primitive and
      zero-probability antecedents allowed, every value
      \(0\leq p(B\mid A)\leq1\) is coherent even when \(p(B)=1\).
      The chapter refers to Pfeifer (2014) for a detailed proof; the
      proof used here is the two-stratum witness in the annex to
      \cref{thm:p15-rat44-paradox}.
\item \emph{Conclusion.} Whether a response to argument~(3) at
      \(p(B)=1\) counts as rational depends on whether the probability
      concept used as the standard can handle zero-probability
      antecedents. Under coherence, the noninformativeness of (3) holds
      for every premise value; under the quotient definition it fails at
      the endpoints.
\end{enumerate}

The conclusion in step~4 is a conditional claim about two formal
standards, and steps~1 and~3 establish it without any data: the same
response, ``any value from \(0\) to \(1\)'' for \(p(B\mid A)\) given
\(p(B)=1\), is an error against the quotient standard and a correct
tight answer against the coherence standard. Step~2 does not prove that
coherence is the right standard. It shows that the reported responses
agree with the coherence verdict and not with the quotient verdict, and
the chapter uses that agreement as a reason to prefer a framework whose
verdicts on such cases do not depend on an undefined ratio.

For a numerical contrast, take \(p(B)=1\) and \(p(A)=0.3\). The
quotient standard gives \(p(A\mathbin{\&}B)=0.3\) and
\(p(B\mid A)=1\), so an answer of \(0.4\) is incoherent there. Under
the coherence standard with \(p(A)=0\) instead, a first measure
supported on \(\sim A\mathbin{\&}B\) and a second measure on \(A\)
giving \(B\) weight \(0.4\) are a coherent witness for \(p(B)=1\) and
\(p(B\mid A)=0.4\). At \(p(B)=1\) the two standards disagree only on
assessments that give \(A\) probability zero: when \(p(A)>0\), coherence
also forces the value one (\cref{thm:p15-rat44-basic-laws}). Argument~(3)
fixes only \(p(B)\), so it leaves that zero-probability case open, and
the chapter's argument turns on it.

\section{Connexivity with a declared negation convention}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-rat44-connexive-reading}{Narrow-scope conditional negation and high acceptance}
For this part of the chapter, negating ``if \(A\), then \(B\)''
means replacing its consequent by \(\sim B\): the conditional event
is \((\sim B)\mid A\). A high-probability acceptance threshold is
\(h>1/2\), with acceptance requiring \(p(B\mid A)>h\).
The chapter also refers to wide-scope interpretations elsewhere; it
does not identify them with this narrow-scope convention.
The threshold implications below are assertions about assessments,
not nested conditional events of the prohibited form
\(C\mid(B\mid A)\).
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-connexive}{proved=annex:p15.rat44.connexive}{Aristotle, Boethius, and Abelard in the selected interpretation}
For a contingent \(A\), the Aristotle principles
\(\sim(\text{if }\sim A\text{ then }A)\) and
\(\sim(\text{if }A\text{ then }\sim A)\) are justified by
\[
 p(A\mid\sim A)=p(\sim A\mid A)=0,\qquad
 p(\sim A\mid\sim A)=p(A\mid A)=1.
\]
Under the selected narrow-scope convention, each Boethius principle
\[
 (A\to B)\to\sim(A\to\sim B),\qquad
 (A\to\sim B)\to\sim(A\to B)
\]
preserves its high-probability assertion because double negation gives
the same conditional assessment. Abelard's prohibition of accepting
both \(A\to B\) and \(A\to\sim B\) holds since their conditional
probabilities sum to one and cannot both exceed \(1/2\).

Replacing every arrow by material implication instead makes the two
Aristotle formulas equivalent to \(\sim A,A\), respectively, and
both Boethius formulas and the negation of their opposing conjunction
equivalent to \(A\). They are contingent, not classical tautologies.
The claimed validation is therefore interpretation-specific, not a
proof that these formulas are valid for every meaning of a conditional
or every scope of negation.
\end{hbtheorem}

The theorem is a formal result and does not depend on any experiment.
Separately, the chapter reports that participants judged that (AT1)
and (AT2) must be true, citing Pfeifer (2012), and that the Aristotle
theses have strong experimental support, citing Pfeifer (2012) and
Pfeifer and Tulkki (2017b); it marks the Boethius theses and Abelard's
first principle as needing further empirical research
\cite[p.~280]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}. The task, as the chapter
describes it, is a judgment of whether each thesis must be true; the
chapter does not give the materials, the response options, the
proportion of participants or responses classified as ``must be
true'', the sample, its recruitment, the design, or any interval of
uncertainty, and none is supplied here. The boundaries of this report
are set out with the other reports in
\cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-evidence-boundaries}. The chapter does not
transfer the Aristotle result to Boethius or Abelard merely because all
three have a formal justification.
Its comparison with connexive logics is not a proposal to add these
formulas, with every arrow still material, to classical logic. The
chapter describes connexive calculi as alternatives not simply contained
in, or proper extensions of, the classical calculus. The assessment
interpretation above is correspondingly not being advertised as a
complete axiomatization of a particular connexive calculus.

\subsection{Comparison with Abelard's self-negation constraint}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-abelard}

The historical treatment of Abelard in Part~XIII
(\cref{sec:p13-abelard-topics}) records his rejection of any
consequence of the form \(X\supset\sim X\), where \(\supset\) is
Abelard's conditional judgment and not material implication, and
reconstructs two chains, the opposite-topic chain and Alberic's chain,
that derive such a consequence from accepted judgments together with
projection, contraposition, combination and composition
(\cref{thm:p13-abelard-diagnostic}). That result shows only that those
commitments cannot all stand with the prohibition; which one to give up
is the point on which the rival schools differ.

The two treatments differ in kind. The Abelard prohibition belongs to a
historical criterion of strong entailment, in which the antecedent must
of itself require the consequent; it is reconstructed from Wilks's
account, not from probabilities. Pfeifer's Aristotle theses are claims
about coherent assessments of conditional events under the
narrow-scope, high-threshold reading of
\cref{def:p15-rat44-connexive-reading}, and Pfeifer's own label
``Abelard's first principle'' names the formula (AFP), which forbids
accepting both \(A\to B\) and \(A\to\sim B\), not the self-negation
constraint. The shared content is that the self-negating form
\(\sim(A\to\sim A)\) is an Aristotle thesis in Pfeifer's list. Reading
Abelard's \(\supset\) as a conditional event under that convention is an
interpretation adopted here to compare the two, not Abelard's semantics;
under it, the following holds.

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-alberic}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-038-alberic}{Alberic's chain read probabilistically}
Let \(p,q\) be logically independent, put
\(D=p\mathbin{\&}\sim q\), and read an accepted judgment
\(X\supset Y\) as \(p(Y\mid X)>h\) for a fixed \(h\in(1/2,1)\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every coherent assessment gives
      \(p(\sim q\mid D)=p(p\mid D)=1\),
      \(p(\sim p\mid D)=p(\sim D\mid D)=0\), and
      \(p(\sim D\mid\sim p)=1\).
\item For all \(x,z\in[0,1]\), the assessment
      \(p(q\mid p)=x\), \(p(\sim p\mid\sim q)=z\), together with the
      five values in item~1, is coherent.
\end{enumerate}
Hence the chain's conclusion \(D\supset\sim D\) is never accepted, and
both projections and the second contraposition are accepted. The first
contraposition, from \(p\supset q\) to \(\sim q\supset\sim p\), can
fail: take \(x>h\geq z\). When it does not fail (\(x,z>h\)), the
composition from \(D\supset\sim q\) and \(\sim q\supset\sim p\) to
\(D\supset\sim p\) has both premises accepted and a conclusion of
probability zero.
\end{hblemma}

Under this reading the chain is therefore blocked at two of its
operations, contraposition and composition, which are the probabilistic
counterparts of the noninformative contraposition and transitivity forms
of \cref{thm:p15-rat44-noninformation}; the self-negation prohibition
itself is the coherence identity \(p(\sim D\mid D)=0\). The
opposite-topic chain is not treated in this way here: its combination
step concerns conjoined antecedents and consequents, for which the
chapter gives no probabilistic counterpart. The lemma is a statement
about one formal reading; it is not evidence about what Abelard or his
contemporaries meant, and the P13 reconstruction makes no probabilistic
claim.

\section{Default contexts and tight propagation}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-noninformation}{proved=annex:p15.rat44.noninformation}{Strengthening, contraposition, and ordinary transitivity}
With the indicated propositions logically independent and all
conditioning events possible, the following conclusion values range
over all \([0,1]\), for every stated premise value:
\begin{enumerate}
\item From \(p(F\mid B)=x\): \(p(F\mid(B\mathbin{\&}P))\).
\item From \(p(B\mid A)=x\): \(p(\sim A\mid\sim B)\).
\item From \(p(B\mid A)=x\) and \(p(C\mid B)=y\): \(p(C\mid A)\).
\end{enumerate}
Here \(x,y\in[0,1]\); the endpoint cases can require null-event
conditioning strata. These are the chapter's probabilistically
noninformative counterparts of classically valid material-conditional
arguments, not proofs that every uncertain transitivity inference is
useless once additional dependence information is supplied.
\end{hbtheorem}

The bird/penguin example illustrates why ``normally a bird flies''
does not license the same assessment for a bird known to be a penguin.
``Normally'' scopes over the whole conditional, not only the consequent.
System P is the chapter's reference family for basic default-inference
constraints. It cites Gilio's coherent probability propagation for its
rules and notes that unrestricted strengthening, contraposition, or
transitivity would destroy the intended nonmonotonic discipline
\cite[pp.~280--281, note~5]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-rat44-monotonicity-distinction}{Changing a conditioning context is not adding a numerical constraint}
For a fixed target and fixed assessment language, adding numerical
premise constraints can only shrink the set of coherent extensions;
its lower bound cannot decrease and its upper bound cannot increase.
That constraint consequence is monotone. The chapter's nonmonotonicity
concerns revising default conclusions or changing \(B\) to
\(B\mathbin{\&}P\) as the conditioning context. Treating these
two operations as the same would contradict the definition of tight
bounds.
\end{hbwarning}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-propagation}{proved=annex:p15.rat44.propagation}{The selected AND, CUT, and MP bounds}
For \(0\leq x_L\leq x_U\leq1\) and
\(0\leq y_L\leq y_U\leq1\), the constraints
\(p(B\mid A)\in[x_L,x_U]\) and
\(p(C\mid A)\in[y_L,y_U]\) give exactly
\[
 p((B\mathbin{\&}C)\mid A)\in
 [\max\{0,x_L+y_L-1\},\min\{x_U,y_U\}].
\]
For cumulative transitivity, \(p(B\mid A)=x\) and
\(p(C\mid(A\mathbin{\&}B))=y\) give exactly
\[
                         p(C\mid A)\in[xy,xy+1-x].
\]
For probabilistic modus ponens, \(p(B)=x\) and \(p(C\mid B)=y\)
give exactly the same interval for \(p(C)\). All bounds are sharp,
including \(x=0,1\), and do not multiply marginals under an unstated
independence assumption. MP is CUT with \(A=\top\).
\end{hbtheorem}

For example \(p(B\mid A)=0.8,p(C\mid A)=0.7\) gives the AND
interval \([0.5,0.7]\), not the independence point \(0.56\).
MP with \(x=0.8,y=0.7\) instead gives \([0.56,0.76]\).
These calculations use stipulated assessments, not measured responses.
When both AND premise values are one, its conclusion is one; exact
premise values otherwise need not give an exact conclusion value.

\subsection{System P under coherence and under preferential models}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-system-p}

Part~VIII treats System P qualitatively
(\cref{sec:p08-paraconsistent-and-nonmonotonic}). There a defeasible
assertion \(\alpha\mathrel{|\!\sim}\beta\) holds in a smooth
preferential model when every most-preferred \(\alpha\)-state satisfies
\(\beta\), and a relation satisfies the System P rules exactly when some
smooth preferential model represents it
(\cref{thm:p08-klm-representation}; the construction is proved in
\cref{sec:p08-klm-preferential-representation},
\cref{thm:p08-klm-pref-representation}). The chapter instead reads a
default ``if \(A\), then normally \(B\)'' as a high, possibly
interval-valued, coherent conditional probability, and it attributes
to Gilio (2002) a probability propagation rule for each System P rule;
it displays the rules for AND and CUT, which are proved above in
\cref{thm:p15-rat44-propagation}, and does not display the others
\cite[pp.~280--281]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}. The propagation rules
for the other System P rules, including Or and Cautious Monotonicity,
are not reconstructed here. The chapter
also lists several probability-logical semantics for nonmonotonic
reasoning and points to experimental studies of a possibilistic semantics
of System P, without reporting their results; no comparison with those
semantics is drawn here.

The two semantics share a flat conditional: neither nests a conditional
inside another, and in both a conclusion about \(B\) given \(A\) is not a
conclusion about the material conditional. They differ materially in
four respects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Kind of output.} A preferential model decides whether an
      assertion holds. The coherence semantics returns the set of
      coherent values for the conclusion, which is an interval even from
      exact premise values.
\item \emph{Contradictory and null antecedents.} In a preferential
      model an antecedent with no states makes every consequent hold
      vacuously (\cref{def:p08-klm-model}). The coherence semantics
      excludes \(\bot\) as a conditioning event
      (\cref{def:p15-rat44-events}), while a possible antecedent of
      probability zero keeps a conditional value constrained by
      coherence and logic.
\item \emph{Acceptance by threshold.} The And rule is sound in every
      smooth preferential model (\cref{thm:p08-klm-soundness}). Reading
      acceptance as a probability above a fixed threshold below one does
      not preserve it (\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-threshold-and}): the
      coherence semantics validates And as a propagation of bounds,
      not as closure of the high-probability assertions under a fixed
      cutoff.
\item \emph{How the excluded rules fail.} Monotonicity, contraposition
      and transitivity fail in preferential semantics through a
      countermodel, and in the coherence semantics through
      noninformativeness: the conclusion may take every value in
      \([0,1]\) (\cref{thm:p15-rat44-noninformation}).
\end{enumerate}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-threshold-and}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-038-threshold-and}{A fixed threshold below one is not closed under AND}
Let \(B,C\) be logically independent given a possible \(A\), and let
\(0\leq h<1\). There are coherent assessments with
\(p(B\mid A)>h\), \(p(C\mid A)>h\) and
\(p((B\mathbin{\&}C)\mid A)\leq h\). If instead
\(p(B\mid A)=p(C\mid A)=1\), coherence forces
\(p((B\mathbin{\&}C)\mid A)=1\).
\end{hbtheorem}

The chapter's claim that adding monotonicity or contraposition to
System P, or adding transitivity, would make System P monotonic
\cite[p.~281]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic} can be checked in the
Part~VIII rule system, using the rules of
\cref{def:p08-klm-assertions}: Reflexivity, Left Logical Equivalence
and Right Weakening are all it needs.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-collapse}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-038-collapse}{Contraposition or transitivity added to System P yields monotonicity}
Write Monotonicity for the rule from \(\alpha\mathrel{|\!\sim}\gamma\)
to \(\alpha\wedge\beta\mathrel{|\!\sim}\gamma\), Contraposition for
the rule from \(\alpha\mathrel{|\!\sim}\beta\) to
\(\neg\beta\mathrel{|\!\sim}\neg\alpha\), and Transitivity for the rule
from \(\alpha\mathrel{|\!\sim}\beta\) and
\(\beta\mathrel{|\!\sim}\gamma\) to
\(\alpha\mathrel{|\!\sim}\gamma\). A relation closed under
Reflexivity, Left Logical Equivalence and Right Weakening that is also
closed under Contraposition, or under Transitivity, is closed under
Monotonicity. The two-state preferential model of
\cref{thm:p08-klm-penguins} is closed under none of Monotonicity,
Contraposition and Transitivity.
\end{hbtheorem}

Adding Monotonicity itself makes the relation monotonic by definition
(\cref{def:p08-nonmonotonic}). So in the qualitative system the
chapter's claim is a derivation from three System P rules, and the
penguin model shows that System P alone does not have these rules. In
the coherence semantics the same three forms are blocked by
noninformativeness, and the chapter reports that most participants in
the cited studies recognized strengthening and contraposition as
noninformative; that report, its sources and its boundaries are given
in \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-evidence-boundaries}.
Part~VIII also treats Rational Monotony and ranked models
(\cref{thm:p08-rational-representation}); the chapter says nothing
about Rational Monotony, and no probabilistic counterpart of it is
claimed here.

\section{What the experimental comparisons establish}

The chapter contrasts the former classical-logic benchmark with the
probabilistic new paradigm. Its examples are Braine and O'Brien's
mental logic and Rips's mental rule theory, using syntactic proof
standards, and Johnson-Laird's mental-model account, using a semantic
model standard. Changing the rationality framework changes what counts
as an error and motivates different tasks; it does not by itself show
that a participant used a particular formal procedure. The chapter's
empirical interest is general response patterns in uncertain reasoning,
not selecting a probability model merely for its fit to data
\cite[pp.~277,279]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.

The ordinary truth-table task asks whether the four cases confirm,
disconfirm, or are irrelevant to an indicative conditional. The chapter
reports that most people judge that \(A\mathbin{\&}B\) confirms the
conditional, that \(A\mathbin{\&}\sim B\) disconfirms it, and that
\(\sim A\) is irrelevant to it, giving Wason and Johnson-Laird (1972)
as an example of the supporting work (``see, e.g.''); its claim that
this pattern matches the conditional-event semantics cites Kleiter,
Fugard and Pfeifer (2018), Over and Baratgin (2017) and Pfeifer and
Tulkki (2017b) \cite[p.~278]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}. The chapter
gives no proportion for ``most'', no population and no design for
this pattern.
Calling this a defective truth table already presupposes the material
conditional as the benchmark; the conditional-event semantics assigns
the reported cases true, false, and void instead. That match is an
interpretation of a response pattern: a participant who answers
``irrelevant'' for \(\sim A\) may also be reporting that the case
does not bear on the conditional for some other reason, so the pattern
is consistent with the conditional-event semantics without showing that
participants use it.

In the probabilistic truth-table task (PTTT), the input is
\[
 p(A\mathbin{\&}B)=x_1,\quad
 p(A\mathbin{\&}\sim B)=x_2,\quad
 p(\sim A\mathbin{\&}B)=x_3,\quad
 p(\sim A\mathbin{\&}\sim B)=x_4,
\]
where the nonnegative values sum to one. With \(x_1+x_2>0\), the
competing predictions are \(x_1/(x_1+x_2)\) for a conditional event,
\(x_1\) for conjunction, and \(x_1+x_3+x_4\) for a material
conditional. For stipulated \((0.2,0.1,0.3,0.4)\), these give
\(2/3,0.2,0.9\), respectively. They are hypotheses about interpreting
the task, not three estimators of the same defined event. When
\(x_1+x_2=0\), the ratio supplies no answer; a primitive conditional
assessment then requires its own coherent specification rather than
evaluation of an undefined quotient.

The following are the chapter's reports of the cited studies, not fresh
population estimates \cite[pp.~278--281]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{PTTT, indicative conditionals} (p.~279). The task is
      attributed to Evans, Handley and Over (2003) and Oberauer and
      Wilhelm (2003). The reported results, that most participants gave
      responses consistent with \(x_1/(x_1+x_2)\), that a significant
      minority gave \(x_1\), that material-conditional responses were
      negligible, and that participants who did not begin with
      conditional-probability responses shifted toward them when the
      task was repeated, reaching about 80\% of responses in the last
      tasks, are cited to Fugard, Pfeifer, Mayerhofer and Kleiter (2011)
      and Pfeifer (2013). The materials were mostly abstract, such as
      ``If the figure shows a square, then the figure is red''. The
      80\% figure has task responses as its denominator, not people,
      and it concerns the final tasks of a repeated-task design, not a
      first exposure or everyday conditionals.
\item \emph{PTTT, other kinds of conditional} (p.~279). The chapter
      says the result was replicated for causal, counterfactual and
      abductive conditionals. Its citations are attached as follows:
      Over, Hadjichristidis, Evans, Handley and Sloman (2007) and
      Pfeifer and St\"ockle-Schobel (2015) to the counterfactual item,
      Pfeifer and Tulkki (2017a) to the abductive item, and no study
      to the causal item. These involve different formulations and
      materials; no common effect size or universal equivalence of
      those tasks is established here.
\item \emph{AND interval tasks} (p.~281), cited to Pfeifer and Kleiter
      (2005): most responses were coherent intervals, and most of the
      incoherent responses violated the lower bound rather than the
      upper bound. An upper-bound violation would be the formal
      conjunction fallacy; these errors therefore differ from that
      particular error pattern.
\item \emph{Other System P rules} (pp.~280--281), cited to Pfeifer and
      Kleiter (2005, 2006b, 2010) and Pfeifer and Tulkki (2017b): strong
      agreement between participants' interval responses and the
      coherent intervals. The chapter does not say which of these
      studies tested which rule.
\item \emph{Strengthening and contraposition} (p.~281): the majority of
      participants recognized both as noninformative. The chapter
      attaches no citation to this sentence of its own; it follows the
      System P sentence and its four citations above. Its boundaries
      are in \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-evidence-boundaries}.
\item \emph{Transitivity read as CUT} (p.~281), cited to Pfeifer and
      Kleiter (2006b, 2010): participants are said to interpret the
      material of a transitivity task, presumably for pragmatic reasons,
      as Cumulative Transitivity. This is an interpretation of the
      responses, not direct evidence that the ordinary transitivity
      bounds become informative.
\item \emph{Modus ponens} (p.~281): categorical endorsement cited to
      Evans, Newstead and Byrne (1993), and agreement with the
      probabilistic MP interval cited to Pfeifer and Kleiter (2009) and
      Pfeifer and Tulkki (2017b); treated in
      \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-evidence-boundaries}.
\end{itemize}

For none of these reports does the chapter specify sample recruitment,
sample sizes, response coding, confidence intervals, or task-by-task
effect sizes; the same holds for the truth-table report above and for
the Aristotle-thesis report after \cref{thm:p15-rat44-connexive}. Its
broad descriptive conclusion must be read with that reporting limit;
those details are not invented or pooled here. Because the cited
studies are not consulted directly here, a claim that a study supports
a report means only that the chapter cites that study for it.
The Linda-task comparison also raises an interpretation
objection: merely using the words ``and'' and ``probability'' does not
ensure that participants interpreted the ranking task as the formal
conjunction-probability problem. Neither this objection nor the interval
results disprove every finding of a conjunction error. Descriptive fit
alone does not establish the normative authority of coherence.

\subsection{Four reports and their boundaries}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-evidence-boundaries}

Four empirical claims in the chapter are examined here in more detail:
the Aristotle-thesis report, the strengthening and contraposition
report, the report on argument~(3) at \(p(B)=1\), and the modus ponens
reports. Each is a report of cited studies. Each is given with what the
chapter says about its task and with what it leaves unstated; none is
used as a premise of any formal result above.

\emph{Aristotle's theses.} The chapter reports that people believe
(AT1) and (AT2) must be true, citing Pfeifer (2012), says that this
matches the probability-logic validation, citing Pfeifer (2012) and
Pfeifer and Tulkki (2017b), and calls the experimental support strong
\cite[p.~280]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Task and measure, as described.} Participants judged
      whether \(\sim(\text{if }\sim A\text{ then }A)\) and
      \(\sim(\text{if }A\text{ then }\sim A)\) must be true. The
      chapter does not give the natural-language wording, the response
      options, or the proportion of participants or responses that
      answered ``must be true''; ``strong'' is the chapter's summary,
      not a reported statistic.
\item \emph{Population, design and uncertainty.} None is stated in the
      chapter for either cited study: no sample, recruitment, design,
      or interval of uncertainty, and none is supplied here.
\item \emph{Competing explanations.} A ``must be true'' response fits
      the narrow-scope conditional-event reading of
      \cref{def:p15-rat44-connexive-reading}, under which both theses
      receive probability one for contingent \(A\). It does not by
      itself show that participants used that reading: the same answer
      could come from rejecting a self-contradictory-sounding conditional
      with no probability assessment at all, or from a wide-scope reading
      of the negation, a reading for which the chapter refers to other work
      (its note~4). The
      response does count against the material reading only on the
      assumption that participants parsed the negation and the
      conditional as the theorist does, since materially (AT1) and (AT2)
      are equivalent to \(A\) and \(\sim A\).
\item \emph{Generalization.} The report concerns the two Aristotle
      theses. The chapter states that the Boethius theses and Abelard's
      first principle still need empirical research, so the report is
      not evidence for them, and it is not evidence about connexive
      calculi as wholes.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Strengthening and contraposition.} The chapter reports that the
majority of participants correctly understood that strengthening of
the antecedent, its argument~(6), and contraposition are
probabilistically noninformative \cite[p.~281]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
The sentence carries no citation of its own; it follows, with
``Moreover'', the report of strong agreement on the other System P
rules cited to Pfeifer and Kleiter (2005, 2006b, 2010) and Pfeifer and
Tulkki (2017b), and the chapter does not say which of these studies
included the two tasks.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Task and measure, as described.} The forms are those of
      items~1 and~2 of \cref{thm:p15-rat44-noninformation}: a premise
      value \(x\) for \(p(F\mid B)\), or for \(p(B\mid A)\), and a judged
      value or interval for \(p(F\mid(B\mathbin{\&}P))\), or for the
      contrapositive conditional. The coherent answer is the whole unit
      interval. The chapter does not say how responses were elicited or
      classified, or what proportion ``majority'' denotes.
\item \emph{Population, design and uncertainty.} Not stated, and not
      supplied here.
\item \emph{Competing explanations.} ``Correctly'' in the report
      already takes coherence as the standard. A whole-interval response
      is what that standard prescribes, but it is also how a participant
      could express not knowing, or declining to infer anything, without
      having judged that the premise leaves the conclusion unconstrained.
      With content-rich material such as the bird and penguin example,
      background knowledge could also move a response away from the
      unit interval without any failure to grasp noninformativeness; the
      chapter does not say which kind of material was used.
\item \emph{Generalization.} The report covers these two forms. It is
      separate from the transitivity report, which the chapter interprets
      as a CUT reading, and it says nothing about cases in which extra
      dependence information makes the conclusion informative.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Argument (3) at \(p(B)=1\).} The chapter reports that people
interpret ``\(B\); therefore, if \(A\), then \(B\)'' as
probabilistically noninformative even when \(p(B)=1\), citing Pfeifer
and Kleiter (2011) \cite[p.~279]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Task and measure, as described.} The chapter describes the
      finding only at the level of argument~(3): a premise probability
      for \(B\), including the value one, and a judged probability for
      the conditional. It does not state the materials, the response
      format (point value or interval), how a response was classified as
      noninformative, or the proportion of participants or responses so
      classified.
\item \emph{Population, design and uncertainty.} None is stated in the
      chapter: no sample, recruitment, design, or interval of
      uncertainty is reported, and none is supplied here.
\item \emph{Competing explanations.} A response of the whole interval
      \([0,1]\) is what the coherence standard prescribes for logically
      independent \(A\) and \(B\), but the chapter's report does not
      separate that from
      other routes to the same response, such as treating the
      conditional as unrelated to the premise whatever its value, or
      reading a stated certainty as less than certain. Which of these
      produced the responses is not settled by the report.
\item \emph{Generalization.} The report concerns this argument form
      at the stated premise value. It is not a finding about
      zero-probability antecedents in general, and it does not show that
      participants assigned \(A\) probability zero, which is the only
      case in which coherence and quotient conditioning differ at
      \(p(B)=1\).
\end{itemize}
The chapter uses this report as the empirical step of its argument
about zero-probability antecedents (step~2 of the reconstruction in
\cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-endpoint-argument}). The formal step that coherence
allows every value at \(p(B)=1\) is proved independently of it.

\emph{Nonprobabilistic modus ponens.} The chapter reports that most
participants usually endorse the nonprobabilistic version of modus
ponens, citing Evans, Newstead and Byrne (1993), and then reports that
a clear majority of responses in probabilistic modus ponens tasks agree
with the probability-logic predictions, citing Pfeifer and Kleiter (2009)
and Pfeifer and Tulkki (2017b) \cite[p.~281]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Task and measure, as described.} The first report says
      only that the nonprobabilistic version, the argument
      ``if \(B\), then \(C\); \(B\); therefore \(C\)'' stated without
      probabilities, is endorsed; the chapter does not say how
      endorsement was elicited. The second concerns tasks on the
      probabilistic version, for which the probability-logic prediction
      given \(p(B)=x\) and \(p(C\mid B)=y\) is the interval
      \([xy,xy+1-x]\) of \cref{thm:p15-rat44-propagation}; the chapter
      does not say how responses were compared with it.
      The chapter gives no endorsement rate for the first and no
      proportion for the second beyond ``usually'', ``most'' and
      ``clear majority''.
\item \emph{Population, design and uncertainty.} Not stated for either
      report, and not supplied here.
\item \emph{Competing explanations and limits.} High endorsement of
      categorical modus ponens is consistent with classical validity and
      also with the probabilistic rule, since premises judged certain
      give conclusion probability one under the interval above. The
      report therefore does not discriminate between the two frameworks,
      and it is not evidence for the interval predictions, which concern
      the separate probabilistic task. Endorsement in the categorical
      task does not transfer to other argument forms or to tasks with
      uncertain premises.
\end{itemize}
These are descriptive reports. That most participants respond as a
standard predicts does not make the standard correct, and that some do
not does not by itself make their responses irrational.

\section{Nested-conditionals boundary and handbook comparison}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-rat44-nested-source}{cited=Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}{The chapter's reported nested-MP extension}
The chapter reports an extension using conditional random quantities
and conditional previsions: from ``the cup breaks if dropped'' and
``if the cup breaks if dropped, then the cup is fragile,'' the lower
conclusion bound is the product of the two premise assessments and
the upper bound is that product plus one minus the first assessment.
This is the reported \([xy,xy+1-x]\) nested-MP result. It requires
the richer conditional-random-quantity framework cited there; it is
not obtained by forming \(p(F\mid(B\mid D))\) in the simple
conditional-event language. The chapter states that import--export
fails in that richer approach and connects the distinction to avoiding
Lewis-type triviality. It gives no complete construction or derivation
of that extension, and explicitly calls for further psychological
evaluation \cite[p.~282, note~1]{Pfeifer2021ProbabilityLogic}.
\end{hbtheorem}

The other handbook treatments that this chapter touches compare as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item H\'ajek and Staffel's survey of subjective probability
      (\cref{sec:p15-rationality-4-1}) is the chapter's own reference
      for no-Dutch-book coherence, and the finite Dutch-book
      equivalence is \cref{thm:p17-finite-price-coherence}.
      \Cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book-bridge} shows that the
      called-off condition agrees with that criterion on unconditional
      events, and that its restriction to live cases is what makes it
      bite on conditional events. The survey's ratio conditional,
      undefined at probability zero, its objections to the betting
      identification, and its diachronic arguments are kept apart from
      this chapter's synchronic primitive conditional prices in
      \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-dutch-book}.
\item Finite probability supplies masses, expectations, and quotient
      conditioning at positive probability
      (\cref{sec:p15-finite-probability,def:p15-finite-conditioning}).
      The primitive conditional assessment used here remains meaningful
      on a possible null event; it does not repair the quotient by
      assigning a value to \(0/0\). Finite-portfolio coherence supplies
      finite additivity; countable additivity in the measurable-probability
      treatment is an additional requirement, not an implication of
      the finite betting test alone.
\item Bayesian confirmation and model comparison evaluate evidence
      under a stated prior/likelihood model
      (\cref{sec:p15-bayes-odds,sec:p15-bayesian-model-comparison}).
      Tight consequence here ranges over \emph{all} coherent extensions
      of the given premises and does not silently select a prior,
      update, or conditional-independence hypothesis.
\item The lower-prevision treatment has one-sided acceptable buying
      prices, full gamble domains, and separately specified natural or
      regular conditional extensions
      (\cref{def:p15-ip-coherence,def:p15-ip-conditional,def:p15-ip-updates}).
      Interval constraints here describe possible precise selections;
      equal-looking intervals are not automatically the same coherent
      lower-prevision assessment or update rule.
\item PCTL compares a path-event measure with a threshold in a Markov
      model, possibly optimizing over schedulers (\cref{def:p09-pctl}).
      It supplies neither this conditional-event semantics nor its
      Dutch-book consequence relation.
\item The Part~VIII System P treatment gives a qualitative semantics by
      smooth preferential models
      (\cref{sec:p08-paraconsistent-and-nonmonotonic,sec:p08-klm-preferential-representation}).
      Its differences from the coherence semantics, and the derivation
      of monotonicity from contraposition or transitivity, are in
      \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-038-system-p}.
\item The Part~XIII account of Abelard's rejection of self-negating
      consequences is historical and makes no probabilistic claim
      (\cref{sec:p13-abelard-topics}). Its relation to the connexive

The chapter's final proposal combines qualitative nonmonotonic and
connexive principles with quantitative beliefs. Its suggested explanation
for the earlier neglect of nonclassical logics is research tradition,
not a demonstrated causal finding. Its cited technical results,
experimental reports, and future directions have been kept separate;
its reference list is not a new obligation to reconstruct every cited
book in full.
