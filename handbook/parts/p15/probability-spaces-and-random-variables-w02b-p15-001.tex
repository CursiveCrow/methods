\chapter{Explicit Moments, Norm Inequalities and Integral Approximations}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-poisson-geometric}

This chapter completes three groups of calculations that rest on the
integral of Chapter~\ref{sec:p15-integration-expectation}. The source
is Sections~1.4--1.6 of Durrett's author version of
\emph{Probability: Theory and Examples}, dated 11 January 2019
\cite[pp.~18--37]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The first group computes
moments: those of the uniform variable on \((0,1)\) directly from
simple-function bounds, and those of the Poisson and geometric laws
from series, following Examples~1.6.13 and~1.6.14
\cite[pp.~34--35]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The second group proves the
general \(L^p\) facts of Durrett's Exercises~1.5.1--1.5.3: the
essential bound, the limit of \(p\)-norms and Minkowski's inequality
\cite[p.~28]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The third group proves the
approximation statements of Exercises~1.4.2, 1.5.7 and~1.5.9
\cite[pp.~24, 28]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The purpose throughout is to
show each limit interchange and each inequality with its hypotheses
in place, so that the formulas can be used later without hidden
conditions.

\emph{Inputs.} A measure space \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)\), with no
finiteness or sigma-finiteness assumed unless a statement says so; real
or nonnegative measurable functions on it; and, for the moment
calculations, a real random variable with a uniform, Poisson or
geometric law. The integral, expectation, variance and the sizes
\(\|f\|_p\) are those of
\cref{def:p15-nonnegative-integral,def:p15-integrable-functions,def:p15-lp-convergence,def:p15-expectation-integral}.
\emph{Conclusions.} The mean and second moment of the uniform variable;
every factorial moment of the Poisson and geometric laws, with their
means and variances; the essential bound, the limit
\(\|f\|_p\to\|f\|_\infty\) on finite measures, and Minkowski's
inequality for \(1\leq p\leq\infty\) on every measure space; and three
approximations of an integral or of a function by simple functions.

Durrett states his integration results for a sigma-finite measure
\cite[p.~18]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. No proof below uses
sigma-finiteness. Two features of the moment calculations also differ
from the source. Durrett obtains the series behind the geometric moments
by differentiating the geometric series term by term and refers to an
appendix result for the exchange
\cite[Example~A.5.6, pp.~472--473]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The proofs
below use no differentiation: they regroup a nonnegative double series,
which \cref{thm:p15-countable-atomic} permits without any convergence
hypothesis. Second, the geometric factorial moments are stated for every
order \(r\); Durrett states only the orders one and two, and the higher
orders are an extension made here.

\section{The uniform moments from simple bounds}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-uniform}

Let \((\Omega_U,\mathcal F_U,\mathbb U)\) be the uniform probability
space on \((0,1)\) and \(U(u)=u\) its identity observation
(\cref{thm:p15-lebesgue-unit-interval}). The computation in
\cref{def:p15-computed-expectations} obtains the mean and second moment
of \(U\) by trapping \(U\) between two simple functions. The next
theorem supplies the finite sums that the trap needs and the limit
argument.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-uniform-moments}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-001-uniform-moments}{Sums of integers and squares, and the uniform moments}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every integer \(m\geq1\),
      \[
       \begin{aligned}
       \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}k&=\frac{(m-1)m}2,\qquad
       \sum_{k=1}^{m}k=\frac{m(m+1)}2,\\
       \sum_{k=0}^{m-1}k^2&=\frac{(m-1)m(2m-1)}6,\\
       \sum_{k=1}^{m}k^2&=\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}6.
       \end{aligned}
      \]
\item For every integer \(m\geq1\), the cells
      \(C_k\defeq(k/m,(k+1)/m]\cap(0,1)\), \(0\leq k\leq m-1\), are
      pairwise disjoint events of probability \(1/m\) with union
      \((0,1)\). The simple functions
      \[
       s_m\defeq\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac km\mathbf1_{C_k},\qquad
       t_m\defeq\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{k+1}m\mathbf1_{C_k}
      \]
      satisfy \(0\leq s_m\leq U\leq t_m\) and
      \(s_m^2\leq U^2\leq t_m^2\) everywhere on \((0,1)\), and
      \[
       \begin{aligned}
       \frac{m-1}{2m}&\leq\mathbb E[U]\leq\frac{m+1}{2m},\\
       \frac{(m-1)m(2m-1)}{6m^3}&\leq\mathbb E[U^2]
         \leq\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}{6m^3}.
       \end{aligned}
      \]
\item \(\mathbb E[U]=1/2\), \(\mathbb E[U^2]=1/3\) and
      \(\operatorname{Var}(U)=1/12\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

The lower and upper bounds in part~(2) differ by \(1/m\) for the mean and
by \(1/m\) for the second moment as well, since
\(\bigl(m(m+1)(2m+1)-(m-1)m(2m-1)\bigr)/(6m^3)=6m^2/(6m^3)\). With
\(m=10\) the second moment lies between \(0.285\) and \(0.385\).
Part~(3) is reached without a density and without the fundamental
theorem of calculus: only the interval lengths of
\cref{thm:p15-lebesgue-unit-interval} and the monotonicity of the
integral enter.

\section{Laws, descending products and the series tools}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-laws}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-laws}{Poisson and geometric laws and descending products}
Let \(\lambda>0\). A real random variable \(X\) has the \emph{Poisson
distribution with parameter \(\lambda\)} if
\[
 \mathbb P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}
 \qquad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).
\]
These masses sum to one by \cref{thm:p15-stirling-poisson-premises},
so \(\mathbb P(X\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\})=1\). The parameter-zero law, the
point mass at zero, is the convention of
\cref{def:p15-stirling-poisson-laws}.

Let \(0<p<1\) and write \(q\defeq1-p\). A real random variable \(N\)
has the \emph{geometric distribution with success probability \(p\)} if
\[
 \mathbb P(N=k)=p(1-p)^{k-1}=pq^{k-1}\qquad(k=1,2,\ldots).
\]
These masses sum to one by part~(1) of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-geometric-moments}, so
\(\mathbb P(N\in\{1,2,\ldots\})=1\). The endpoint \(p=1\) is the point
mass at one, as in \cref{def:p15-collector-and-cycle-models}.

For a real number \(x\) and an integer \(k\geq1\), the
\emph{descending product of order \(k\)} is
\(x(x-1)\cdots(x-k+1)\), the product of the \(k\) factors \(x-i\),
\(0\leq i\leq k-1\). Its expectation, when defined, is the
\emph{factorial moment of order \(k\)}. For an integer \(j\geq0\) the
descending product is zero when \(j<k\), because the factor with
\(i=j\) vanishes, and it equals \(j!/(j-k)!\) when \(j\geq k\). On the
nonnegative integers it is therefore nonnegative.
For integers \(0\leq r\leq n\),
\(\binom nr\defeq n!/(r!\,(n-r)!)\).
\index{Poisson distribution!moments}\index{geometric distribution}
\index{factorial moment}
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-series-tools}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-001-series-tools}{Expectations as series and the negative-binomial series}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let \(X\) be a real random variable and \(S\subseteq\mathbb R\)
      a countable set with \(\mathbb P(X\in S)=1\). For every Borel
      \(h:\mathbb R\to[0,\infty)\),
      \[
       \mathbb E[h(X)]=\sum_{s\in S}h(s)\,\mathbb P(X=s)
      \]
      in \([0,\infty]\). If instead \(h:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is
      Borel and \(h\geq0\) on \(S\), the random
      variable \(h(X)\) is nonnegative a.s., its expectation is defined,
      and the same identity holds.
\item Let \(0\leq q<1\) and \(p=1-q\). For every integer \(r\geq0\),
      \[
       \sum_{m\geq0}\binom{m+r}{r}q^m=p^{-(r+1)}.
      \]
\end{enumerate}
Here \(q^0\defeq1\), also when \(q=0\).
Part~(2) with \(r=0\) is the geometric series
\(\sum_{m\geq0}q^m=1/p\).
\end{hblemma}

\section{The Poisson calculation}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-poisson}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-poisson-moments}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-001-poisson-moments}{Poisson factorial moments}
This is Durrett's Example~1.6.13 \cite[p.~34]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}.
Let \(X\) have the Poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda>0\).
For every integer \(k\geq1\), the descending product
\(X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)\) is nonnegative a.s. and
\[
 \mathbb E\bigl(X(X-1)\cdots(X-k+1)\bigr)=\lambda^k.
\]
Consequently \(\mathbb EX=\lambda\), \(\mathbb E[X(X-1)]=\lambda^2\),
\(\mathbb EX^2=\lambda^2+\lambda<\infty\), and
\[
 \operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb EX^2-(\mathbb EX)^2
   =\mathbb E[X(X-1)]+\mathbb EX-\lambda^2=\lambda.
\]
For the point mass at zero every factorial moment and the variance are
zero, which is the value \(\lambda^k\) at \(\lambda=0\).
\end{hbtheorem}

The orders one and two, and the variance, are also the moment assertions
of \cref{thm:p15-stirling-poisson-premises}; the theorem here adds every
higher order by the source's single index shift.

\section{The geometric calculation}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-geometric}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-geometric-moments}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-001-geometric-moments}{Geometric moments and first occurrence}
Parts~(1)--(3) are Durrett's Example~1.6.14
\cite[p.~35]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}, with part~(2) extended to every order.
Let \(0<p<1\) and \(q=1-p\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(\sum_{k\geq1}pq^{k-1}=1\).
\item If \(N\) has the geometric distribution with success probability
      \(p\), then for every integer \(r\geq1\) the descending product
      \(N(N-1)\cdots(N-r+1)\) is nonnegative a.s. and
      \[
       \mathbb E\bigl(N(N-1)\cdots(N-r+1)\bigr)
        =\frac{r!\,q^{r-1}}{p^r}.
      \]
\item In particular
      \[
       \mathbb EN=\frac1p,\qquad
       \mathbb E[N(N-1)]=\frac{2(1-p)}{p^2},
      \]
      and
      \[
       \operatorname{Var}(N)=\frac{2(1-p)}{p^2}+\frac1p-\frac1{p^2}
        =\frac{1-p}{p^2}.
      \]
\item Let \(A_1,A_2,\ldots\) be independent events with
      \(\mathbb P(A_i)=p\) for every \(i\). Put
      \(N(\omega)\defeq\min\{i\geq1:\omega\in A_i\}\), and
      \(N(\omega)\defeq\infty\) when \(\omega\) lies in no \(A_i\).
      Then \(N\) is an extended real random variable,
      \(\mathbb P(N=\infty)=0\), and
      \(\mathbb P(N=k)=pq^{k-1}\) for every \(k\geq1\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Part~(4) makes precise Durrett's reading of \(N\) as the count of
independent trials up to and including the first occurrence of an event
of probability \(p\)
\cite[p.~35]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. Changing \(N\) to the value one on
the null event \(\{N=\infty\}\) gives a real random variable with the
same law, to which parts~(2) and~(3) apply. At \(p=1\) the variable is
one a.s.; part~(2) then reads \(1\) for \(r=1\) and \(0\) for
\(r\geq2\), which is the displayed formula with the convention
\(0^0=1\). The orders one and two also appear, by a differentiation
argument, inside the proof of \cref{thm:p15-coupon-collector-law}.

\section{Procedure, worked values and limits of the series moments}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-procedure}

\begin{hbmethod}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-method}{Computing a moment of a variable on the nonnegative integers}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item Check that the variable takes values in \(\{0,1,2,\ldots\}\)
      with probability one. This is the side condition that makes each
      descending product nonnegative and makes the terms below its order
      vanish.
\item Write the factorial moment as a nonnegative series by part~(1) of
      \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-series-tools}. Discard the terms
      \(j<k\), replace the descending product by \(j!/(j-k)!\), and
      shift the index. A nonnegative series may be reindexed, regrouped
      and multiplied by a finite nonnegative constant without any
      convergence check (\cref{thm:p15-countable-atomic}).
\item Recover ordinary moments from the pointwise identities
      \(x^2=x(x-1)+x\) and \(x^3=x(x-1)(x-2)+3x(x-1)+x\), whose
      summands are nonnegative on the nonnegative integers, so that
      nonnegative additivity applies.
\item Subtract only finite quantities. The variance formula
      \[
       \operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X(X-1)]+\mathbb EX-(\mathbb EX)^2
      \]
      is used only after \(\mathbb EX^2<\infty\) has been shown
      (\cref{thm:p15-expectation-bounds}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbmethod}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-worked}{A third Poisson moment and a fair-coin waiting time}
Let \(X\) be Poisson with \(\lambda=2\). The theorem gives
\(\mathbb EX=2\), \(\mathbb E[X(X-1)]=4\) and
\(\mathbb E[X(X-1)(X-2)]=8\). Expanding,
\(x(x-1)(x-2)+3x(x-1)+x=x^3-3x^2+2x+3x^2-3x+x=x^3\). Each summand
is nonnegative on the nonnegative integers, so nonnegative additivity
gives
\[
 \mathbb EX^3=8+3\cdot4+2=22.
\]
Also \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=4+2-4=2\).

Let \(N\) be geometric with \(p=1/2\), the law of
\cref{def:p15-countable-waiting}. Then
\(\mathbb EN=2\), \(\mathbb E[N(N-1)]=2\cdot(1/2)/(1/4)=4\),
\(\mathbb EN^2=6\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(N)=6-4=2=q/p^2\).
As a direct check of the mean, the partial sums of
\(\sum_{k\geq1}k2^{-k}\) are
\(1/2,\ 1,\ 11/8,\ 13/8,\ 57/32\) for one to five terms; the
annex shows that the \(K\)-th partial sum is \(2-(K+2)2^{-K}\).
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-limits}{Where the calculation fails}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Values off the nonnegative integers.} If \(X=1/2\) a.s.,
      then \(X(X-1)=-1/4\) a.s. The descending product is negative and
      no term of the series vanishes for being below the order, so the
      index shift of the method has nothing to act on. The factorial
      moment route requires the side condition of step~(1).
\item \emph{An infinite mean.} Let \(M\) take the value \(k\geq1\) with
      probability \(1/(k(k+1))\). These masses sum to one, but
      \(\mathbb EM=\sum_{k\geq1}1/(k+1)=\infty\) and
      \(\mathbb E[M(M-1)]=\infty\). The expression
      \(\mathbb E[M(M-1)]+\mathbb EM-(\mathbb EM)^2\) would subtract an
      infinite quantity from an infinite quantity, and \(M\) has no
      variance since \(\mathbb EM^2=\infty\). Step~(4) of the method
      blocks this computation.
\item \emph{Success probability zero.} At \(p=0\) every geometric mass
      \(p(1-p)^{k-1}\) is zero, so the formula defines no probability
      law. In part~(4) of the geometric theorem with
      \(\mathbb P(A_i)=0\), the first-occurrence index equals \(\infty\)
      almost surely. The restriction \(0<p\) is therefore needed.
\end{enumerate}
These three assertions, and the partial sums of the worked example, are
proved at the end of the annex item for
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-geometric-moments}.
\end{hbwarning}

\section{Essential bounds and Minkowski's inequality}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp}

Throughout this section \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)\) is an arbitrary
measure space and \(f,g\) are real measurable functions. The sizes
\(\|f\|_p\), \(1\leq p<\infty\), and \(\|f\|_\infty\) are those of
\cref{def:p15-lp-convergence}; each lies in \([0,\infty]\).
Durrett writes the essential supremum as the infimum of all \(M\) with
\(\mu(\{x:|f(x)|>M\})=0\), without the restriction \(M\geq0\)
\cite[Exercise~1.5.1, p.~28]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The two versions
agree whenever \(\mu(\Omega)>0\), because a negative \(M\) then fails
the condition; for the zero measure Durrett's version gives
\(-\infty\) and the version used here gives zero.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-space}{The space \(L^p(\mu)\)}
For \(1\leq p\leq\infty\), \(\mathcal L^p(\mu)\) is the set of real
measurable functions \(f\) on \(\Omega\) with \(\|f\|_p<\infty\), and
\(L^p(\mu)\) is the set of its classes under equality \(\mu\)-a.e.
For \(p=1\) this is the space \(L^1(\mu)\) of
\cref{def:p15-lp-convergence}. A \emph{seminorm} on a real vector space
is a map \(N\) into \([0,\infty)\) with \(N(cf)=|c|N(f)\) for real
\(c\) and \(N(f+g)\leq N(f)+N(g)\); it is a \emph{norm} when moreover
\(N(f)=0\) only for the zero vector.
\index{Lp space@\(L^p\) space}
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-results}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-results}{Essential bound, limit of norms and Minkowski's inequality}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Essential bound.} If \(\|f\|_\infty<\infty\), then
      \(|f|\leq\|f\|_\infty\) a.e., so the infimum defining
      \(\|f\|_\infty\) is attained. For every real \(M\geq0\),
      \(\|f\|_\infty\leq M\) holds exactly when \(|f|\leq M\) a.e.
      Consequently
      \(\int|fg|\,d\mu\leq\|f\|_1\|g\|_\infty\), with
      \(0\cdot\infty=0\).
\item \emph{Limit of norms.} If \(0<\mu(\Omega)<\infty\), and in
      particular if \(\mu\) is a probability measure, then
      \[
       \|f\|_\infty=\lim_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p
      \]
      in \([0,\infty]\), the limit being taken over real \(p\geq1\).
      If \(\mu\) is a probability measure, then
      \(\|f\|_p\leq\|f\|_\infty\) for every \(p\geq1\).
\item \emph{Minkowski's inequality.} For \(1\leq p\leq\infty\),
      \[
       \|f+g\|_p\leq\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p
      \]
      in \([0,\infty]\). For \(1\leq p<\infty\) also
      \(|f+g|^p\leq2^p(|f|^p+|g|^p)\) pointwise.
\item \emph{The space.} For \(1\leq p\leq\infty\),
      \(\mathcal L^p(\mu)\) is a real vector space under pointwise
      operations, \(\|\cdot\|_p\) is a seminorm on it, \(\|f\|_p=0\)
      exactly when \(f=0\) a.e., and functions equal a.e. have the same
      size. Hence \(L^p(\mu)\) is a real vector space and
      \(\|\cdot\|_p\) is a norm on it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Part~(1) is Durrett's Exercise~1.5.1; its inequality is also the case
\(p=1\), \(q=\infty\) of \cref{thm:p15-holder-integral}. Part~(2) is
his Exercise~1.5.2, stated there for a probability measure; the
finite-measure form is an extension made here. Part~(3) is his
Exercise~1.5.3, and its proof follows his hint: the pointwise bound
gives finiteness, and H\"older's inequality applied to
\(|f|\,|f+g|^{p-1}\) and \(|g|\,|f+g|^{p-1}\) gives the inequality
\cite[p.~28]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. For probability measures the norm
laws of part~(4) are also part of \cref{thm:p15-conditional-lp}; here
they hold on every measure space. No completeness of \(L^p(\mu)\) is
asserted.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-worked}{Norms on a two-point space}
Let \(\Omega=\{1,2\}\) with \(\mu(\{1\})=\mu(\{2\})=1/2\), and let
\(f(1)=1\), \(f(2)=2\). Then \(\|f\|_\infty=2\) and
\[
 \|f\|_p=\Bigl(\frac{1+2^p}2\Bigr)^{1/p},\qquad
 2\cdot2^{-1/p}\leq\|f\|_p\leq2,
\]
so \(\|f\|_p\to2\). At \(p=1\) and \(p=2\) the values are \(3/2\) and
\(\sqrt{5/2}\approx1.58\). For Minkowski's inequality let
\(g(1)=1\), \(g(2)=0\) and \(h(1)=0\), \(h(2)=1\). Then
\(\|g\|_p=\|h\|_p=2^{-1/p}\) for finite \(p\),
\(\|g\|_\infty=\|h\|_\infty=1\), and \(\|g+h\|_p=1\) for every \(p\).
The inequality reads \(1\leq2^{1-1/p}\) for finite \(p\) and
\(1\leq2\) at \(p=\infty\). It is an equality at \(p=1\) and strict for
\(1<p\leq\infty\).
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-limits}{Where the norm statements fail}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An infinite measure.} On \(\mathbb R\) with Lebesgue
      measure, let \(f=1\). Then \(\|f\|_\infty=1\), but
      \(\|f\|_p^p\) is the Lebesgue measure of \(\mathbb R\), which is
      infinite, for every real \(p\geq1\). The limit in part~(2) fails, so its finiteness
      hypothesis is needed.
\item \emph{Exponents below one.} For \(0<p<1\) put
      \(\|f\|_p\defeq(\int|f|^p\,d\mu)^{1/p}\) by the same formula.
      On the two-point space of the example, \(\|g+h\|_p=1\) while
      \(\|g\|_p+\|h\|_p=2^{1-1/p}<1\). The triangle inequality fails,
      so part~(3) needs \(p\geq1\).
\item \emph{Supremum and essential supremum.} On \(\mathbb R\) with
      Lebesgue measure, \(f=5\cdot\mathbf1_{\{0\}}\) has supremum five
      and \(\|f\|_\infty=0\), because the singleton is null. Part~(1)
      bounds \(|f|\) only outside a null set.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbwarning}

\section{Further approximations of the integral}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-001-approximation}

A \emph{real simple function} is a function
\(\varphi=\sum_{m=1}^nc_m\mathbf1_{A_m}\) with real \(c_m\) and
\(A_m\in\mathcal F\); this is Durrett's definition
\cite[Exercise~1.3.7, p.~17]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. Such a function is
measurable and takes finitely many real values. For real \(x\),
\(\lfloor x\rfloor\) is the largest integer at most \(x\).

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-approximations}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-001-approximations}{Dyadic sums, truncation, small sets and simple approximation in \(L^p\)}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Dyadic level sums.} Let \(f:\Omega\to[0,\infty)\) be
      measurable and
      \(E_{n,m}\defeq\{x:m/2^n\leq f(x)<(m+1)/2^n\}\) for integers
      \(n,m\geq0\). As \(n\uparrow\infty\),
      \[
       \sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac m{2^n}\mu(E_{n,m})\ \uparrow\ \int f\,d\mu.
      \]
\item \emph{Truncation.} If \(f:\Omega\to[0,\infty]\) is measurable,
      then \(\int f\wedge n\,d\mu\uparrow\int f\,d\mu\) as
      \(n\to\infty\), where \(f\wedge n\defeq\min(f,n)\).
\item \emph{Small sets.} If \(g\) is real and integrable and
      \(\epsilon>0\), there is \(\delta>0\) such that
      \(\mu(A)<\delta\) implies \(\int_A|g|\,d\mu<\epsilon\) for
      \(A\in\mathcal F\).
\item \emph{Simple approximation in \(L^p\).} Let \(1\leq p<\infty\)
      and let real measurable \(f\) satisfy \(\|f\|_p<\infty\). For
      integers \(n\geq1\) put
      \[
       \varphi_n\defeq\operatorname{sgn}(f)\,
         \min\bigl(n,\,2^{-n}\lfloor2^n|f|\rfloor\bigr),
      \]
      where \(\operatorname{sgn}(f)\) is \(1\), \(0\) or \(-1\) as
      \(f\) is positive, zero or negative. Each \(\varphi_n\) is a real
      simple function with \(|\varphi_n|\leq|f|\) and
      \(\mu(\{\varphi_n\neq0\})<\infty\), and
      \(\|\varphi_n-f\|_p\to0\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Parts~(1)--(4) are Durrett's Exercises~1.4.2, 1.5.7(i), 1.5.7(ii)
and~1.5.9 \cite[pp.~24, 28]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}. The explicit
sequence in part~(4), and the finite measure of its nonzero sets, are
choices made here; the exercise asks only for some simple functions.
Part~(3) holds on every measure space; the probability version for a
whole family of variables is the small-event control of
\cref{def:p15-uniform-integrability}. Exercise~1.4.3 (interval, step
and continuous approximation on the line) and Exercise~1.4.4 (the
Riemann--Lebesgue lemma) are proved in
\cref{thm:p15-criteria-real-integration}, and Exercise~1.4.1 is part~(1)
of \cref{thm:p15-nonnegative-integral-laws}.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-approx-worked}{Dyadic sums for the uniform variable}
On the uniform probability space take \(f=U\). For \(1\leq m\leq2^n-1\),
\(E_{n,m}=[m/2^n,(m+1)/2^n)\), an interval inside \((0,1)\) of length
\(2^{-n}\); for \(m\geq2^n\) the set is empty. The first identity of
part~(1) of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-uniform-moments}, with \(2^n\)
in place of \(m\), gives
\[
 \sum_{m=1}^{2^n-1}\frac m{2^n}\,2^{-n}
 =\frac{(2^n-1)2^n}{2\cdot4^n}=\frac12-2^{-n-1},
\]
which increases to \(1/2=\mathbb E[U]\), as part~(1) of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-approximations} requires. For \(n=1,2,3\)
the sums are \(1/4\), \(3/8\) and \(7/16\).
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-approx-limits}{Where the approximations fail}
\par\nobreak
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{An infinite value.} Let \(\Omega=\{1\}\),
      \(\mu(\{1\})=1\) and \(f(1)=\infty\). Every set \(E_{n,m}\) is
      empty, so every dyadic sum is zero, while \(\int f\,d\mu=\infty\).
      Part~(1) needs \(f\) to be finite. Part~(2) has no such
      restriction: \(\int f\wedge n\,d\mu=n\uparrow\infty\).
\item \emph{A nonintegrable function.} Let \(\Omega=\{1,2,\ldots\}\)
      with all subsets as events and \(\mu(\{k\})=2^{-k}\), and let
      \(g(k)=2^k\). Then \(\int_{\{k\}}|g|\,d\mu=1\) while
      \(\mu(\{k\})\to0\), so no \(\delta\) serves \(\epsilon=1\) in
      part~(3). Here \(\int|g|\,d\mu=\infty\).
\item \emph{The exponent infinity.} On \(\mathbb R\) with Lebesgue
      measure, \(f=1\) has \(\|f\|_\infty=1\). Every real simple
      \(\varphi\) whose nonzero set has finite measure vanishes on a
      set of infinite measure, where \(|\varphi-f|=1\); hence
      \(\|\varphi-f\|_\infty\geq1\). Part~(4), with nonzero sets of
      finite measure, has no analogue at \(p=\infty\).
\end{enumerate}
These assertions and the sums of the example are proved at the end of
the annex item for \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-approximations}; those of
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-worked,def:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-limits}
at the end of the item for \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-001-lp-results}.
\end{hbwarning}
