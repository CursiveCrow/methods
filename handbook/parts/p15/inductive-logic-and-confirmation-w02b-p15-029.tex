\section{The infinite system and its limit functions}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-infinite}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-systems}{The systems \(\mathfrak L_N\) and \(\mathfrak L_\infty\)}
The system \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) has a denumerable sequence of
individual constants \(a_1,a_2,\ldots\), designating all individuals of
its domain, with distinct constants designating distinct individuals.
It has finitely many primitive predicates, the identity sign \(=\), the
connectives, the universal quantifier and individual variables.
For \(N\geq1\), \(\mathfrak L_N\) has only \(a_1,\ldots,a_N\) and
otherwise the same signs. A sentence \(i\) of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\)
\emph{occurs} in \(\mathfrak L_N\) when every constant in \(i\)
has index at most \(N\). In each system a state description decides
every atomic sentence; an identity sentence with two different constants
is L-false, and one with the same constant twice is L-true. A sentence
is \emph{general} if it contains a variable, \emph{nongeneral}
otherwise, and \emph{purely general} if it is general and contains no
individual constant \cite[sec.~15A; D16-6f--h]{Carnap1950Probability}.
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-limits}{Limit functions: D56-1 and D56-2}
Let \(m_1,m_2,\ldots\) be regular \(m\)-functions for
\(\mathfrak L_1,\mathfrak L_2,\ldots\), and let \(c_N\) be the
regular \(c\)-function based on \(m_N\). For a sentence \(i\) of
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\), put
\[
 m_\infty(i)\defeq\lim_{N\to\infty}m_N(i)
\]
when this limit exists, the sequence being taken over the systems in
which \(i\) occurs; otherwise \(m_\infty\) has no value for \(i\).
Likewise \(c_\infty(h,e)\defeq\lim_N c_N(h,e)\) when the limit
exists, taken over those \(N\) for which \(c_N(h,e)\) is defined.
These are D56-1 and D56-2. By T56-2, \(c_\infty(h,e)\) has a value
exactly when \(e\) is not L-false in infinitely many
\(\mathfrak L_N\) and the values \(c_N(h,e)\) for those \(N\)
converge \cite[sec.~56, pp.~302--304]{Carnap1950Probability}.
\end{hbdefinition}

Two hypotheses are built into these definitions. The domain is
countable and every individual is named, so that \(\mathfrak L_\infty\)
is approached by the finite systems obtained by deleting all but the
first \(N\) names. Second, a value is defined only when the limit
exists; the definition does not itself assert existence. Carnap rejects
the alternative of a measure on the state descriptions of
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\) itself: that set has the cardinality of the
continuum, and a direct quotient \(m_\infty(e\land h)/m_\infty(e)\)
gives no value when \(m_\infty(e)=0\), although the finite quotients
may still converge. For \(e=\forall x\,M(x)\) with \(M\) factual,
\(c_N(M(a_1),e)=1\) for every \(N\), so \(c^*_\infty(M(a_1),e)=1\),
although \(m^*_\infty(e)=0\) by \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-monadic-limit}
\cite[sec.~56, discussion following T56-2; T56-4]{Carnap1950Probability}.

In the appendix the existence of the \(m^*\)-limit is asserted for
every general sentence, together with an effective and practicable
reduction to a nongeneral sentence, but no proof is printed. That
passage stands under the declared restriction of the remainder of
sec.~110 to systems whose primitive predicates all have degree one
\cite[sec.~110A, pp.~566--567]{Carnap1950Probability}. The next two
results supply proofs of those claims for the monadic systems.
Throughout, \(\mathfrak L_N\) and \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) have
\(\pi\geq1\) primitive predicates, all of degree one, and
\(\kappa=2^\pi\) \(Q\)-properties \(Q_1,\ldots,Q_\kappa\). The
quantifier rank of a formula is its maximal nesting depth of quantifiers.

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-saturation}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-saturation}{Counts above the quantifier rank do not matter}
Let \(i\) be a sentence of a monadic system whose individual constants
are exactly \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\) (\(m\geq0\)), and let \(r\) be its
quantifier rank. Let \(\mathcal A,\mathcal B\) be structures for the
predicates and these constants, with \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\) denoting
distinct elements in each. Suppose that each \(b_l\) has the same
\(Q\)-property in \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\), and that for
each \(j\) the numbers of elements of \(Q_j\) not denoted by any
\(b_l\) are either equal in \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) or are
both at least \(r\) (possibly infinite). Then
\(\mathcal A\models i\) if and only if \(\mathcal B\models i\).
\end{hblemma}

For a word \(u=(u_1,\ldots,u_m)\) of \(Q\)-properties, let
\(D_{r,u}\) be the finite structure with elements \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\)
of \(Q\)-properties \(u_1,\ldots,u_m\) and exactly \(r\) further
elements in every \(Q_j\). Let \(\langle u\rangle\) be the nongeneral
conjunction \(Q_{u_1}(b_1)\land\cdots\land Q_{u_m}(b_m)\).

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-monadic-limit}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-monadic-limit}{Existence, effective reduction, and 110A (5)}
Let \(i\) be any sentence of monadic \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), with
constants \(b_1,\ldots,b_m\) and quantifier rank \(r\). Put
\[
 S_i\defeq\{u\in\{1,\ldots,\kappa\}^m:D_{r,u}\models i\},\qquad
 i'\defeq\bigvee_{u\in S_i}\langle u\rangle ,
\]
with the empty disjunction read as \(\neg t\) and, when \(m=0\), the
single empty word giving \(t\) or \(\neg t\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \(m^*_\infty(i)\) exists and equals \(m^*(i')\). The sentence
       \(i'\) is nongeneral, contains no individual constant not in
       \(i\), and is computed by evaluating \(i\) in the
       \(\kappa^m\) finite structures \(D_{r,u}\).
 \item If \(i\) is purely general, \(m^*_\infty(i)\) is \(0\) or
       \(1\); it is \(1\) exactly when \(D_{r,\varnothing}\models i\).
 \item If \(M\) is a molecular predicate of width \(0<w<\kappa\),
       \(m^*_\infty(\forall x\,M(x))=0\).
 \item If \(m^*_\infty(e)>0\), then \(c^*_\infty(h,e)\) exists for
       every \(h\) and equals
       \(m^*_\infty(e\land h)/m^*_\infty(e)\), computable by (1).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Item (2) is Carnap's 110A (5); item (3) is the remark following it,
which leads to his formula (12), \(c^*(l,e)=0\) in
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\). The procedure in (1) is effective. Its cost
is \(\kappa^m\) evaluations of \(i\) in structures with
\(m+r\kappa\) elements; no claim is made here that this agrees with
the practicable procedure Carnap announces for his second volume. The
reduced sentence \(i'\) need not be L-equivalent to \(i\): for
\(i=\exists x\,Q_1(x)\), \(i'=t\), although \(i\) is not L-true.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-limit-example}{A general sentence with a constant}
Take \(\pi=1\), so \(\kappa=2\), and
\(i=P(a_1)\land\exists x(x\ne a_1\land\neg P(x))\), of rank one.
\(D_{1,u}\) contains \(a_1\) and one further element in each of
\(P,\neg P\); so \(S_i=\{P\}\), \(i'=P(a_1)\), and
\(m^*_\infty(i)=1/2\). Directly, \(i\) fails in a state where
\(a_1\) is \(P\) exactly when every individual is \(P\), and that
state has \(m^*_N\)-mass \(1/(N+1)\); so
\(m^*_N(i)=1/2-1/(N+1)\), which tends to \(1/2\).
\end{hbexample}

The comparative theorem T81-2a in the 1950 text transfers the finite
adequacy result of \cref{thm:p15-carnap-comparative} to
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\). Its printed proof rests on one logical step:
L-truth of the conditions of D81-1c in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) passes to
every \(\mathfrak L_N\) of a final segment (T20-11 is cited for it)
\cite[sec.~81, pp.~440--441]{Carnap1950Probability}. The following
results prove that step for arbitrary general sentences and then the
transfer.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-ltruth-transfer}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-ltruth-transfer}{L-truth passes from the infinite system to a final segment}
Let \(i\) be a sentence of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item (Monadic systems, no choice principle.) Let \(i\) have
       \(m\) constants, largest index \(n_0\), and rank \(r\geq1\).
       If \(i\) is L-true in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), then \(i\) is
       L-true in every \(\mathfrak L_N\) with
       \(N\geq\max\{n_0,\,m+\kappa(r-1)+1\}\). Conversely, if
       \(i\) is L-true in every \(\mathfrak L_N\) with
       \(N\geq\max\{n_0,\,m+\kappa r\}\), then it is L-true in
       \(\mathfrak L_\infty\).
 \item (Arbitrary finite relational signature, assuming the Axiom of
       Choice as in \cref{thm:fol-compactness,thm:downward-ls}.)
       If \(i\) is L-true in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), there is
       \(N_0\) such that \(i\) is L-true in every
       \(\mathfrak L_N\) with \(N\geq N_0\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-t81-transfer}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-t81-transfer}{T81-2a for general sentences}
Let \(h,e,h',e'\) be sentences of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), general or
not, with \(e,e'\) not L-false in any system in which they occur, and
suppose \(\operatorname{MC}(h,e;h',e')\) holds in
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\) (\cref{def:p15-carnap-comparative}). Then
\(\operatorname{MC}(h,e;h',e')\) holds in every \(\mathfrak L_N\)
of a final segment, so \(c_N(h,e)\geq c_N(h',e')\) there for every
regular sequence. Hence
\(c_\infty(h,e)\geq c_\infty(h',e')\) whenever both limits exist.
In the monadic systems the final segment is explicit by
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-ltruth-transfer}, and for \(c^*\) both
limits exist whenever \(m^*_\infty(e)>0\) and \(m^*_\infty(e')>0\).
\end{hbtheorem}

The limit-existence hypothesis cannot be dropped from the conclusion in
general; it is the condition T81-2a itself states. The converse, T81-2b,
is stated by Carnap for nongeneral sentences only, and is not extended
here.

\section{Probability\texorpdfstring{\(_1\)}{1} as support, betting quotient, and estimate}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-interpretations}

Section~41 clarifies the explicandum before any explicatum is
defined. It gives three explanations of the probability\(_1\) of
\(h\) on \(e\): a measure of evidential support, a fair betting
quotient, and an estimate of relative frequency
\cite[sec.~41, pp.~162--175]{Carnap1950Probability}. The circularity
among them is permitted, Carnap says, only because these are
clarifications of explicanda; the later system defines its concepts
in a noncircular chain.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-betting}{Stakes, betting quotient, estimate}
In a bet on \(h\) between \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), \(X_1\) receives
\(u_2\) if \(h\) is true and pays \(u_1\) if it is false;
\(u_1,u_2\geq0\) and \(u_1+u_2>0\). The \emph{betting ratio} is
\(u_1:u_2\) and the \emph{betting quotient} is
\(q=u_1/(u_1+u_2)\). The bettors pool their knowledge \(e\). The
statement that the probability\(_1\) of \(h\) on \(e\) is \(q\)
is read as: a bet on \(h\) with quotient \(q\) is \emph{fair}, that
is, favours neither side. If a magnitude has finitely many possible
values \(u_1,\ldots,u_n\), its \emph{estimate} on \(e\) is the
probability\(_1\)-weighted mean of these values (41D (3)).
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-betting}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-betting}{The arithmetic of sec.~41}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Every betting quotient lies in \([0,1]\), and \(q\) for
       \(h\) is the quotient \(1-q\) for \(\neg h\) from the other
       side (41B (1), (2)).
 \item If \(X_1\) makes the same bet with quotient \(q\) on each of
       \(n\) individuals of a class \(K\) having \(M\), and the
       relative frequency of \(M\) in \(K\) is \(r\), his total gain
       is \(n(u_1+u_2)(r-q)\).
 \item If the possible numbers of \(M\)-members of \(K\) carry
       probabilities\(_1\) summing to one, the estimates satisfy
       \(r'=m'/n\) and \(g'=n(u_1+u_2)(r'-q)\) (41D (4), (5)). The
       estimated gain is zero exactly when \(q=r'\) (41D (6)).
 \item (41A) Let support values be nonnegative, at most one, and
       additive over L-exclusive hypotheses, with the L-true sentence
       at one. If \(j_1,\ldots,j_n\) are L-exclusive in pairs and
       L-disjunct and equally supported by \(e\), each has support
       \(1/n\) and a disjunction of \(k\) of them has \(k/n\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Carnap regards the assumptions in item (4) as arbitrary when only
evidential support is in view; they are justified by the betting and
estimate explanations, which yield items (1)--(3). When \(e\) states
that \(r\) is the frequency of \(M\) in \(K\), the only quotient
neither bettor will refuse is \(r\) (41C); when \(r\) is unknown, the
fair quotient is its estimate (41D (6)). Two further claims make the
estimate explanation coherent: the estimate of the frequency of \(M\)
in an unobserved class does not depend on the class (41D (7)), and it
equals the probability\(_1\) of the singular prediction (41D (8)).
Carnap prints the derivation of (8) from (7) and defers (7) to the
later T106-1d. More generally, among hypotheses of equal
probability\(_1\) \(q\), the estimated relative frequency of truth
is \(q\) (41D (9), later T104-2c). For symmetric \(c\)-functions these
are the identities of \cref{thm:p15-carnap-frequency-reliability}.

The probability\(_2\) of an outcome is a physical magnitude whose value
may be unknown, and one may ask for its probability\(_1\) on evidence.
A probability\(_1\) statement is analytic, so its own probability\(_1\)
on any evidence is \(0\) or \(1\). Carnap applies this to the die
example of 41D: from a statement (ii) that the probability\(_1\) of an
ace is \(1/6\) on symmetric-shape evidence, the factual statement that
the long-run frequency \emph{will be} \(1/6\) does not follow; the
statement that its \emph{estimate} is \(1/6\) does, and a high
probability\(_1\) for a frequency near \(1/6\) follows only with the
restricting conditions of Bernoulli's theorem
(\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-bernoulli}).

\section{Other conceptions, presuppositions of induction, and the two senses of probability (secs.~41E--F, 42)}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-two-senses}

These passages contain no theorem. Their claims are of three kinds,
marked below: \emph{historical} (what an author held or how a usage
arose), \emph{philosophical} (about the nature or justification of
inductive statements), and \emph{analytic in the system} (statements
that, by Carnap's thesis, are to be proved from the later definitions).
The explicatum is not yet defined in this part of the book; the
statements are explanations of explicanda
\cite[sec.~41F, p.~182]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\emph{Reichenbach (41E; historical and philosophical).} Carnap finds
agreement with Reichenbach on the two explicanda: Reichenbach's
frequency concept is probability\(_2\), and his logical concept or
\emph{weight}, which depends on the state of knowledge, is
probability\(_1\) or close to it. Reichenbach's analysis of wagers
leads him to estimation (appraisal), and the height of a bettor's
stake reflects an appraised weight; Carnap reads this as the bettor's
estimate of a relative frequency fixing the betting quotient. The
parting comes when Reichenbach identifies weight with relative
frequency itself. Carnap proposes identifying it instead with the
\emph{estimate} of relative frequency, which would bring the two
views into agreement on fundamentals, and argues that Reichenbach
cannot reject probability\(_1\) in this interpretation, since in some
applications it coincides with his own concept of an estimate of
relative frequency; a theory of estimation is what would complete his
system \cite[sec.~41E, pp.~175--176]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\emph{Truth and probability (41E; philosophical).} Against the view
that probability\(_1\) should replace truth, and Reichenbach's view of
probability logic as a many-valued logic replacing the two truth
values, Carnap distinguishes \emph{true} from \emph{known to be true},
\emph{completely verified} or \emph{confirmed to the maximal degree}.
The latter group is relative to evidence and is properly replaced by
the graded concept of probability\(_1\); truth is not relative to
evidence. Inductive logic therefore remains two-valued: the
counterpart in deductive logic of the scale of probability\(_1\) is the
dichotomy between L-implication and its absence for a pair of
sentences, not that between truth and falsity. If the probability\(_1\)
of \(h\) on \(e\) is \(2/3\), \(h\) is still true or false
\cite[sec.~41E, pp.~176--177]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\emph{Presuppositions of induction (41F; philosophical).} A person
\(X\) decides by probabilities\(_1\) relative to his evidence. Carnap
numbers the statements of the argument:
(1) a prediction (rain tomorrow);
(2) the statement that its probability\(_1\) on the available evidence
is high, all that \(X\) can know and sufficient for a decision, for
instance a bet at quotient \(4/5\) if that is the value;
(3) the statement that \(X\) will be successful in the long run if he
keeps deciding by probabilities\(_1\) and estimates, and in particular
that a long series of bets at quotients never above the
probability\(_1\) will not end in a loss.
Statement (3) is factual, not logically necessary; it would be true if
the world had a suitable uniformity. Two formulations of the principle
of uniformity are (4) that the degree of uniformity of the world is
high, and (5) that a high relative frequency \(r\) of a property in a
long initial segment of a series is followed by approximately \(r\) in a
sufficiently long continuation. Carnap prefers the quantitative form
(4). The principle is synthetic, and the familiar dilemma follows:
confirming it inductively is circular if induction presupposes it,
which leads some to skepticism and others to abandon empiricism
\cite[sec.~41F, pp.~177--179]{Carnap1950Probability}.

Carnap's reply has two steps. First, \(X\) does not need certainty of
long-run success; its probability suffices, so (3) may be replaced by
(6a), that a long series of such bets will \emph{very probably} not end
in a loss, or (6b), that the \emph{estimate} of the total balance is
not negative; and (4), (5) by (7a), (7b) (high probability, or high
estimate, of the degree of uniformity on the available evidence) and
(8a), (8b) (high probability that the frequency continues near \(r\),
or an estimate near \(r\), on evidence of a long initial segment with
frequency \(r\)). Many contemporary philosophers, he says, accept that
probable success suffices, but some, Russell among them, still treat
the probability of uniformity as a synthetic statement that cannot be
confirmed without circularity, and conclude that empiricism must give
way. Second, on Carnap's conception every statement of
probability\(_1\) or of an estimate is, if true, analytic, including
(7a)--(8b); it needs no empirical confirmation, and the circle
disappears. A decision rests on two statements: the factual report of
the total evidence and the analytic statement of probability\(_1\),
which adds no factual content but makes explicit the inductive relation
between them. A decision made by the probabilities\(_1\) on the
available evidence is reasonable even if it fails
\cite[sec.~41F, pp.~179--181]{Carnap1950Probability}.

The analytic status of (8a), (8b), and statements like (7a), (7b)
after definitions of uniformity and randomness, is promised for the
second volume, on the basis of the definition of degree of
confirmation and of an estimate function; Carnap calls the discussion
of sec.~41F preliminary and nonexact \cite[sec.~41F, pp.~181--182]{Carnap1950Probability}.
In the 1950 volume itself, the nearest proved statements of the kind
(8a), (8b) are those for \(c^*\) in sec.~110: the predictive value
\((s_1+w)/(s+\kappa)\) of \cref{thm:p15-carnap-predictive} tends to the
observed relative frequency as \(s\) grows, and
\cref{thm:p15-carnap-frequency-reliability} gives the estimate of a
frequency in an unobserved class. These are statements about \(c^*\),
not proofs of (7a) or (7b), whose concept of degree of uniformity the
1950 volume does not define.

\emph{The shift in meaning (42A; historical).} By etymology and early
usage, ``probable'' meant something not certain but reasonably
expected, and the early books used ``probability'' for the numerical
degree of evidential support or rational credibility, that is,
probability\(_1\); the frequency sense is about a century old in 1950.
Carnap gives two routes by which the new sense could arise without
being noticed. In the first, the evidence (1) states that the relative
frequency of myopia among the inhabitants of Chicago is \(1/5\), and
the hypothesis (2) is that John Doe, defined as inhabitant number 117,
is myopic. The complete statement (3) is that the probability of (2)
on (1) is \(1/5\), which the classical theory and T94-1e agree with
(\cref{thm:p15-carnap-direct}). Writers before Keynes usually omitted
the evidence and wrote (4), ``the probability that John Doe is myopic
is \(1/5\)'', or the conditional forms (5)--(7), the general form (6)
being the customary shape of a classical theorem. Taking (6), hence
(7), as analytic and its converse as true, a writer could regard (1)
and (4) as equivalent, and then express the frequency fact (1) by
(8), ``the probability of an inhabitant of Chicago being myopic is
\(1/5\)''; ``probability'' had become a name for relative frequency in
the whole population. In the second route, the evidence describes an
observed sample and (9) states that the probability\(_1\) of an
unobserved member's having \(M\) is \(1/3\); by 41D (8) this is
equivalent to (10), that the estimate of the relative frequency of
\(M\) in any unobserved class is \(1/3\). The phrase ``we may expect
to find one third'' is right if it states an estimate and wrong if it
states a prediction; slipping from estimate to predicted value moves
``probability'' to probability\(_2\)
\cite[sec.~42A, pp.~182--185]{Carnap1950Probability}.

Carnap reads Leslie Ellis (a paper read in 1842 and published in 1844,
which Keynes dates 1843) as the first such blurred step: Ellis's
statements that the event will tend to recur in proportion to its
probability, and that he cannot separate a judgement of greater
likelihood from a belief in greater long-run frequency, can be read as
an estimate, as a loose form of Bernoulli's theorem, or as a plain
frequency prediction. Cournot (1843) combined the classical definition
with a frequency interpretation; Boole (1854) is not counted a
frequentist, his basic concept being probability\(_1\); Venn (1866) was
the first to take the frequency concept systematically as explicandum
and the limit of relative frequency as explicatum, followed by Peirce
(1878), and the systematic frequency theories came with Reichenbach,
von Mises and Fisher. Carnap's criticism is that these authors treat
their departures from Bayes and Laplace as improved explicata of one
explicandum, whereas the explicandum itself had changed; it is directed
at this historical misdescription, not at frequency theories, whose
importance for statistics he affirms
\cite[sec.~42A, pp.~185--187]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\emph{Interpreting given probability statements (42B; philosophical).}
A probability\(_1\) statement on evidence that reports a frequency
is often said to be ``derived from'' it; the frequency is in fact
part of the evidence argument, and the value is equal or close to the
reported frequency. The traditional probability \emph{a posteriori},
with evidence reporting an observed frequency, and \emph{a priori},
with weak or tautological evidence and the principle of indifference,
are both probability\(_1\), as Bernoulli's own introduction of the
terms shows; the growing distrust of indifference, which shifted
emphasis to the a posteriori case, may have prepared the frequency
view \cite[sec.~42B, pp.~187--189]{Carnap1950Probability}.

Carnap's test case is a loaded die. \(X\) asserts (13) that the
probability of an ace is \(0.15\), and on being asked for his basis
reports (14), \(150\) aces in \(1{,}000\) throws and no other known
results, and (15), ``since there were \(150\) aces among \(1{,}000\)
throws, the probability is \(0.15\)''. Statement (15) can mean
probability\(_2\), or (16): the probability\(_1\) on (14) that a
future throw is an ace is \(0.15\). Asked about (17), that the
long-run frequency of aces will be \(0.15\), \(X\) answers (18) that
certainty is impossible but a frequency of about \(0.15\) is sensible to
expect. Carnap argues that (18) is an inductive statement and can be read
as (19), a high probability\(_1\) on (14) that the long-run frequency
lies in an interval about \(0.15\), or (20), that the estimate of that
frequency on (14) is \(0.15\); (20) is equivalent to (16) by 41D (8).
So frequency-based evidence does not show that a statement is about
probability\(_2\) \cite[sec.~42B, pp.~189--191]{Carnap1950Probability}.

The same analysis applies to a physicist's statement on molecular
velocities or on emissions of \(\alpha\)-particles in an hour, and to an
actuary's death rate. Each can be explicated as (i) the long-run
relative frequency is \(q\), or (ii) the probability\(_1\) of a single
instance on the statistical evidence is \(q\). Both carry the same
number and the same practical consequences, since (ii) says that the
estimate of the frequency is \(q\). They differ when later
observations deviate markedly from \(q\): statement (i), complete and
factual, is rejected as probably false; statement (ii), elliptical and
analytic, remains valid but becomes practically irrelevant and is
replaced by a new analytic statement on the enlarged evidence
\cite[sec.~42B, pp.~191--192]{Carnap1950Probability}. In the system of
this book that replacement is computed: under \(c^*\) with a division
of widths \(w,\kappa-w\), the value \((s_1+w)/(s+\kappa)\) on the first
\(1{,}000\) throws becomes \((s_1+t_1+w)/(s+t+\kappa)\) after \(t\)
further throws with \(t_1\) aces (\cref{thm:p15-carnap-predictive}),
and no earlier statement is withdrawn.

\section{The binomial law and Bernoulli's theorem}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-bernoulli}

Here, as in T94-1, a division \(M_1,\ldots,M_p\) is given; \(e\)
states that a population of \(n\) named individuals has \(n_i\)
members of \(M_i\), so \(r_i=n_i/n\); \(h\) is an individual
distribution assigning categories to \(s\) specified members, with
counts \(s_i\leq n_i\); and \(h_{st}\) is the corresponding statistical
distribution. The function \(c\) is any symmetric \(c\)-function, and
the exact value of \(c(h_{st},e)\) is the hypergeometric value of
\cref{thm:p15-carnap-direct}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-binomial}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-binomial}{T95-1: the binomial law and its two senses}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[(A)] For every population,
       \[
        \prod_i\Bigl(1-\frac{s_i-1}{n_i}\Bigr)^{s_i}
        \leq\frac{c(h,e)}{\prod_i r_i^{s_i}}
        \leq\Bigl(1-\frac{s-1}{n}\Bigr)^{-s},
       \]
       (factors with \(s_i=0\) read as one, and \(n\geq s\)). Hence
       \(c(h,e)\) is approximately \(\prod_ir_i^{s_i}\) when \(n\) is
       large relative to \(s\) and each \(n_i\) relative to \(s_i\).
       In the two-category case the law of the sample count differs from
       the binomial law by at most \(s(s-1)/(2n)\) in total variation.
 \item[(B)] If \(n\to\infty\) with \(n_i/n\to r_i\) for every
       \(i\) and \(s,s_i\) fixed, then
       \(c(h,e)\to r_1^{s_1}\cdots r_p^{s_p}\) (T95-1a),
       \(c(h_{st},e)\to\frac{s!}{s_1!\cdots s_p!}r_1^{s_1}\cdots r_p^{s_p}\)
       (T95-1b), and for \(p=2\) this is the binomial law
       \(\binom s{s_1}r_1^{s_1}r_2^{s-s_1}\) (T95-1c).
 \item[(d)] For \(p=2\) and \(0<r_1<1\), put \(q=(s+1)r_1\). The
       binomial values have a unique maximum at \(s_1=\lfloor q\rfloor\)
       when \(q\) is not an integer, and two equal maxima at \(q-1\)
       and \(q\) when it is. In particular the maximum is at \(sr_1\)
       when \(sr_1\) is an integer (T95-1d).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-bernoulli}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-bernoulli}{T96-1: Bernoulli's theorem under its restricting conditions}
Let \(p=2\), \(M=M_1\), \(0<r_1<1\), \(r_2=1-r_1\), and
\(\sigma=\sqrt{sr_1r_2}\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[(e)] (Limit theorem, exact form.) For every symmetric \(c\),
       every finite population with \(n\geq s\), and every \(g>0\),
       \[
        c\Bigl(\Bigl|\frac{s_1}s-r_1\Bigr|\leq g,\;e\Bigr)
        \geq1-\frac{r_1r_2}{sg^2}\cdot\frac{n-s}{n-1}
        \geq1-\frac1{4sg^2}.
       \]
       Thus for given \(g,\varepsilon>0\) there is \(s'\) such that the
       value exceeds \(1-\varepsilon\) for every \(s\geq s'\) and every
       population size, which is T96-1e.
 \item[(a)--(d)] (Normal law.) For the binomial values \(B_s\) of
       \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-binomial}(B), as \(s\to\infty\) with
       \(r_1\) fixed, \(\sigma B_s(s_1)-\phi(\delta/\sigma)\to0\)
       uniformly for \(|\delta|\leq A\sigma\), where
       \(\delta=s_1-sr_1\) and \(0<A<\infty\). For fixed
       \(u_1<u_2\) and \(\delta_l=u_l\sigma\), the \(B_s\)-value of
       \(\{\delta_1\leq\delta\leq\delta_2\}\) tends to
       \(\Phi(u_2)-\Phi(u_1)\), and so does the value of the interval
       widened by \(\tfrac12\) at each end. Hence the symmetric-interval
       value tends to \(2\Phi(u)-1\) and the outside-interval value to
       \(2(1-\Phi(u))\). Along populations with \(n_1/n=r_1\), the
       same interval limits hold for the exact finite-population values
       when \(s^2/n\to0\), and the local limit when
       \(s^{5/2}/n\to0\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Carnap's statements (a)--(d) are approximations: they hold, in his
words, when \(s\) and even \(sr_1r_2\) are sufficiently large and the
population is very large relative to the sample. He gives no proof and
refers to textbooks \cite[sec.~96, pp.~503--505]{Carnap1950Probability}.
The limits above are the exact content that such approximations can
have; Carnap's ranking of the forms (1), (2), (3) by accuracy is a
numerical observation, not a theorem with an error bound, and it is not
asserted here. His statement (e) holds for a fixed \(r_1\); the bound
proved above is uniform in the population and needs no normal
approximation.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-chicago}{The source's Chicago example}
Carnap takes \(s=2400\), \(n=3{,}000{,}000\), \(r_1=0.6\), so
\(sr_1r_2=576\), \(\sigma=24\), and \(sr_1=1440\). For the interval
\(1440\pm24\), (c)(1) gives \(u=24.5/24\) and about \(0.692\); the
rougher form gives \(u=1\) and about \(0.682\)
\cite[sec.~96, pp.~506--507]{Carnap1950Probability}. Here
\(s(s-1)/(2n)\) is about \(0.96\), so the total-variation bound of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-binomial}(A) does not by itself certify the
transfer of the binomial value to this finite population, while the
exact bound of (e) gives only about \(0.0008\) for \(g=0.01\), the
relative width of that interval: it is uninformative there. Both comparisons show how much the stated
conditions carry. For \(g=0.05\) the exact bound gives at least
\(0.96\).
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-binomial-misuse}{Restricting conditions}
The binomial law in T95-1c presupposes evidence stating the population
frequency \(r_1\). Carnap rejects the traditional use that replaces it
by the frequency observed in an earlier sample, for example
\(\binom{100}{20}(0.12)^{20}(0.88)^{80}\) for twenty aces in the next
hundred throws after twenty-four aces in two hundred: that inference
is predictive, not direct, and the binomial form holds there only
approximately under stronger conditions. Sampling with replacement is,
strictly, sampling from a series of populations with the same counts.
For an infinite population the law holds exactly when \(e\) states the
limit of the frequency, but such evidence can hardly be the observational
evidence actually available \cite[sec.~95, pp.~499--502]{Carnap1950Probability}.
For \(c^*\), the predictive value is given by
\cref{thm:p15-carnap-predictive}, not by the binomial formula.
\end{hbwarning}

\section{The variety of instances}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-variety}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-variety}{The 110I requirement}
Two investigators share prior knowledge and each tests a law \(l\) on
one hundred specimens with all results positive; the first uses
specimens of one kind, the second of various kinds. Let \(e_1,e_2\) be
their total evidence. The requirement is that an adequate explicatum
\(c\) give \(l\), or the prediction \(h\) of a further instance, a
higher value on \(e_2\) than on \(e_1\)
\cite[sec.~110I, p.~575]{Carnap1950Probability}.
\end{hbdefinition}

Carnap asserts that \(c^*\) satisfies this requirement and refers for
the demonstration to his 1945 paper \emph{On Inductive Logic}, sec.~15;
the 1950 book contains no proof. The requirement is stated informally:
it does not say which sentences express ``kinds'' or which further
instance is meant. The following exact calculation shows how the answer
depends on that choice for \(c^*\); it is an independent illustration,
not a reconstruction of the 1945 argument.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-variety}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-variety}{Variety under \(c^*\) in a two-predicate language}
Let the primitive predicates be \(K\) (a kind) and \(W\), so
\(\kappa=4\), and let \(l=\forall x\,W(x)\). Let \(e_1\) report one
hundred individuals all \(K\land W\), and \(e_2\) report fifty
\(K\land W\) and fifty \(\neg K\land W\). For a new individual \(a\),
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \(c^*_N(l,e_1)=c^*_N(l,e_2)\) for every \(N\geq100\), and
       \(c^*(W(a),e_1)=c^*(W(a),e_2)=51/52\);
 \item \(c^*(W(a),e_1\land\neg K(a))=1/2\), while
       \(c^*(W(a),e_2\land\neg K(a))=51/52\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

So under \(c^*\) the variety of the sample leaves the law and the
unqualified next instance unchanged, and raises the prediction for an
instance of the kind that the uniform sample omitted. Whether this is
what the requirement demands depends on its intended formalization.

\section{The law, its restricted form, and its instances}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-laws}

Section~110F treats the inference from a sample to a law
\(l=\forall x(M(x)\supset M'(x))\), with \(M,M'\) factual molecular
predicates of a monadic system with \(\kappa\) \(Q\)-properties. Put
\(M_1\defeq M\land\neg M'\), of width \(w_1\) with \(0<w_1<\kappa\),
so that \(l\) is L-equivalent to \(\forall x\,\neg M_1(x)\). Let \(e\)
report \(s\geq1\) named individuals, all \(\neg M_1\). Carnap's
formula (10) is the first value of \cref{thm:p15-carnap-universal},
which in his notation reads
\[
 c^*_N(l,e)=\binom{s+\kappa-1}{w_1}\Big/\binom{N+\kappa-1}{w_1}
 \qquad(N\geq s),
\]
and (12), \(c^*(l,e)=0\) in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), is
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-monadic-limit}(3). For
\(\pi=1\) the value is \((s+1)/(N+1)\); Carnap notes that some
authors give this value as holding generally, and argues that a
stronger law should receive less confirmation, so that the value must
depend on \(w_1\) \cite[sec.~110F, pp.~570--571]{Carnap1950Probability}.
His formula (11) is the approximation
\(c^*(l,e)\cong(s/N)^{w_1}\) for \(s\) very large in relation to
\(\kappa\) (p.~571). The next result states exactly when it holds.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-law-approx}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-law-approx}{Formula (11): the large-sample form of the law's confirmation}
Under the hypotheses just stated, for every \(N\geq s\),
\[
 \Bigl(\frac sN\Bigr)^{w_1}\leq c^*_N(l,e)
 \leq\Bigl(\frac sN\Bigr)^{w_1}\exp\Bigl(\frac{w_1(\kappa-1)}s\Bigr).
\]
The ratio \(\rho_N=c^*_N(l,e)/(s/N)^{w_1}\) increases with \(N\), and
its supremum over \(N\geq s\) is
\(\prod_{j=0}^{w_1-1}\bigl(1+(\kappa-1-j)/s\bigr)\), which is at least
\(1+w_1(\kappa-1)/(2s)\). Hence
\(\sup_{N\geq s}\lvert\rho_N-1\rvert\) tends to zero along a sequence
of cases if and only if \(w_1(\kappa-1)/s\) tends to zero; this holds
in particular when \(s/\kappa^2\to\infty\), and, for bounded
\(w_1\), when \(s/\kappa\to\infty\).
\end{hbtheorem}

So formula (11) is a statement about ratios, uniform in the population
size. It cannot be read as an absolute-error statement that would be
informative for small values: both sides tend to zero as
\(N\to\infty\). With \(\pi=1\) the bounds read
\(s/N\leq(s+1)/(N+1)\leq(s/N)e^{1/s}\).

Formula (13) concerns evidence \(e'\) that reports \(s\) individuals,
\(s_1\) of them \(M_1\) (negative instances) and \(s-s_1\) of them
\(\neg M_1\). The hypothesis is then the restricted law \(l'\): every
individual of \(\mathfrak L_N\) outside the sample is \(\neg M_1\).
Carnap prints (13) with the sign \(\cong\); the following result shows
that it is an exact identity.

\begin{hbcorollary}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-restricted-law}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-restricted-law}{Formula (13): the restricted law}
Let \(M_1\) have width \(w_1\), \(0<w_1<\kappa\), let \(e'\) report
\(s\) individuals of which \(s_1\) are \(M_1\), and let \(l'\) say
that every individual of \(\mathfrak L_N\) outside the sample is
\(\neg M_1\). Then, for \(N\geq s\),
\[
 c^*_N(l',e')=
 \binom{s-s_1+N-s+\kappa-w_1-1}{N-s}\Big/\binom{N+\kappa-1}{N-s}
 =\binom{s+\kappa-1}{s_1+w_1}\Big/\binom{N+\kappa-1}{s_1+w_1},
\]
which decreases strictly in \(s_1\) for fixed \(s,N\) with
\(N>s\), and for fixed \(s,s_1\) tends to zero as \(N\to\infty\).
\end{hbcorollary}

For \(s_1=0\) the restricted law is the law \(l\) itself and the
formula is (10). Carnap adds without proof that, under ordinary
circumstances with large \(N\), the value of \(c^*\) rises moderately
when a new individual is observed that satisfies \(l\), and falls to a
small fraction of its previous value when the new individual violates
\(l\) \cite[sec.~110F, p.~571]{Carnap1950Probability}. The hypothesis
after the new observation is the restricted law for the enlarged
sample.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-law-update}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-law-update}{A conforming and a violating new instance}
Let \(e',l'\) be as in \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-restricted-law},
with \(N>s\), and let \(a\) be an individual of \(\mathfrak L_N\) not
in the sample. Let \(l''\) say that every individual outside the
sample and other than \(a\) is \(\neg M_1\). Put \(k=s_1+w_1\). Then
\[
 \frac{c^*_N(l'',e'\land\neg M_1(a))}{c^*_N(l',e')}
 =\frac{s+\kappa}{s+\kappa-k},\qquad
 \frac{c^*_N(l'',e'\land M_1(a))}{c^*_N(l',e')}
 =\frac{s+\kappa}{N+\kappa-1-k}.
\]
The first factor exceeds one, does not depend on \(N\), and equals
\(1/c^*(\neg M_1(a),e')\); it tends to one as \(s\to\infty\) with
\(s_1\) bounded. The second factor is less than one exactly when
\(N>s+k+1\), and for fixed \(s,s_1\) tends to zero as
\(N\to\infty\).
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-swans}{Swans in a population of one million}
Take Swan and White as the primitive predicates, so \(\kappa=4\) and
non-white swan has width \(w_1=1\). After \(s=100\) individuals with
no non-white swan (\(s_1=0\), \(k=1\)) in a population of
\(N=10^6\), a further conforming individual multiplies \(c^*\) of the
restricted law by \(104/103\), about \(1.0097\); a non-white swan
multiplies it by \(104/(10^6+2)\), about \(1.04\times10^{-4}\).
Formula (11) gives \(10^{-4}\) for \(c^*(l,e)\); the exact value is
\(103/(10^6+3)\), and the ratio lies within the bounds of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-law-approx}.
\end{hbexample}

Section~110G replaces the law by its instances. For a simple law
\(l\) and nongeneral non-L-false \(e\), the \emph{instance confirmation}
\(c^*_i(l,e)\) is \(c^*(h,e)\), where \(h\) is an instance of \(l\)
for an individual not occurring in \(e\) (definition (14)); for
\(l=\forall x(M(x)\supset M'(x))\) the \emph{qualified-instance
confirmation} \(c^*_{qi}(M,M',e)\) is \(c^*(h',e\land j)\), where \(j\)
says that a new individual is \(M\) and \(h'\) that it is \(M'\)
(definition (15)). Let \(M_2\defeq M\land M'\), of width \(w_2\), and
let \(e\) report \(s\) individuals, \(s_1\) of them \(M_1\), \(s_2\) of
them \(M_2\), and the rest \(\neg M\). Carnap's formulas are
\[
 c^*_i(l,e)=1-\frac{s_1+w_1}{s+\kappa}\quad(16),\qquad
 c^*_{qi}(M,M',e)=1-\frac{s_1+w_1}{s_1+w_1+s_2+w_2}\quad(17),
\]
which are the two values of \cref{thm:p15-carnap-universal}; they do
not depend on \(N\) and so hold in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) as limits of
constant sequences \cite[sec.~110G, pp.~572--573]{Carnap1950Probability}.
Carnap then asserts that if \(s_1\) is zero or a fixed small number,
both values grow close to one as the sample size \(s\) increases.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-instance-limits}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-instance-limits}{When instance confirmation tends to one}
Let \(s_1\leq S\) for a fixed bound \(S\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \(1-c^*_i(l,e)\leq(S+w_1)/(s+\kappa)\), so \(c^*_i(l,e)\to1\)
       as \(s\to\infty\), uniformly in \(N\) and in \(s_2\).
 \item \(c^*_{qi}(M,M',e)\to1\) if and only if \(s_2\to\infty\).
       If \(s\to\infty\) while \(s_1\) and \(s_2\) stay fixed (every
       further individual is \(\neg M\)), \(c^*_{qi}\) stays equal to
       \((s_2+w_2)/(s_1+w_1+s_2+w_2)<1\).
\end{enumerate}
More generally, without the bound \(S\) and with \(\kappa,w_1,w_2\)
fixed, \(c^*_i\to1\) if and only if \(s\to\infty\) and \(s_1/s\to0\), and \(c^*_{qi}\to1\) if and only if
\((s_1+w_1)/(s_2+w_2)\to0\).
\end{hbtheorem}

Carnap's claim is therefore exact for the instance confirmation. For the
qualified-instance confirmation it holds when the growth of the sample
is growth in the number \(s_2\) of positive instances \(M\land M'\); a
growing sample of non-swans leaves the qualified value of
``all swans are white'' fixed, at \(1/2\) for \(s_1=s_2=0\) and
\(w_1=w_2=1\). On these values Carnap rests his reading of the
scientist's ``well-confirmed law'' as a high instance confirmation,
consistent with \(c^*(l,e)\) being small in a large system and zero in
the infinite one (p.~573).

\section{Regular functions: null confirmation, fitting sequences, almost L-true sentences}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular}

Sections~57--58 of the 1950 book collect the laws of regular
\(m\)-functions, introduce the confirmation on tautological evidence,
state when the functions for different finite systems fit together, and
name the sentences of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) on which a limit function
behaves like an L-true or L-false sentence without being one
\cite[secs.~57--58, pp.~305--315]{Carnap1950Probability}. The theorems
T57-1 on single regular \(m\)-functions (bounds, monotonicity under
L-implication, inclusion--exclusion, additivity over L-exclusive
sentences) are the finite-measure laws behind
\cref{thm:p15-carnap-regular-laws}. Here \(t\) is the L-true
sentence, and an \(m\)-function \(m_N\) and the limit \(m_\infty\) are
as in \cref{def:p15-carnap-regular-confirmation,def:p15-w02b-p15-029-limits}.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-null}{Null confirmation (D57-1)}
For a regular \(c\)-function of a finite or the infinite system and a
sentence \(j\) of that system, the \emph{null} (or \emph{initial})
confirmation is \(c_0(j)\defeq c(j,t)\).
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-fitting}{Fitting sequences (D57-3, D57-4, D57-5)}
A sequence \(m_1,m_2,\ldots\) is a \emph{fitting \(m\)-sequence for
state descriptions} if (a) each \(m_N\) is a regular \(m\)-function
for the state descriptions of \(\mathfrak L_N\), and (b) for every
\(N\) and every state description \(z\) of \(\mathfrak L_N\),
\(m_N(z)\) is the sum of the values \(m_{N+1}(z')\) over the state
descriptions \(z'\) of \(\mathfrak L_{N+1}\) of which \(z\) is a
subconjunction. A \emph{fitting \(m\)-sequence for sentences} extends
such a sequence to sentences by summation over ranges, and a
\emph{fitting \(c\)-sequence} consists of the regular \(c\)-functions
based on a fitting \(m\)-sequence for sentences.
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-null-fitting}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-null-fitting}{Null confirmation and fitting (T57-3, T57-5, T57-6)}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item For a regular \(m_N\) and its \(c_N\), \(c_{N,0}(j)=m_N(j)\)
       for every sentence \(j\) of \(\mathfrak L_N\). For a regular
       sequence, \(c_{\infty,0}(j)\) exists exactly when
       \(m_\infty(j)\) exists, and then the two are equal.
 \item Let \((m_N)\) be a fitting \(m\)-sequence for sentences and
       \(j\) a nongeneral sentence occurring in \(\mathfrak L_N\).
       Then \(m_N(j)=m_{N+n}(j)\) for every \(n\geq0\), and
       \(m_\infty(j)=m_N(j)\).
 \item For a fitting \(c\)-sequence and nongeneral \(h,e\) occurring
       in \(\mathfrak L_N\), with \(e\) not L-false in
       \(\mathfrak L_N\): \(e\) is not L-false in any
       \(\mathfrak L_{N+n}\) or in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), and
       \(c_\infty(h,e)=c_{N+n}(h,e)=c_N(h,e)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

The sequence for \(m^*\) is fitting (\cref{thm:p15-carnap-star-construction}).
Carnap defends null confirmation against the objection that nothing can
be said about a hypothesis before any factual knowledge: a statement
\(c(h,e)=r\) is, if true, L-true, so \(c_0(h)=r\) states a logical
property of \(h\) fixed by its range. He distinguishes three senses of
``probability a priori''---confirmation on L-true evidence, values
computed mainly by the principle of indifference, and the value before a
planned observation---and accepts only the first; for the third he uses
``prior confirmation'' (sec.~60). There is no frequency analogue of
\(c_0\), since a relative frequency has no value in the null class
\cite[sec.~57B, pp.~308--309]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular-extremes}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular-extremes}{Extreme values and L-concepts (T58-1)}
The following hold (i) for any regular \(m\), \(m'\) of
\(\mathfrak L_N\) and any sentences \(i,j\) of \(\mathfrak L_N\); and
(ii) for the limit functions \(m,m'\) of any two fitting
\(m\)-sequences and any nongeneral sentences \(i,j\) of
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[(a,b)] \(m(i)=0\) if and only if \(i\) is L-false.
 \item[(c,d)] \(m(i)=1\) if and only if \(i\) is L-true.
 \item[(e,f)] \(0<m(i)<1\) if and only if \(i\) is factual.
 \item[(g)] If \(i\) L-implies \(j\) but not conversely, \(m(i)<m(j)\).
 \item[(h)--(k)] \(m(i)=0\) iff \(m'(i)=0\), and \(m(i)=1\) iff
       \(m'(i)=1\).
 \item[(l)] (Case (i).) If \(i\) is factual and \(0<r<1\), some regular
       \(m''\) of \(\mathfrak L_N\) has \(m''(i)=r\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-almost}{Almost L-true sentences (D58-1)}
Let \(m\) be a limit \(m\)-function for \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) and
\(i,j\) sentences of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\). (a) \(i\) is \emph{almost
L-true} if \(i\) is not L-true but \(m(i)=1\); (b) \emph{almost
L-false} if \(\neg i\) is almost L-true; (c) \(i\) \emph{almost
L-implies} \(j\) if \(i\supset j\) is almost L-true; (d) \(i\) is
\emph{almost L-equivalent} to \(j\) if \(i\equiv j\) is almost L-true.
All four are relative to \(m\).
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-almost}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-almost}{Almost L-true sentences and inductive replacement (T58-3, T58-4, T58-5)}
Let \(m\) be the limit function of a fitting \(m\)-sequence.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \(i\) is almost L-false iff \(i\) is not L-false and
       \(m(i)=0\). An almost L-true or almost L-false sentence is
       general and factual. If \(i\) is almost L-true and L-implies
       \(j\), then \(m(j)=1\), so \(j\) is L-true or almost L-true.
 \item For \(m^*_\infty\) in a monadic system, \(\exists x\,Q_1(x)\)
       and \(\neg\forall x\,M(x)\), for \(M\) of width \(0<w<\kappa\),
       are almost L-true.
 \item (Replacement.) Let \(i'\) come from \(i\) by replacing some
       occurrences of a subsentence \(j\) by \(j'\), in a finite system
       or in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) where the values below exist. If
       \(m(j)=m(j')=0\), or if \(m(j)=m(j')=1\), then
       \(m(i)=m(i')\). Equal intermediate values do not suffice.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Carnap explains the term by measure theory: the values of \(m\) on the
ranges of sentences form an additive measure on the state descriptions
of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), and an almost L-true sentence holds in all
of them except a class of measure zero, which is in general infinite;
the usage is not the ``all but finitely many'' sense of the phrase
\cite[sec.~58, p.~313]{Carnap1950Probability}. The restriction of
(ii) to nongeneral sentences in \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular-extremes}
is necessary: the sentences in item~(2) above violate (c,d).

\section{Theorems on regular confirmation functions and Bayes's theorem}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-bayes}

The theorems of secs.~59--60 hold for every regular \(c\)-function of
every finite system and for every limit function \(c_\infty\) of a
regular sequence, under three conditions: (A) in \(\mathfrak L_N\), all
sentences occur in \(\mathfrak L_N\) and no evidence is L-false there;
(B) in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), every \(c\)-expression that occurs has
a value; (C) every \(c\)-expression in a denominator is positive. The
one exception is T59-5, restricted as stated below
\cite[sec.~59, pp.~315--316]{Carnap1950Probability}. The addition,
complement, equivalence and multiplication laws of T59-1 are those of
\cref{thm:p15-carnap-regular-laws}. Carnap's point is that the class of
regular functions is very wide: by T59-5f a regular \(c\) can give a
hypothesis not L-dependent on the evidence any value strictly between
zero and one, so a theory containing only the theorems valid for all
regular functions, as he says of the axiom systems without a principle
of indifference, is correct but very weak (p.~323). The narrowing comes
in two steps: symmetry (secs.~90--92,
\cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}) and then \(c^*\) (sec.~110A).
In \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), a hypothesis that a sentence is L-false,
or that one sentence L-implies another, is used through
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-ltruth-transfer}, which in a relational
system assumes the Axiom of Choice; this is the step for which Carnap
cites T20-11.

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-limit-laws}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-limit-laws}{Passage of the laws to \(\mathfrak L_\infty\)}
Let \(c_\infty\) be the limit function of a regular sequence.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item If an equation or non-strict inequality between polynomial
       expressions in finitely many values
       \(c_N(x_1,y),\ldots,c_N(x_r,y)\) with one evidence \(y\) holds in every \(\mathfrak L_N\) in which \(y\) is not
       L-false, it holds for \(c_\infty\) whenever these limits exist.
 \item \(c_\infty(h\land i,e)=c_\infty(i,e)\,c_\infty(h,e\land i)\)
       whenever the three values exist.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hblemma}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular-c}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular-c}{Mixtures, chains, and extreme values (T59-2q, T59-3d, T59-4, T59-5, T59-6)}
Under (A)--(C):
\begin{enumerate}
 \item (T59-2q.) If \(e_1\land e_2\) is L-false, \(c(h,e_1\lor e_2)\)
       lies between \(c(h,e_1)\) and \(c(h,e_2)\) (in
       \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), provided \(c(e_1,e_1\lor e_2)\) exists).
       (T59-3d.) If \(j_1,\ldots,j_n\) are L-exclusive in pairs and
       \(j=j_1\lor\cdots\lor j_n\), \(c(h,j)\) lies between the least
       and the greatest of the \(c(h,j_p)\) (in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\),
       provided the values \(c(j_p,j)\) exist).
 \item (T59-4.) \(c(h_1\land\cdots\land h_n,e)
       =\prod_{p=1}^nc(h_p,e\land h_1\land\cdots\land h_{p-1})\), and
       \(c(h_n,e)\) is at least this product.
 \item (T59-5.) In \(\mathfrak L_N\) for any sentences, and in
       \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) for nongeneral sentences and a fitting
       sequence, with \(e\) not L-false: (a, b) \(c(h,e)=1\) iff
       \(e\) L-implies \(h\); (c, d) \(c(h,e)=0\) iff \(e\land h\) is
       L-false; (e) \(c(h,e)=0\) iff \(c(e,h)=0\), when \(h\) is not
       L-false; if \(c(h,e)=1\) and \(\neg h\) is not L-false, then
       \(c(\neg e,\neg h)=1\) and \(c(e,\neg h)=0\); (f) in
       \(\mathfrak L_N\), if neither \(h\) nor \(\neg h\) is
       L-implied by \(e\), every \(r\in(0,1)\) is \(c(h,e)\) for some
       regular \(c\).
 \item (T59-6a--g.) If \(c(h,e)=0\), then \(c(h\land i,e)=0\), and
       \(c(h,e\land i)=0\) when \(c(i,e)>0\). If \(c(h,e)=1\) and
       \(c(i,e)>0\), \(c(h,e\land i)=1\). If \(c(h,e)=1\),
       \(c(h\land i,e)=c(i,e)\). If \(c(h_1\supset h_2,e)=1\),
       \(c(h_1,e)\leq c(h_2,e)\); if \(c(h_1\equiv h_2,e)=1\), the two
       values are equal.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-two-exceptions}{Value one without entailment}
Carnap's example for the restriction in T59-5: \(e\) says that all
individuals except two have \(M\), and \(h\) is \(M(b)\) for an
individual \(b\) \cite[sec.~59, p.~322]{Carnap1950Probability}. For
every regular sequence symmetric in individuals and every
\(N\geq3\) in which \(b\) occurs, \(c_N(h,e)=1-2/N\) (annex proof of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-regular-c}), so \(c_\infty(h,e)=1\),
although \(e\) does not L-imply \(h\): \(b\) may be an exception. Thus
probability\(_1\) one in an infinite domain is not entailment, and
probability\(_1\) zero is not incompatibility.
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-chain}{High confirmation is not transitive}
Carnap's example on p.~320: an \(m\) with
\(m(e\land h_1\land h_2)=0\),
\(m(e\land h_1\land\neg h_2)=0.00004995\),
\(m(e\land\neg h_1\land h_2)=0\),
\(m(e\land\neg h_1\land\neg h_2)=0.00000005\),
\(m(\neg e\land h_1\land h_2)=0.0999\),
\(m(\neg e\land h_1\land\neg h_2)=0.00005005\), the remaining mass on
\(\neg e\land\neg h_1\). Then \(c(h_1,e)=0.999\) and
\(c(h_2,h_1)=0.999\), but \(c(h_2,e)=0\). A chain of inference is
valid in the cumulative form of item~(2): if
\(c(h_1,e)\), \(c(h_2,e\land h_1)\), \ldots{} are all high, the last
hypothesis keeps at least their product on \(e\).
\end{hbwarning}

Section~60 names the parts of the division theorem. With \(e\) the
present evidence, \(h\) a hypothesis and \(i\) a prediction of a new
observation, \(c(h,e)\) is the \emph{prior confirmation},
\(c(h,e\land i)\) the \emph{posterior confirmation}, \(c(i,e\land h)\)
the \emph{likelihood} of \(i\), and \(c(i,e)\) its
\emph{expectedness} \cite[sec.~60, pp.~326--327]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-bayes}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-bayes}{The division theorem and Bayes's theorem (T60-1, T60-2, T60-3, T60-5, T60-6)}
Under (A)--(C):
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[T60-1.] (a) \(c(i,e\land h)=c(h\land i,e)/c(h,e)\);
       (b) \(c(h,e)\,c(i,e\land h)=c(i,e)\,c(h,e\land i)\);
       (c) \(c(h,e\land i)=c(h,e)\,c(i,e\land h)/c(i,e)\)
       (general division theorem);
       (d) \(c(h,e\land i)/c(h,e)=c(i,e\land h)/c(i,e)\);
       (e) \(c(i,e\land h)=c(h\land i,e)/\bigl(c(h\land i,e)+c(h\land\neg i,e)\bigr)\).
 \item[T60-2.] \(\dfrac{c(h_1,e\land i)}{c(h_2,e\land i)}
       =\dfrac{c(h_1,e)\,c(i,e\land h_1)}{c(h_2,e)\,c(i,e\land h_2)}\).
 \item[T60-3.] \(\dfrac{c(h,e\land i_1)}{c(h,e\land i_2)}
       =\dfrac{c(i_1,e\land h)}{c(i_2,e\land h)}\cdot\dfrac{c(i_2,e)}{c(i_1,e)}\).
 \item[T60-5.] If \(c(h_1,e\land i)+c(h_2,e\land i)=1\), then
       \(c(h_1,e\land i)=\dfrac{c(h_1,e)\,c(i,e\land h_1)}
       {c(h_1,e)\,c(i,e\land h_1)+c(h_2,e)\,c(i,e\land h_2)}\), and if
       moreover \(c(h_1,e)=c(h_2,e)\) the prior factors cancel.
 \item[T60-6.] (Bayes's theorem.) Let \(c(i,e)>0\), let \(e\land i\)
       L-imply \(h_1\lor\cdots\lor h_n\) (\(n\geq2\)), and let
       \(e\land i\land h_1,\ldots,e\land i\land h_n\) be L-exclusive in
       pairs. For each \(h=h_p\),
       \[
        c(h,e\land i)=\frac{c(i\land h,e)}{\sum_{p=1}^nc(i\land h_p,e)}
        =\frac{c(h,e)\,c(i,e\land h)}{\sum_{p=1}^nc(h_p,e)\,c(i,e\land h_p)},
       \]
       and if all \(c(h_p,e)\) are equal,
       \(c(h,e\land i)=c(i,e\land h)/\sum_pc(i,e\land h_p)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-diagnosis}{Two diagnoses}
In Carnap's diagnostic example, two diseases \(D_1,D_2\) have about
equal prior confirmation, and a test result \(i\) is five times as
likely under \(D_1\) as under \(D_2\); by T60-2 the posterior
confirmation of \(D_1\) is about five times that of \(D_2\) (p.~329).
A numerical case with a third alternative: with \(c(h_1,e)=c(h_2,e)=0.4\), \(c(h_3,e)=0.2\) for a third
exclusive alternative, likelihoods \(0.5,0.1,0.05\), T60-6 gives
posteriors \(0.2/0.25=0.8\), \(0.04/0.25=0.16\) and \(0.01/0.25=0.04\).
\end{hbexample}

Carnap holds Bayes's theorem in the form T60-6 to be valid on the
assumptions common to practically all theories of probability\(_1\);
the objections he accepts are aimed at an obscure classical
formulation, which he traces to confusing probability\(_1\) with
probability\(_2\), and at uses of the principle of indifference. Its
limitation is the limitation of every theorem of the chapter: it
connects \(c\)-values and computes none. In the inverse inference from
a sample to a population, the likelihoods are values of direct inference
(solved by symmetry, \cref{thm:p15-carnap-direct}) and the priors over
population frequencies need the much stronger rule that leads to \(c^*\)
\cite[sec.~60, pp.~331--333]{Carnap1950Probability}. Bayes's own
theorem is, he says, a special case of T60-6b for the inverse inference,
deferred to the second volume.

\section{The problem of a quantitative inductive logic (secs.~46--51)}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-problem}

Sections~46--51 close chapter~IV of the 1950 book. They contain no
definition of \(c\). Their purpose is to answer two objections---that
the factors on which confirmation depends cannot be measured, and that
no single function can combine them---and to say how a quantitative
\(c\), once found, would be used in science and in practical decisions
\cite[secs.~46--51, pp.~219--279]{Carnap1950Probability}. The inputs
are the three explanations of probability\(_1\) of
\cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-interpretations} and the estimate of
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-betting}. The conclusions are of three
types, marked below: philosophical claims about the task and limits of
inductive logic, psychological or empirical claims about how people use
probability and value money, and mathematical results about the
decision rules, which are proved in the annex.

\subsection*{Secs.~46--47: is a quantitative inductive logic impossible?}

\emph{Sec.~46 (philosophical).} Carnap separates the logical factors
that bear on confirmation from methodological, technological and
subjective ones. Inductive logic is to represent only the former, in
the way deductive logic certifies that a conclusion is entailed without
guiding the search for it. Of the factors von~Kries named, four are
logical: (i) the extent of the confirming material, (ii) its variety,
(iii) its precision, and (iv) the extent and kind of the disconfirming
material. Carnap rejects von~Kries's first thesis, that these factors
cannot be given numbers in principle. Counting confirming cases needs a
definition of ``confirming case'', which Hempel's paradox shows to be
delicate: \((x)(Mx\supset M'x)\) and \((x)(\neg M'x\supset\neg Mx)\) are
L-equivalent, so any observation must confirm both or neither, yet a
non-white non-swan does not seem to confirm that all swans are white.
Carnap holds that an adequate definition can be found (Hempel's is
discussed in secs.~87--88 and in the second volume). Given one,
variety can be measured from the number of kinds and the number of
cases of each kind, together with dispersions for quantitative kinds,
and precision by the methods of the theory of errors. Precision does not
arise in the systems \(\mathfrak L_N\), which contain no quantitative
magnitudes \cite[sec.~46, pp.~219--226]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\emph{Sec.~47 (philosophical, with one mathematical example).} The
second thesis, that no function of these numbers is an adequate degree
of confirmation, is met by five difficulties A--E, each of which Carnap
describes as an excess of eligible functions rather than an absence
\cite[sec.~47, pp.~226--233]{Carnap1950Probability}.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[A.] \emph{Singular prediction.} With evidence that \(s_1\) of
       \(s\) observed individuals are \(M\), the straight rule
       \(c=s_1/s\) gives \(c=1\) when \(s_1=s\), which would license a
       bet at any odds; so \(c\) should be below \(r=s_1/s\) when
       \(r=1\) or near it. Laplace's rule of succession and the family
       of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-laplace-family} meet this, and
       Carnap reports that Laplace's rule applied generally leads to
       contradictions.
 \item[B.] \emph{Enumeration.} With \(n\) confirming and no
       disconfirming cases, \(c(h,e)\) should be a nondecreasing
       function of \(n\) whose relative increase per new case falls as
       \(n\) grows; infinitely many functions qualify.
 \item[C.] \emph{Partial cases.} Evidence such as \(Mb\land(M'b\lor Pb)\),
       together with a frequency of \(M'\) among \(P\)-things, should
       count for something between nothing and one confirming case.
 \item[D.] \emph{Disconfirming cases.} For the restricted law
       (D37-2b) on a sample with \(s_1\) violating and \(s_2\)
       conforming individuals, \(c\) should drop at the first
       violation and fall as \(s_1\) grows; how many conforming cases
       restore the earlier value is left open by the requirements.
 \item[E.] \emph{Variety of instances.} The methodological rule to
       vary the conditions not specified in a law must be justified by
       a comparative or quantitative theorem that spreading the test
       cases over more kinds yields higher confirmation; an adequate
       \(c\) must yield such a theorem without ad hoc stipulation.
       Carnap acknowledges Nagel's numerical examples as showing how
       hard this is and defers the test to sec.~110I; the requirement
       and what is proved about it here are in
       \cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-variety}.
 \item[F.] \emph{The task.} Impossibility would require proving the
       requirements logically incompatible. Carnap rejects a patchwork
       of separate numerical decisions in favour of two over-all
       decisions that determine \(c^*\) (sec.~110A,
       \cref{def:p15-carnap-star}), with adequacy judged by comparing
       computed values against intuitive values in simple cases that
       respect the requirements of independence and completeness
       (sec.~18B) and of total evidence (sec.~45B). He states that the
       section proves no possibility, only the insufficiency of the
       impossibility arguments.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-laplace-family}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-laplace-family}{The family \((s_1+m)/(s+2m)\) of sec.~47A}
Let \(s\geq1\), \(0\leq s_1\leq s\), \(r=s_1/s\) and \(m\geq0\), and
put \(c_m=(s_1+m)/(s+2m)\). Then
\[
 c_m-r=\frac{m(1-2r)}{s+2m},\qquad
 c_m-\tfrac12=\frac{s(2r-1)}{2(s+2m)}.
\]
Hence \(c_0=r\) (the straight rule), and for \(m>0\): \(c_m=\tfrac12\)
if \(r=\tfrac12\), and otherwise \(c_m\) lies strictly between \(r\)
and \(\tfrac12\); in particular \(0<c_m<1\), and \(c_m<r\) whenever
\(r>\tfrac12\). The case \(m=1\) is Laplace's rule
\((s_1+1)/(s+2)\).
\end{hbtheorem}

For Carnap's example \(s=100\), \(s_1=80\): \(c_1=81/102\approx0.794\),
below \(r=0.8\). The family is Carnap's illustration in sec.~47A; he
notes that contradictions are avoided if different properties receive
suitably different functions, and he does not adopt any member here.
The later \(c^*\) gives \((s_1+w)/(s+\kappa)\) for a property of width
\(w\) (\cref{thm:p15-carnap-predictive}), which for \(w/\kappa=\tfrac12\)
is a member of the family with \(m=\kappa/2\).

\subsection*{Sec.~48: is probability\texorpdfstring{\(_1\)}{1} used quantitatively?}

Section~48 makes psychological claims about use, not about the nature of
probability\(_1\), and Carnap says they prove nothing about an
objective explicatum \cite[sec.~48, pp.~233--241]{Carnap1950Probability}.
(A) Probability\(_1\) is used comparatively, at least for two hypotheses
on one evidence or one hypothesis on two nested evidences. (B) For games
of chance, numerical values are generally accepted: on evidence that a
symmetric die gave 1{,}000 aces in 6{,}000 throws, almost everyone
assigns \(1/6\) to an ace on the next throw and accepts odds of one to
five as equitable. (C) In direct inference with a known frequency---\(c\)
is one of the 1{,}000 inhabitants of Norville, of whom 800 are of
Norwegian descent---the value \(0.8\) is taken, as sec.~41C justifies.
(D) Where no frequency fixes the value, a value can still be measured
from choices: offered \$10 either if a friend arrives by train or if a
die known to be fair does not show an ace, a person who takes the first
option reveals a value of at least \(5/6\) for the train hypothesis, and
one who takes the second a value of at most \(5/6\); repeated choices
narrow the interval (Borel's device). What is measured is the
pragmatical degree of belief of a person at a time, not the logical
concept. (E) People treat disagreement about a value on shared evidence
as they treat disagreement about the distance of the moon, not as they
treat a difference of taste: if one is right, the other is wrong.
Relativity to evidence is not subjectivity. These habits indicate an
implicit belief in an objective concept and give some ground for hope,
nothing more.

\subsection*{Sec.~49: usefulness, and probability as a guide of life}

\emph{Theoretical usefulness (philosophical).} Like deductive logic,
inductive logic applies where problems are simple or can be fully
formulated, which excludes, Carnap says, an evaluation of general
relativity on the whole evidence available in 1919, and includes
statistical inference. He expects inductive logic to found the
inductive part of mathematical statistics as Frege, Russell and
Whitehead founded mathematics on logic, and anticipates small
corrections to small-sample estimates; Jeffreys is credited as the
pioneer \cite[sec.~49A, pp.~241--246]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\emph{Practical usefulness (philosophical).} A frequency-in-the-long-run
statement can guide a decision only if known, and as a limit it never is.
A statement of an estimate relative to evidence is analytic, hence known,
and together with the evidence it can ground a decision. The two
examples are three measurements \(80.0\), \(80.1\), \(80.5\) of a rod,
whose estimate is the mean \(80.2\), and a sample of \(80\) persons
from Chicago with \(60\) having \(M\), where Carnap assumes, for
illustration only, that the probability\(_1\) of \(M\) for a further
person is \(0.73\); by 41D (8) this is also the estimate of the
frequency of \(M\) in the population, and a later census showing a
different frequency would not refute it. Neither empirical science nor
inductive logic alone is a guide of life, only both together
\cite[sec.~49B, pp.~246--252]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\subsection*{Secs.~50--51: rules for decisions}

Carnap stresses that a rule for decisions belongs to the methodology of
induction and to psychology, not to inductive logic
\cite[sec.~50A, pp.~252--254]{Carnap1950Probability}. The agent \(X\)
uses his total evidence \(e\) and can compute \(c\) on it.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-decision}{Actions, outcomes, gains, estimates (sec.~50E)}
Let \(j_1,\ldots,j_a\) describe the possible actions of \(X\) and
\(h_1,\ldots,h_b\) the possible resulting events, each set L-exclusive
in pairs and L-disjunct with respect to \(e\) (Carnap takes both sets
finite for simplicity). Let \(f_0\) be the present fortune of \(X\) in
money units and \(f_{ik}\) his fortune if he performs \(j_i\) and \(h_k\)
occurs; the gain is \(g_{ik}=f_{ik}-f_0\), a loss being a negative
gain. With \(q_{ik}=c(h_k,e\land j_i)\), the \emph{estimate of the gain}
of \(j_i\) is \(g'_i=\sum_kq_{ik}g_{ik}\), and the estimate of fortune
is \(f'_i=f_0+g'_i\). For two actions, \(j_1\) is \emph{favorable},
\emph{unfavorable} or \emph{neutral} for \(X\) according as
\(f'_1-f'_2\) is positive, negative or zero.

Given a total utility \(\bar u\) of fortune, the \emph{utility} of the
gain \(g_{ik}\) is \(\bar u(f_{ik})-\bar u(f_0)\) and the \emph{estimate
of utility} of \(j_i\) is \(\bar g'_i=\sum_kq_{ik}(\bar u(f_{ik})-\bar u(f_0))\);
the subjective counterparts of favorable, unfavorable and neutral use
\(\bar g'\) in place of \(g'\) (sec.~51B). Only differences of total
utility are interpreted. If \(\bar u\) is continuous and strictly
increasing, the \emph{money equivalent} \({}^*g'_i\) of \(\bar g'_i\) is
the gain whose utility is \(\bar g'_i\), and \({}^*f'_i=f_0+{}^*g'_i\).
\end{hbdefinition}

The five rules of secs.~50--51, in the order Carnap improves them
\cite[secs.~50B--51A, pp.~254--270]{Carnap1950Probability}:
\begin{description}
 \item[\(R_1\)] (high probability) treat events with high \(c\) on
   \(e\) as certain;
 \item[\(R_2\)] (maximum probability) among exhaustive, pairwise
   exclusive events, treat the most probable as certain;
 \item[\(R_3\)] (use of estimates) if a feature of the decision would be
   \(F(u)\) for a known value \(u\) of an unknown magnitude, act in
   certain respects as though \(u\) were equal or near to its estimate
   \(u'\), taking the feature \(F(u')\);
 \item[\(R_4\)] (maximum estimated gain) choose an action whose
   \(g'_i\) is not lower than that of any other, any maximizer being
   permitted; its special form \(R_4^*\) for an offer: accept if
   favorable, reject if unfavorable, either if neutral;
 \item[\(R_5\)] (maximum estimated utility) choose an action whose
   \(\bar g'_i\) is a maximum.
\end{description}

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-decision-examples}{Carnap's four examples (sec.~50C--D)}
\emph{Bookshop.} The probabilities of the possible demands for a book
form a bell curve symmetric about 80. \(R_1\) does not apply, and
\(R_2\) and \(R_3\) both give 80, because a distribution symmetric
about its mode has its estimate at the mode
(\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-decision}(1)). With losses larger for
too few copies than for too many, \(R_4\) orders somewhat more.

\emph{Restaurant.} The number of orders of a dish is \(0,1,\ldots,6\)
with probabilities \(0.20, 0.19, 0.18, 0.17, 0.16, 0.10, 0\). \(R_2\)
says to prepare none, although at least one order has probability
\(0.8\). The estimate is
\(0.19+2(0.18)+3(0.17)+4(0.16)+5(0.10)=2.2\), and \(R_3\) prepares for
two.

\emph{Lottery.} One of 100 equally likely tickets wins \$100. \(R_1\)
and \(R_2\) treat a held ticket as a sure loser and would sell it for
10 cents; its estimated gain is \(100/100=\$1\), the price below which
\(R_3\) and \(R_4\) forbid selling. No possible gain lies near \$1, so
acting as if the gain were near its estimate is wrong in other respects.

\emph{Fire insurance.} A house worth \(10{,}000\) burns within the year
with probability \(0.001\), independently of insuring. Insuring at
premium \(r\) gives the sure gain \(-r\); not insuring has estimated
gain \(-10{,}000\times0.001=-10\). Insuring is favorable, unfavorable or
neutral as \(r<10\), \(r>10\), \(r=10\). \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) reject
even a premium of \$5.
\end{hbexample}

Two cases defeat \(R_4^*\) \cite[sec.~50E, pp.~262--264]{Carnap1950Probability}.
With fortune \(10{,}000\), an even-odds bet of \(8{,}000\) on a fair
coin is neutral and one of \(8{,}000\) against \(8{,}001\) favorable, yet
both are unreasonable; and insurance at premium \(12\) is unfavorable,
yet reasonable for an owner whose fortune is little more than the house.
Raising the threshold for acceptance cannot repair both, since the
second case calls for accepting an unfavorable offer. Carnap's diagnosis
in sec.~51A is that a large loss should weigh more than in proportion to
its amount, because the agent suffers from it more than in proportion.

\emph{Utility laws (psychological hypotheses).} Carnap states two laws
due to Daniel Bernoulli and declines to adopt either, since deciding
them is a task for psychology \cite[sec.~51A--C, pp.~266--273]{Carnap1950Probability}.
(1) \emph{Diminishing marginal utility}: the utility of a fixed gain
added to a fortune \(f_0\) is smaller the larger \(f_0\). (2)
\emph{Bernoulli's law}: for a gain \(\Delta f\) small compared with
\(f_0\), the utility is \(\Delta\bar f=k\Delta f/f_0\) with a constant
\(k>0\) depending on the person and time; its exact form is the
differential law (4) \(d\bar f=k\,df/f\). Carnap reports that the
quantitative law (2) is generally regarded as an oversimplification and
summarizes Karl Menger's analysis, which adds saturation, distorted
weighting of very small and very large probabilities, and a maximal
fraction of fortune a person will risk. He also records that von
Neumann and Morgenstern derive a utility unique up to zero and unit from
complete preferences over lotteries with given probabilities, and argues
that their restriction to known probability\(_2\) values is unnecessary
once probability\(_1\) is admitted, since the latter is never unknown in
that sense. The modern rule of maximizing expected utility for finite
acts is in \cref{sec:p17-finite-expected-utility}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-decision}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-decision}{The decision rules and the consequences of Bernoulli's law}
Use \cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-decision}, with \(f_0>0\) and every
possible fortune positive.
\begin{enumerate}
 \item (\(R_2\) and \(R_3\).) If an unknown magnitude has finitely many
       values with probabilities symmetric about \(u_0\), its estimate
       is \(u_0\).
 \item (\(R_4\) and \(R_4^*\).) For two actions \(R_4\) chooses
       \(j_1\) exactly when \(f'_1\geq f'_2\); so it accepts favorable
       and rejects unfavorable offers, and permits either for neutral
       ones. If \(\bar u\) is affine and increasing on an interval
       containing all possible fortunes, \(R_4\) and \(R_5\) select the
       same actions.
 \item (Law (1).) Suppose \(\bar u(f+g)-\bar u(f)\) is strictly
       decreasing in \(f\) for each \(g>0\). For an even-odds bet with
       stake \(0<u<f_0\) on an event of probability \(\tfrac12\), the
       estimated gain is \(0\) and the estimate of utility is negative.
 \item (Laws (4), (5), (3).) Suppose \(\bar u\) is differentiable on
       \((0,\infty)\) with \(\bar u'(f)=k/f\), \(k>0\). Then
       \(\bar u(f_1)-\bar u(f_0)=k\log(f_1/f_0)\), and
       \(\bar u(f_1)-\bar u(f_0)=\bar u(f_3)-\bar u(f_2)\) if and only
       if \(f_1/f_0=f_3/f_2\).
 \item (Formula (7).) Under (4), for an action with gains \(g_k\) of
       probabilities \(q_k\) (summing to one),
       \[
        {}^*f'=f_0+{}^*g'=\prod_k(f_0+g_k)^{q_k}.
       \]
       \({}^*g'\) is a strictly increasing function of \(\bar g'\), so
       \(R_5\) may maximize \({}^*g'\) instead; and \({}^*g'\leq g'\), with
       equality exactly when all \(g_k\) with \(q_k>0\) are equal.
 \item (Fair bets.) Under (4), the even-odds bet of item (3) has
       \({}^*f'=\sqrt{(f_0+u)(f_0-u)}<f_0\).
 \item (Insurance.) Under (4), let a house of value \(h\) be part of a
       fortune \(f_0>h\), burn with probability \(p\in(0,1)\)
       independently of insuring, and let full insurance cost a premium
       \(r<f_0\). Insuring has larger estimate of utility than not
       insuring if and only if \(r<r^*\), where
       \[
        r^*=f_0-f_0^{1-p}(f_0-h)^p=f_0(1-1/q),\qquad
        q=\bigl(f_0/(f_0-h)\bigr)^p.
       \]
       Moreover \(r^*>ph\), the fair premium; \(r^*\) is strictly
       decreasing in \(f_0\) on \((h,\infty)\); and \(r^*\to ph\) as
       \(f_0\to\infty\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Item (3) is Carnap's comparative argument, which uses only law (1):
the utility from \(f_0-u\) to \(f_0\) exceeds that from \(f_0\) to
\(f_0+u\). Items (6) and (7) are his quantitative results, which he
derives by dividing the utility scale into equal steps and the fortune
scale into equal ratios. Item (5) is Daniel Bernoulli's main theorem in
Carnap's form, and item (2) contains Carnap's remark that \(R_4\) and
\(R_5\) agree where utility is proportional to gain. Carnap states the
rule \(R_5\) as adequate as a guide of life only under two conditions:
a quantitative utility law, which psychology must supply and which is
probably more complex than Bernoulli's, and an adequate explicatum of
estimate, which inductive logic must supply \cite[sec.~51C, pp.~278--279]{Carnap1950Probability}.
He adds that \(R_5\) is a rule of applied logic, not a moral rule: it
takes the agent's interests as given, so a small pleasure in betting can
make a small fair bet acceptable.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-bernoulli-tables}{Carnap's two tables (sec.~51C)}
\emph{Even-odds bets with \(f_0=10{,}000\)} (item (6)): the money
equivalent of the estimate of utility is
\({}^*g'=\sqrt{10^8-u^2}-10^4\). Exact values: \(u=8{,}000\) gives
\({}^*f'=6{,}000\), \({}^*g'=-4{,}000\); \(u=1{,}000\) gives
\(9{,}949.874\ldots\) and \(-50.126\ldots\); \(u=100\) gives
\(-0.500\ldots\); \(u=10\) gives \(-0.005\ldots\); \(u=1\) gives
\(-0.00005\ldots\). The favorable bet of \(8{,}000\) against \(8{,}001\)
has \({}^*f'=\sqrt{18{,}001\times2{,}000}=6{,}000.17\ldots\), still a
utility loss equivalent to about \(4{,}000\).

\emph{Insurance with \(h=10{,}000\), \(p=0.001\)} (item (7)): the
subjectively neutral premium \(r^*=f_0(1-1/q)\) is \(46.505\ldots\) for
\(f_0=10{,}100\); \(16.470\ldots\) for \(15{,}000\); \(13.858\ldots\)
for \(20{,}000\); \(11.505\ldots\) for \(40{,}000\); and \(10.535\ldots\)

\end{hbexample}

\section{Arguments, conventions, and the reduction to state descriptions (secs.~52--54)}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-conventions}

\emph{Sec.~52 (logical form).} The arguments of probability\(_1\) can
be the propositions or events expressed by sentences, written with
sentences in argument place (method~1, used by Keynes and Jeffreys), or
the sentences themselves, written by their names in the metalanguage
(method~2, used by Mazurkiewicz, Hosiasson and Carnap). Under method~1
the probability operator is not truth-functional---two true sentences
can receive different probabilities on the same evidence---so the
theory needs a modal object language: the theorem that \(c(h,e)=1\)
when \(e\) is not L-false and L-implies \(h\) becomes a statement with a
necessity operator. Under method~2 the same theorem is a semantical
statement about L-truth, and the object language can stay extensional.
Carnap calls the difference technical and says every theorem
translates. For probability\(_2\), whose arguments are properties and
whose values are invariant under coextensive replacement, method~1 in
the object language is preferable \cite[sec.~52, pp.~279--284]{Carnap1950Probability}.
Any sentence may be the hypothesis, and any sentence that is not
L-false may be the evidence; the exclusion corresponds to leaving
\(0/0\) undefined. A value of \(c\) is relative to the language system
as well as to the evidence: the number of individual constants matters
for general sentences, and the number of primitive predicates can
matter through logical width.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-conventions}{The conventions of sec.~53 (C53-1, C53-2, C53-3)}
Let \(c\) be a real function defined for some pairs of sentences of a
system \(\mathfrak L\).
\begin{description}
 \item[C53-1.] \(c\) is not adequate as an explicatum of
   probability\(_1\) unless, for all sentences for which it is defined:
   (a) \(c(h,e)=c(h,e')\) if \(e\), \(e'\) are L-equivalent;
   (b) \(c(h,e)=c(h',e)\) if \(h\), \(h'\) are L-equivalent;
   (c) \(c(h\land j,e)=c(h,e)\times c(j,e\land h)\) (general
   multiplication principle);
   (d) \(c(h\lor j,e)=c(h,e)+c(j,e)\) if \(e\land h\land j\) is
   L-false (special addition principle).
 \item[C53-2.] \(c(t,e)=1\) for every \(e\) that is not L-false.
 \item[C53-3.] In a finite system \(\mathfrak L_N\), \(c(z,t)>0\) for
   every state description \(z\).
\end{description}
\end{hbdefinition}

The conventions are heuristic, used only in secs.~53--54; C53-1 is
necessary for adequacy, not sufficient. Carnap motivates (c) and (d)
by betting: if \(X\) would bet at quotient \(r_1\) that a candidate is
nominated and, given nomination, at \(r_2\) that he is elected, he will
bet at \(r_1r_2\) on both; and quotients for incompatible nominations
add. The interval \([0,1]\) is chosen because \(c\) is read as a fair
betting quotient and, sometimes, as an estimate of frequency. C53-3
says that before any observation no possible case of a finite system is
excluded; Carnap notes that it is not generally recognized, that some
methods examined in the second volume violate it implicitly, and that it
is not laid down for \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), which has infinitely many
state descriptions \cite[sec.~53, pp.~284--287]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-t53}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-t53}{T53-1, and the conventions characterize the regular functions}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item (T53-1.) Let \(c\) satisfy C53-1a--d and C53-2 and be defined
       for every pair whose second member is not L-false. For \(e\) not
       L-false:
       (a) \(c(e\land h,t)=c(e,t)\times c(h,e)\);
       (b) if \(c(e,t)\ne0\), \(c(h,e)=c(e\land h,t)/c(e,t)\);
       (c) if \(h_1,\ldots,h_n\) are such that \(e\land h_p\land h_{p'}\)
       is L-false for \(p\ne p'\), then
       \(c(h_1\lor\cdots\lor h_n,e)=\sum_pc(h_p,e)\);
       (d) \(c(h,e)+c(\neg h,e)=1\);
       (e) \(c(\neg t,e)=0\);
       (f) \(c(h,e)=0\) if \(h\) is L-false.
 \item (Sec.~54B--D, p.~294.) In \(\mathfrak L_N\), a function \(c\)
       defined exactly on the pairs \((h,e)\) with \(e\) not L-false
       satisfies C53-1a--d, C53-2 and C53-3 if and only if it is a
       regular \(c\)-function
       (\cref{def:p15-carnap-regular-confirmation}). The measure is then
       unique: \(m(z)=c(z,t)=c_0(z)\), and \(c(h,e)=c_0(e\land h)/c_0(e)\)
       with \(c_0(j)=\sum_{z\in R(j)}c_0(z)\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

\emph{Sec.~54 (the reduction).} Carnap reduces the search for \(c\) in
three steps and then states two requirements
\cite[sec.~54, pp.~287--292]{Carnap1950Probability}. (A) For sentences
of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), \(c(h,e)\) is to be the limit of its values
in \(\mathfrak L_N\) as \(N\to\infty\) when the limit exists (his (1);
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-limits}). (B) In \(\mathfrak L_N\), with the
null confirmation \(c_0(j)=c(j,t)\) (his (2);
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-null}), \(c(h,e)=c_0(e\land h)/c_0(e)\) (his
(3)); this step is made after (A) because only in the finite systems is
\(c_0(e)=0\) restricted to L-false \(e\). (C) \(c_0(j)\) is the sum of
\(c_0\) over the state descriptions in the range of \(j\), and \(0\)
for L-false \(j\) (his (4)). (D) A function \(c_0\) on state
descriptions must be positive and sum to one (his (5)); the resulting
\(c\) is called regular. (E) The functions for different \(N\) must fit
together: each \(c_0(z)\) in \(\mathfrak L_N\) is the sum of the values
in \(\mathfrak L_{N+1}\) of the state descriptions of which \(z\) is a
subconjunction (his (6)), because a nongeneral sentence means the same
in every system containing it. This requirement is D57-3
(\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-fitting}), and its effect on nongeneral
sentences is \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-null-fitting}(2). Theorem
\ref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-t53}(2) is the exact form of steps (B)--(D).
Carnap compares the choice among regular functions to the choice among
temperature scales: many are not entirely inadequate, some are better
than others, and the choice of one, \(c^*\), is deferred to the second
volume.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-axiom-systems}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-axiom-systems}{What the comparison establishes}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Every regular \(c\)-function of \(\mathfrak L_N\) satisfies
       T59-1a, b, c, e, h, i, k, l, n, p and T59-2a, h, for all
       sentences with evidence not L-false.
 \item (Jeffreys's first defect.) Let \(P\) be defined on all pairs of
       sentences including L-false evidence, and satisfy: \(P(q\mid p)=1\)
       if \(p\) L-implies \(q\), and \(P(q\mid p)=0\) if \(p\) L-implies
       \(\neg q\), with one value per pair. Then no such \(P\) exists;
       the contradiction disappears when L-false evidence is excluded.
 \item (Carnap's weakness argument.) In \(\mathfrak L_N\), if \(e\) is
       not L-false and L-implies neither \(h\) nor \(\neg h\), the
       axioms valid for all regular \(c\)-functions impose no condition
       on \(c(h,e)\) beyond \(0<c(h,e)<1\); every value in \((0,1)\) is
       taken by some regular \(c\) (T59-5f).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Jeffreys's second defect is essential, Carnap argues. Convention~1
assigns equal numbers to equally probable propositions but states no
condition under which propositions are equally probable; Jeffreys later
reads it as the principle of indifference in the strong form that
hypotheses with no reason to prefer one are equally probable, and uses
it in proofs. Carnap holds that the principle in that general form
yields contradictions, to be shown in the second volume, so the system
as used is inconsistent, and without the principle it holds for all
regular functions. The same verdict applies to the classical theory:
read as an uninterpreted system with ``equipossible'' primitive, it is
consistent and as weak as the modern systems. Frequency axiom systems
(Bernstein, Reichenbach, Kolmogorov, D\"orge, Evans and Kleene,
Cram\'er, and probably Copeland) take classes as arguments and become
equally weak when reinterpreted on ranges. Since a value \(c(h,e)=r\)
is L-determinate, Carnap concludes that a logical theory may and should
state such values; the regular theory is the first step, to be narrowed
by further requirements that achieve what indifference intended without
its contradictions. The inconsistency of the strong principle is not
proved in the 1950 volume and is not asserted here.

\section{Symmetrical functions (secs.~90--92) and their deductive prerequisites}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}

Chapter~VIII of the 1950 book narrows the regular functions to the
symmetrical ones, those that treat all individuals on a par, and proves
the theorems on them that hold before any particular \(c\)-function is
chosen. It presupposes secs.~25--27 of the chapter on deductive logic
\cite[sec.~90, pp.~483--484]{Carnap1950Probability}. The chapter's
sections are numbered 90, 91, 92, 94, 95 and 96: the 1950 edition has
no section numbered 93, and sec.~92 is followed directly by sec.~94 on
p.~492; the table of contents lists the same sequence. Secs.~94--96
are treated in \cref{thm:p15-carnap-direct} and
\cref{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-bernoulli}. The inputs are the systems of
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-systems}, possibly with primitive predicates
of degree greater than one, the regular \(m\)- and \(c\)-functions of
\cref{def:p15-carnap-regular-confirmation}, the fitting sequences of
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-fitting}, and the limit functions of
\cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-limits}. No choice principle is used.
Every value computed below is a rational multiple of a value of the
given function, and the theorems do not supply a numerical value for a
hypothesis about an individual not mentioned in the evidence.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-division}{Divisions, in-correlations, distributions, structure descriptions (D25-4, D26-1--6, D27-1)}
Let \(\mathfrak L\) be \(\mathfrak L_N\) or \(\mathfrak L_\infty\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Molecular predicates \(M_1,\ldots,M_p\) of degree one
       (\(p\geq2\)) form a \emph{division} if
       \(\forall x(M_1x\lor\cdots\lor M_px)\) is L-true, any two distinct
       ones are L-exclusive, and none is L-empty (D25-4). A molecular
       predicate expression is built by connectives from primitive
       predicates applied to one and the same argument expression
       (A25-1), so a molecular predicate of degree one contains only
       primitive predicates of degree one.
 \item An \emph{in-correlation} \(C\) in \(\mathfrak L\) is a one-one
       map of the set of individual constants of \(\mathfrak L\) onto
       itself (D26-1). For an expression \(i\), \(C(i)\) is the result
       of replacing each individual constant in \(i\) by its correlate
       (D26-2). Two expressions are \emph{isomorphic} if one is
       \(C\) of the other for some \(C\), up to the order of conjunctive
       components (D26-3a). For a state description \(z\), the state
       description constructed from \(z\) by \(C\) is \(C(z)\) with
       its components put back into the fixed order (D26-4).
 \item For \(n\) given individual constants and a division, an
       \emph{individual distribution} \(i\) is a conjunction, in the
       fixed order, of full sentences \(M_m b\), one for each of the
       \(n\) constants \(b\) (D26-6a). If \(i\) contains \(n_m\) full
       sentences of \(M_m\), these \(n_1,\ldots,n_p\) are its
       \emph{cardinal numbers}. The \emph{statistical distribution}
       \(j\) corresponding to \(i\) is the disjunction, in the fixed
       order, of all individual distributions for the same constants and
       the same division that are isomorphic to \(i\) (D26-6b, c).
 \item In \(\mathfrak L_N\), the \emph{structure description}
       \(\mathrm{Str}_i\) corresponding to a state description
       \(\mathfrak Z_i\) is the disjunction, in the fixed order, of all
       state descriptions isomorphic to \(\mathfrak Z_i\) (D27-1); its
       number of disjuncts is \(\zeta_i\). Structure descriptions are
       defined only in the finite systems, whose state descriptions are
       sentences.
 \item For integers \(m\geq n\geq0\),
       \(\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{m}{n}\defeq m!/(m-n)!\), the product of
       \(n\) descending factors beginning with \(m\) (D40-3, T40-12).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbdefinition}

Carnap writes \(\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{m}{n}\) to parallel the binomial
coefficient \(\binom mn=\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{m}{n}/n!\) (T40-13e), because
values for an individual distribution come out in the bracket function
and those for the corresponding statistical distribution in the
binomial coefficient with the same arguments
\cite[D40-3, T40-12, T40-13, p.~152; sec.~92, p.~492]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hblemma}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-isomorphism}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-isomorphism}{Invariance of the L-concepts and the distribution lemmas (T26-2, T26-5, T27-2, T27-3, T40-32)}
Let \(C\) be an in-correlation in \(\mathfrak L\) (\(\mathfrak L_N\) or
\(\mathfrak L_\infty\)), and \(i,j\) sentences with \(i'=C(i)\),
\(j'=C(j)\).
\begin{enumerate}
 \item (T26-2a.) \(R(i')\) is the set of state descriptions constructed
       by \(C\) from the members of \(R(i)\).
 \item (T26-2b--e.) \(i\) is L-true (L-false, factual) iff \(i'\) is;
       L-implication, L-equivalence, L-exclusion and L-disjunctness hold
       between \(i\) and \(j\) iff they hold between \(i'\) and \(j'\).
 \item (T26-5.) For a division and \(n\) given constants: every
       individual distribution is factual; two distinct individual
       distributions are L-exclusive; if \(j\) corresponds to \(i\),
       then \(i\) L-implies \(j\); and for individual distributions
       \(i,k\) the following are equivalent: \(j\) is the statistical
       distribution corresponding to \(k\); \(k\) is isomorphic to
       \(i\); \(k\) L-implies \(j\).
 \item (T40-32a, b.) An individual distribution \(k\) for the same
       constants is isomorphic to \(i\) iff it has the same cardinal
       numbers. Hence \(j\) has exactly \(n!/(n_1!\cdots n_p!)\)
       disjuncts.
 \item (T27-2f, T27-3.) In \(\mathfrak L_N\), \(\mathfrak Z_k\) belongs
       to \(\mathrm{Str}_i\) iff it is isomorphic to \(\mathfrak Z_i\),
       iff it L-implies \(\mathrm{Str}_i\), iff \(\mathrm{Str}_i\)
       holds in \(\mathfrak Z_k\). For a purely general sentence \(l\),
       either \(\mathrm{Str}_i\) L-implies \(l\) or it L-implies
       \(\neg l\); and if \(l\) is not L-false it is L-equivalent to a
       disjunction of structure descriptions.
\end{enumerate}
\end{hblemma}

Item (1) is proved by Carnap by induction on the construction of
sentences from the recursive truth conditions of sec.~18, the
universal quantifier being treated through all its instances; the
annex follows that route. Since every individual is named, the
substitutional clause is exact in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) as well. The
sentences \(i\) and \(C(i)\) are in general not L-equivalent: \(Pa\) and
\(Pb\) are isomorphic but not L-equivalent \cite[T26-2 and the
remark following it, pp.~110--111]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}{Symmetrical \(m\)- and \(c\)-functions (D90-1--4, D91-1--4)}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item A regular \(m\)-function for the state descriptions of
       \(\mathfrak L_N\) is \emph{symmetrical} if isomorphic state
       descriptions receive the same value (D90-1). It is extended to
       sentences by \(m(j)=0\) for L-false \(j\) and otherwise the sum
       over \(R(j)\) (D90-2).
 \item A \emph{fitting symmetrical \(m\)-sequence} (for state
       descriptions or for sentences) is a fitting \(m\)-sequence whose
       \(N\)th member is symmetrical for \(\mathfrak L_N\) for every
       \(N\) (D90-3, D90-4).
 \item A \emph{symmetrical \(c\)-function} for \(\mathfrak L_N\) is one
       based on a symmetrical \(m\)-function for sentences, that is,
       \(c(h,e)=m(e\land h)/m(e)\) for \(e\) not L-false (D91-1). A
       \emph{fitting symmetrical \(c\)-sequence} is the sequence of
       \(c\)-functions based on a fitting symmetrical \(m\)-sequence for
       sentences (D91-2).
 \item For a sequence of symmetrical functions, the corresponding
       symmetrical functions \(m_\infty\), \(c_\infty\) for
       \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) are the limits as in
       \cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-limits}, with no value where the limit
       does not exist (D91-3, D91-4).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbdefinition}

Carnap's motivation is a requirement of nondiscrimination. If
\(e\) is \(Pa\land Pb\), \(h\) is \(Pc\) and \(h'\) is \(Pd\), the
logical relation of \(e\) to \(h\) is the same as to \(h'\), so an
adequate \(c\) must give them the same value, whatever that value is;
the same holds for \(c(Pc,Pc\lor Pa)\) and \(c(Pd,Pd\lor Pa)\), where
the constant shared by evidence and hypothesis must be replaced in both.
He regards this as the inductive analogue of the invariance of the
L-concepts, observes that not every regular function satisfies it, and
holds that it is tacitly accepted by all authors on
probability\(_1\), classical and modern \cite[sec.~90, pp.~484--485;
sec.~91, pp.~488--489]{Carnap1950Probability}. This last claim is a
historical and philosophical assessment, not a theorem.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}{Structure descriptions and invariance (T90-1, T91-1, T91-2, T91-3)}
\begin{enumerate}
 \item (T90-1.) For a regular \(m\) in \(\mathfrak L_N\),
       \(m(\mathrm{Str}_i)\) is the sum of \(m\) over the state
       descriptions isomorphic to \(\mathfrak Z_i\).
 \item (T91-1.) A symmetrical \(c\) for \(\mathfrak L_N\) has a value
       for \(h,e\) iff \(e\) is not L-false, and then
       \(c(h,e)=m(e\land h)/m(e)\) for the symmetrical \(m\) on which
       it is based.
 \item (T91-2.) Let \((m_N)\) be a fitting symmetrical \(m\)-sequence
       for sentences, \((c_N)\) the \(c\)-functions based on it, and
       \(m,c\) either \(m_N,c_N\) for some \(N\) or \(m_\infty,c_\infty\).
       Let \(C\) be an in-correlation in the corresponding system,
       \(i'=C(i)\), \(h'=C(h)\), \(e'=C(e)\). Then (a)
       \(m(i')=m(i)\); (b) \(c(h',e')=c(h,e)\); (c) if \(h\) and \(e\)
       have no individual constant in common, and neither have \(h'\)
       and \(e\), then \(c(h',e)=c(h,e)\). In \(\mathfrak L_N\) the
       values in (b) and (c) exist when \(e\) is not L-false; in
       \(\mathfrak L_\infty\), either both sides have a value or
       neither has.
 \item (T91-3.) For a symmetrical \(m\) in \(\mathfrak L_N\) and \(c\)
       based on it, \(m(\mathrm{Str}_i)=\zeta_i\,m(\mathfrak Z_i)\) and
       \(c(\mathfrak Z_i,\mathrm{Str}_i)=1/\zeta_i\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

Carnap proves the \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) case of (3) from the finite case
by D91-3 and T40-21e: two sequences that agree on a final segment have
the same limit. The annex makes the needed step explicit: for each
large \(N\) there is an in-correlation of \(\mathfrak L_N\) agreeing
with \(C\) on the finitely many constants of the sentences, so the two
value sequences agree from that \(N\) on; this also shows that a value
exists on one side exactly when it exists on the other. An
in-correlation of \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) is any permutation of a
denumerable set, but only its restriction to finitely many constants is
used.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-t92-1}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-t92-1}{Individual and statistical distributions as evidence (T92-1)}
Let \(M_1,\ldots,M_p\) form a division; let \(i,i'\) be isomorphic
individual distributions for \(n\) given constants, with cardinal
numbers \(n_1,\ldots,n_p\), and \(j\) the corresponding statistical
distribution, with \(d\) disjuncts. Let \(h\) and \(e\) be sentences
containing none of the constants of \(j\), \(e\) not L-false, and
\(m,c\) as in \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}(3). Then
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[(a)] \(d=n!/(n_1!n_2!\cdots n_p!)\);
 \item[(b)] \(m(i')=m(i)\);
 \item[(c)] \(m(j)=d\,m(i)\);
 \item[(d)] \(m(h\land i)=m(h\land i')\);
 \item[(e)] \(m(h\land j)=d\,m(h\land i)\);
 \item[(f)] \(c(i,e)=c(i',e)\);
 \item[(g)] \(c(j,e)=d\,c(i,e)\);
 \item[(h)] \(c(h,i)=c(h,i')\);
 \item[(i)] \(c(h,j)=c(h,i)\);
 \item[(j)] \(m(j)=\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_p!}\,m(i)\);
 \item[(k)] \(c(j,e)=\dfrac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_p!}\,c(i,e)\).
\end{enumerate}
In \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) each equation means that one side has a value
iff the other has, and then they are equal; when the sequence is fitting
and \(h,e\) are nongeneral, all the values exist.
\end{hbtheorem}

Item (i) is the one Carnap marks as especially important: for a
hypothesis about other individuals, a symmetrical \(c\) gives the same
value on an individual distribution as on the bare statistical report of
its numbers, so the identity of the observed individuals is irrelevant
\cite[T92-1 and the remark following it, pp.~489--490]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-t92-3}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-t92-3}{A statistical distribution and its part (T92-3)}
Let \(M_1,\ldots,M_p\) form a division. Let \(i\) be an individual
distribution for \(s\) constants with cardinal numbers
\(s_1,\ldots,s_p\), and \(i'\) one for \(s'\) further constants, none
occurring in \(i\), with cardinal numbers \(s'_1,\ldots,s'_p\). Let
\(j,j'\) be the corresponding statistical distributions, and \(e\) the
statistical distribution for all \(s+s'\) constants corresponding to
\(i\land i'\) (cardinal numbers \(s_m+s'_m\)). With \(m,c\) as in
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}(3):
\begin{enumerate}
 \item[(a)] \(j\), \(j'\), \(e\) have \(s!/\prod_ms_m!\),
       \(s'!/\prod_ms'_m!\), \((s+s')!/\prod_m(s_m+s'_m)!\) disjuncts;
 \item[(b)] \(m(e)=\dfrac{(s+s')!}{\prod_m(s_m+s'_m)!}\,m(i\land i')\);
 \item[(c)--(f)] \(j\land j'\) L-implies \(e\); \(e\land j\) L-implies
       \(j'\); \(e\land j\) is L-equivalent to \(j\land j'\); \(e\land i\)
       is L-equivalent to \(i\land j'\);
 \item[(g)] \(m(e\land i)=m(i\land j')=\dfrac{s'!}{\prod_ms'_m!}\,m(i\land i')\);
 \item[(h)] \(m(j\land j')=\dfrac{s!}{\prod_ms_m!}\,m(i\land j')
       =\dfrac{s!}{\prod_ms_m!}\cdot\dfrac{s'!}{\prod_ms'_m!}\,m(i\land i')\);
 \item[(i)] \(c(i,e)=\dfrac{s'!}{\prod_ms'_m!}\cdot
       \dfrac{\prod_m(s_m+s'_m)!}{(s+s')!}
       =\dfrac{\prod_m\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{s_m+s'_m}{s_m}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{s+s'}{s}}\);
 \item[(j)] \(c(j,e)=\dfrac{s!}{\prod_ms_m!}\cdot\dfrac{s'!}{\prod_ms'_m!}
       \cdot\dfrac{\prod_m(s_m+s'_m)!}{(s+s')!}
       =\dfrac{s!}{\prod_ms_m!}\,c(i,e)
       =\dfrac{\prod_m\binom{s_m+s'_m}{s_m}}{\binom{s+s'}{s}}\).
\end{enumerate}
In \(\mathfrak L_\infty\) the values (i) and (j) exist and are the same
numbers. Since the finite values do not depend on \(N\), this holds for
the limit function of any sequence of symmetrical functions, fitting or
not; Carnap indicates the passage through T57-5 or T57-6, which concern
fitting sequences.
\end{hbtheorem}

These are, in Carnap's description, the first theorems of the theory
that give values of \(c\) other than \(0\) and \(1\) absolutely rather
than relative to other values; that is possible at this stage only
because the individuals of the hypothesis already occur in the
evidence. Item (j) is the hypergeometric form used for the direct
inference in \cref{thm:p15-carnap-direct}, which Carnap derives from
T92-3 \cite[T92-3 and remarks, pp.~490--492; T94-1]{Carnap1950Probability}.

\begin{hbexample}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical-example}{Five individuals, two kinds}
Let the division be \(M_1,M_2\) and let \(e\) say that of
\(a_1,\ldots,a_5\) exactly three are \(M_1\) and two are \(M_2\). Take
\(i=M_1a_1\land M_2a_2\) (\(s=2\), \(s_1=s_2=1\)), so \(s'=3\),
\(s'_1=2\), \(s'_2=1\). By (i),
\[
 c(i,e)=\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{3}{1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{2}{1}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0pt}{}{5}{2}}
 =\frac{3\cdot2}{20}=\frac3{10},
\]
and by (j) the statistical hypothesis \(j\) (one of \(a_1,a_2\) is
\(M_1\), the other \(M_2\)) has \(c(j,e)=\binom31\binom21/\binom52=3/5\),
which is \(2\cdot3/10\) as (k) of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-t92-1}
requires. Both values hold for every symmetrical \(c\) and every
\(N\geq5\), and in \(\mathfrak L_\infty\). By
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-symmetrical}(3)(c),
\(c(M_1a_6,M_1a_1\land M_1a_2)=c(M_1a_9,M_1a_1\land M_1a_2)\), but
symmetry fixes no value for either: with one primitive predicate
\(P=M_1\) and \(N=3\), the corresponding values
\(c(Pa_3,Pa_1\land Pa_2)\) under \(c^*\) and \(c^\dagger\) of
\cref{def:p15-carnap-star} are \(3/4\) and \(1/2\)
(\cref{def:p15-carnap-three-states}), and both functions are
symmetrical.
\end{hbexample}

\begin{hbwarning}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-nonsymmetrical}{A regular function that discriminates}
In \(\mathfrak L_2\) with one primitive predicate \(P\), let \(m\) give
the states \(Pa_1\land Pa_2\), \(Pa_1\land\neg Pa_2\),
\(\neg Pa_1\land Pa_2\), \(\neg Pa_1\land\neg Pa_2\) the values
\(0.4\), \(0.3\), \(0.1\), \(0.2\). It is regular, but
\(m(Pa_1)=0.7\ne0.5=m(Pa_2)\), so invariance fails for the correlation
exchanging \(a_1\) and \(a_2\); it also fails for confirmation:
\(c(Pa_2,Pa_1)=4/7\) while \(c(Pa_1,Pa_2)=4/5\). Such functions satisfy
every theorem on regular functions of secs.~55--60, which is why Carnap
regards the regular theory alone as too weak and adds symmetry as a
further requirement.
\end{hbwarning}

\section{Zabell's history: probability, confirmation, exchangeability}
\label{sec:p15-w02b-p15-029-zabell}

Zabell's chapter writes Carnap's theory in event notation from a
Bayesian, subjective standpoint. Sentences are replaced by events, a
state description by an outcome sequence \(e_1,\ldots,e_n\) in
\(t\) categories, and a structure description by its counts
\(n_1,\ldots,n_t\) \cite[secs.~1, 4.2, pp.~265, 272]{Zabell2011CarnapInductiveInference}.
The held copy has the allocated pagination 265--309 and a 2009
copyright footer. The claims below are marked as historical,
philosophical, or mathematical.

\emph{Sec.~2.1 (historical).} Carnap introduced probability\(_1\) and
probability\(_2\) in 1945; the 1950 book assigns probabilities to
sentences; his later work uses events or propositions. Carnap's \(m\)
descends from the probability remarks of Wittgenstein and Waismann, and
\(c(h,e)=m(h\land e)/m(e)\). Hosiasson-Lindenbaum had studied
confirmation axiomatically: \(0\leq c\leq1\); invariance under
equivalence of either argument; \(c(h,e)=1\) if \(e\) implies \(h\);
additivity over hypotheses incompatible given \(e\); and
\(c(h\land h',e)=c(h,e)c(h',h\land e)\) (pp.~266--267).

\emph{Sec.~2.2 (historical and philosophical).} Ramsey derived utility
and probability from preferences; de Finetti took fair odds, with
coherence (no Dutch book) as the rationality constraint, and showed
that coherent odds obey the finitely additive axioms. Zabell credits
Kemeny (1955) with the converse: the five axioms are necessary and
sufficient for coherence. He reports that this persuaded Carnap to
adopt the betting interpretation, abandoning evidential support, one
of the three interpretations of 1950 (pp.~267--268). The 1950 betting
and estimate explanations are those of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-betting}. Zabell states Kemeny's result
without proof; the form below, for a finite system with the conditional
bets de Finetti used, and its proof are an independent reconstruction.
The five axioms are read with \(\leftrightarrow\) and \(\to\) as
L-equivalence and L-implication, and \(c\) is defined exactly on the
pairs whose evidence is not L-false.

\begin{hbdefinition}{def:p15-w02b-p15-029-coherence}{Conditional bets and coherence}
In \(\mathfrak L_N\), a \emph{conditional bet} on \(h\) given \(e\) at
quotient \(c(h,e)\) with stake \(S\in\mathbb R\) pays the bettor
\(S(1-c(h,e))\) in a state of \(R(e)\cap R(h)\), \(-Sc(h,e)\) in a
state of \(R(e)\setminus R(h)\), and nothing outside \(R(e)\), where the
bet is called off; a negative stake takes the other side. A \emph{book}
is a finite family of such bets on pairs \((h_k,e_k)\). The function
\(c\) is \emph{coherent} if no book has negative net gain in every
state in which at least one of its bets is not called off.
\end{hbdefinition}

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-kemeny}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-kemeny}{Coherence and the five axioms (de Finetti; Kemeny's converse)}
In \(\mathfrak L_N\), \(c\) is coherent if and only if it satisfies the
five axioms of confirmation of Zabell's sec.~2.1: (1)
\(0\leq c(h,e)\leq1\); (2) \(c(h,e)=c(h',e')\) if \(h,h'\) and
\(e,e'\) are L-equivalent; (3) \(c(h,e)=1\) if \(e\) L-implies \(h\);
(4) \(c(h\lor h',e)=c(h,e)+c(h',e)\) if \(e\) L-implies
\(\neg(h\land h')\); (5) \(c(h\land h',e)=c(h,e)\,c(h',h\land e)\) when
\(h\land e\) is not L-false.
\end{hbtheorem}

The direction from coherence to the axioms is the one Zabell attributes
to de Finetti; the converse is Kemeny's. In the proof the converse
rests on one fact: with \(E\) the disjunction of the conditions of a
book, the values \(c(z,E)\) on the states \(z\) of \(R(E)\) form a
probability under which every conditional bet has expected gain zero,
so no book can lose in every state.

\emph{Secs.~3--3.1 (mathematical and historical).} If trials are
conditionally independent with success probability \(p\) drawn from a
law \(\mu\) on \([0,1]\), the count \(S_n\) has the binomial mixture
law. The uniform \(\mu\) gives \(P(S_n=k)=1/(n+1)\); Bayes's
argument runs in the opposite direction, from that count law to the
uniform prior, by the uniqueness of a law on \([0,1]\) with given
moments; and the rule of succession follows (pp.~269--270). The
nineteenth-century criticism by Ellis, von Kries, Edgeworth, Venn,
Bertrand and Poincaré is reported as history.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-bayes-laplace}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-bayes-laplace}{Bayes's postulate and the rule of succession}
For an infinite exchangeable binary sequence the following are
equivalent: (i) \(P(S_n=k)=1/(n+1)\) for all \(n\geq1\) and
\(0\leq k\leq n\); (ii) the de Finetti mixing law of
\cref{thm:p15-binary-definetti} is uniform on \([0,1]\). Under them,
\(P(X_{n+1}=1\mid S_n=k)=(k+1)/(n+2)\).
\end{hbtheorem}

\emph{Sec.~4 (mathematical and historical).} Johnson (1924) allows
\(t\geq2\) categories (4.1), introduces the permutation postulate,
which is exchangeability (4.2), and the combination postulate, giving
all count vectors of length \(n\) equal probability (4.3). Exchangeability
replaces the analogy with drawing from an urn of nature: instances are
like draws to the extent that they are judged exchangeable. Under it,
\(P(n_1,\ldots,n_t)=\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_t!}P(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\)
and predictive probabilities depend only on the counts. With the
combination postulate one obtains \(m^*\) and \(c^*\) in the form
\((n_i+1)/(n+t)\) (pp.~271--274). The held copy prints the number of
count vectors as \(\binom{n+t-1}t\); the correct number is
\(\binom{n+t-1}{t-1}\) (for \(n=1\), \(t=2\) there are two), and
the formulas below use the correct value, which agrees with
\cref{thm:p15-carnap-language-counts}.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-johnson}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-johnson}{Permutation and combination postulates}
Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) take values in \(t\) categories with
exchangeable law. Then each count vector has probability
\(\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_t!}\) times that of any sequence with those
counts, and \(P(X_{n+1}=c_i\mid X_1=e_1,\ldots,X_n=e_n)\) depends
only on the counts whenever \(X_1,\ldots,X_{n+1}\) is exchangeable and
the conditioning sequence has positive probability. If moreover all count
vectors have probability \(1/\binom{n+t-1}{t-1}\) at every length, a
sequence with counts \((n_i)\) has probability
\(\prod_in_i!\,(t-1)!/(n+t-1)!\), and the predictive probability is
\((n_i+1)/(n+t)\).
\end{hbtheorem}

\emph{Sec.~5 (mathematical and historical).} Johnson's sufficientness
postulate, so named by Good, requires the predictive probability of
category \(i\) to be a function \(f_i(n_i,n)\) of its own count and
the sample size only.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-johnson-carnap}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-johnson-carnap}{The Johnson--Carnap continuum (Zabell, sec.~5.1)}
Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be an infinite exchangeable sequence with values
in \(t\geq2\) categories such that
\begin{enumerate}
 \item every finite sequence has positive probability;
 \item for every \(n\geq1\) there are functions \(f_i(\cdot,n)\) with
       \(P(X_{n+1}=c_i\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n)=f_i(n_i,n)\);
 \item either \(t\geq3\), or \(t=2\) and each \(f_i(\cdot,n)\) is
       affine: \(f_i(a,n)=g_i(n)+ad_i(n)\).
\end{enumerate}
Then either the \(X_j\) are independent with common law
\(P(X_1=c_i)=p_i>0\), or there are constants \(\alpha_i>0\), with
\(\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i\), such that for all \(n\geq0\)
\[
 P(X_{n+1}=c_i\mid n_1,\ldots,n_t)=\frac{n_i+\alpha_i}{n+\alpha}.
\]
Conversely each such law satisfies (1)--(3)
(\cref{thm:p15-carnap-history-family}).
\end{hbtheorem}

Zabell states the theorem with conditions (1)--(3) for \(t\geq3\) and
says the case \(t=2\) is recovered when sufficientness is replaced by
linearity in \(n_i\), citing his 1982 paper; he notes that with two
categories sufficientness alone is vacuous (pp.~274--275). The proof
in the annex is an independent reconstruction along Johnson's
algebraic route; it uses infinite extendability only to exclude
negative \(\alpha_i\), which is why the finite continuum of his
sec.~8.4 admits negative parameters. Carnap's \(c^*\) is
\(\alpha_i=1\).

\emph{Sec.~5.2 (mathematical).} For an infinitely exchangeable
\(t\)-valued sequence there is a probability \(\mu\) on the simplex
\(\Delta_t\) with
\(P(n_1,\ldots,n_t)=\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_t!}\int\prod_ip_i^{n_i}\,d\mu\)
for every \(n\); one \(\mu\) serves all \(n\), and no restriction is
placed on it (p.~275). This is the case of a finite state space in
\cref{thm:p15-definetti-kernel}, with \(\mu\) the mixing law; its
uniqueness is \cref{thm:p15-directing-law-uniqueness}. Zabell reads
Johnson's theorem as saying that under sufficientness the mixing law is
a Dirichlet law with parameters \(\alpha_j>0\), and quotes Johnson's
remark that his algebra replaces the gamma-function integrals; he notes
that a joint probability is the product of an initial probability and
successive rules of succession (p.~276). The identification is the
following theorem.

\begin{hbtheorem}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-dirichlet}{proved=annex:p15-w02b-p15-029-dirichlet}{The Dirichlet identification (Zabell, sec.~5.2)}
Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) be an infinite exchangeable sequence with values
in \(t\geq2\) categories whose predictive probabilities are
\((n_i+\alpha_i)/(n+\alpha)\) for all \(n\geq0\), with \(\alpha_i>0\)
and \(\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i\). Then
\begin{enumerate}
 \item every sequence with counts \(n_1,\ldots,n_t\) has probability
       \(\prod_i\alpha_i^{(n_i)}/\alpha^{(n)}\), where
       \(x^{(r)}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+r-1)\);
 \item \(\int_{\Delta_t}\prod_ip_i^{\beta_i-1}\,dp_1\cdots dp_{t-1}
       =\prod_i\Gamma(\beta_i)/\Gamma(\sum_i\beta_i)\) for all
       \(\beta_i>0\);
 \item the mixing law of the sequence is the Dirichlet law with density
       \(\Gamma(\alpha)\prod_ip_i^{\alpha_i-1}/\prod_i\Gamma(\alpha_i)\)
       with respect to \(dp_1\cdots dp_{t-1}\), and
       \(P(n_1,\ldots,n_t)=\frac{n!}{n_1!\cdots n_t!}\prod_i\alpha_i^{(n_i)}/\alpha^{(n)}\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{hbtheorem}

With \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-johnson-carnap}, the sequences with
positive probabilities and sufficientness (and, for \(t=2\), linearity)
are exactly the independent ones and the Dirichlet mixtures. For
\(c^*\), all \(\alpha_i=1\) and \(\mu\) is uniform on the simplex, the
\(t\)-category form of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-029-bayes-laplace}.

\emph{Secs.~5.3--5.4 (interpretive and historical).} For \(n>0\) the
continuum value is
\(\frac n{n+\alpha}\frac{n_i}n+\frac\alpha{n+\alpha}\frac{\alpha_i}\alpha\):
a weighted average of the observed frequency and the initial
probability \(\alpha_i/\alpha\), the weight on experience tending to one
as \(n\to\infty\) for every fixed \(\alpha\) (pp.~276--277). Johnson
treated the category-symmetric case \(\alpha_i=\alpha/t\); his proof,
published posthumously in 1932, had lacunae filled in Zabell's 1982
paper, which also treats the asymmetric case, \(t=\infty\), and finite
sequences. Carnap at first assumed both sufficientness and the
predictive form; Kemeny proved their equivalence for \(t>2\); Carnap
and Stegmüller treated \(t=2\); the category-asymmetric form appears
in Carnap's last work published by Jeffrey (1980) and in Kuipers (1978)
(p.~277).
