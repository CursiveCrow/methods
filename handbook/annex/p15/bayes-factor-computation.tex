\label{sec:annex-p15-bayes-factor-computation}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.laplace-relative-error}{thm:p15-laplace-relative-error}{Localization, odd-term cancellation and the tail bound}
\begin{proof}
All constants below can depend on the fixed bounds and \(d\).
The local amplitude is bounded by \(a_++A\rho\). Local quadratic
domination and the tail hypothesis make the integral finite. Continuity
and \(a_n(\theta_n)>0\) make it positive.

Put \(u=\sqrt n(\theta-\theta_n)\) and \(b_n=n^{1/12}\).
For large \(n\), the ball \(\|u\|\leq b_n\) lies in the permitted
neighborhood. Taylor's theorem with its bounded fourth derivative gives
\[
 n(h_n(\theta_n+u/\sqrt n)-h_n(\theta_n))
 =-\tfrac12u^TJ_nu+n^{-1/2}T_n(u)+R_n(u),
\]
where \(T_n(u)=D^3h_n(\theta_n)[u,u,u]/6\) is odd,
\(|T_n(u)|\leq A\|u\|^3/6\), and
\(|R_n(u)|\leq A\|u\|^4/(24n)\).
Similarly
\[
 a_n(\theta_n+u/\sqrt n)=a_n(\theta_n)
       +n^{-1/2}Da_n(\theta_n)[u]+O(n^{-1}\|u\|^2).
\]
On this ball the absolute exponent perturbation is at most
\(C(n^{-1/2}b_n^3+n^{-1}b_n^4)\), tending to zero.
The elementary Taylor estimate
\(|e^t-1-t|\leq e\,t^2/2\) for \(|t|\leq1\) follows from the
second-derivative remainder. It shows that the product of the amplitude
and perturbed exponential, after removing \(e^{-u^TJ_nu/2}\), equals
\[
 a_n(\theta_n)+n^{-1/2}
 \{a_n(\theta_n)T_n(u)+Da_n(\theta_n)[u]\}
             +O\{n^{-1}(1+\|u\|^{10})\}.
\]
The constants are uniform. The order-ten polynomial is a convenient bound
for all products of the displayed remainders, not an omitted smoothness
assumption.

The first-order term is odd. Its integral against the even Gaussian on
the centered ball is exactly zero. Every fixed polynomial moment of
\(e^{-c\|u\|^2/2}\) is finite: a polynomial times
\(e^{-c\|u\|^2/4}\) is bounded, leaving an integrable product Gaussian.
Also, for fixed polynomial \(P\),
\[
 \int_{\|u\|>b}|P(u)|e^{-c\|u\|^2/2}du
 \leq e^{-cb^2/4}\int |P(u)|e^{-c\|u\|^2/4}du.
\]
Thus replacing the small ball's Gaussian integral by the full integral
changes it by \(o(n^{-1})\).

For clarity the full Gaussian integral is
\((2\pi)^{d/2}/\sqrt{\det J_n}\). This follows without an unstated
multivariate Jacobian: partition a positive definite matrix as
\(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b^T\\b&C\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).
Complete the square in the first coordinate. Integration gives
\(\sqrt{2\pi/a}\), leaving the positive definite Schur complement
\(C-bb^T/a\), with determinant \(\det J/a\). Induction and the
one-dimensional Gaussian integral prove the formula. Tonelli justifies
the integrations.

Between \(b_n\) and \(\rho\sqrt n\), local quadratic domination bounds
the rescaled integrand by
\((a_++A\rho)e^{-c_0\|u\|^2}\); its integral is \(o(n^{-1})\).
Outside the fixed local ball, the rescaled normalized integral is at most
\(n^{d/2}A_Te^{-\eta n}=o(n^{-1})\). Combining these regions gives
\[
 n^{d/2}e^{-nh_n(\theta_n)}I_n
 =a_n(\theta_n)(2\pi)^{d/2}(\det J_n)^{-1/2}+O(n^{-1}).
\]
The main term is bounded below by
\(a_-(2\pi)^{d/2}C^{-d/2}>0\), proving relative accuracy.
For two models write \(I_k=\widehat I_k(1+\varepsilon_k)\),
\(\varepsilon_k=O(n^{-1})\). Dividing yields the factor assertion.
For large \(n\), \(|\varepsilon_k|\leq1/2\), and
\(|\log(1+\varepsilon_k)|\leq2|\varepsilon_k|\), proving the log assertion.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.laplace-mode-variants}{thm:p15-laplace-mode-variants}{Substituting the two exponent/amplitude choices}
\begin{proof}
For the posterior choice, \(e^{nh_n}=L_n\pi\), amplitude one, and
\(J_n=H_n/n\). Thus
\(n^{-d/2}(\det J_n)^{-1/2}=(\det H_n)^{-1/2}\).
Substitution into the preceding theorem gives the posterior-mode formula
and its relative error. For the likelihood choice,
\(e^{nh_n}=L_n\), amplitude \(\pi\), and \(J_n=F_n/n\).
The same determinant identity gives the MLE formula. Positivity,
smoothness, local domination and global tails must hold for the chosen
decomposition; they are not transferred from one decomposition by assertion.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.laplace-curvature-replacement}{thm:p15-laplace-curvature-replacement}{A quantitative determinant comparison}
\begin{proof}
Let \(E=J_n^{-1/2}(\widetilde J_n-J_n)J_n^{-1/2}\).
Its operator norm is at most \(\delta_n/c<1/2\). Its real eigenvalues
\(\epsilon_j\) therefore lie in that interval, and
\(\det\widetilde J_n/\det J_n=\prod_j(1+\epsilon_j)>0\).
Since \(|\log(1+t)|\leq2|t|\) for \(|t|<1/2\),
\[
 \left|\tfrac12\log\frac{\det J_n}{\det\widetilde J_n}\right|
 \leq\sum_j|\epsilon_j|\leq d\delta_n/c.
\]
Only this determinant changes in the specified approximation, proving the
bound. Exponentiating an \(O(\delta_n)\) log difference gives
\(1+O(\delta_n)\), and multiplication by the already proved
\(1+O(n^{-1})\) approximation yields the stated orders.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.bic-leading-order}{thm:p15-bic-leading-order}{The exact omitted order in the logarithm}
\begin{proof}
Take logarithms of the MLE approximation and use
\(\det F_{k,n}=n^{d_k}\det J_{k,n}\) and the relative-error theorem.
This gives the displayed expression for \(\log I_{k,n}\). Uniform
eigenvalue bounds bound \(\log\det J_{k,n}\); the upper/lower prior
ordinate bounds bound its logarithm. Hence \(c_{k,n}=O(1)\).
Subtract the two expansions to obtain the BIC relation. Multiplying
the unshortened relation by two gives coefficient two on both prior-log
terms, as displayed in the body.

Let \(S_n=-\tfrac12(\mathrm{BIC}_1-\mathrm{BIC}_0)\).
Then \(\log B_{10}=S_n+c_{1,n}-c_{0,n}+O(n^{-1})\), so
\(e^{S_n}/B_{10}\to e^{-c_*}\) when the constant difference converges.
If \(|\log B_{10}|\to\infty\), dividing the bounded additive difference
by \(\log B_{10}\) gives zero. A bounded denominator supplies no such
conclusion. Parameter-free positive likelihoods need no approximation
and contribute no determinant or parameter-prior term.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.laplace-normal-example}{thm:p15-laplace-normal-example}{Exact normal evidence and failed hypotheses}
\begin{proof}
Write \(s^2=\sigma^2/n\). The completed-square calculation in
\cref{thm:p15-normal-point-null-factor} gives
\(I_n=\phi_{\mu,s^2+\tau^2}(x_n)\) and a product likelihood/prior
whose log is quadratic, with mode
\[
 \widetilde\theta_n=\frac{\tau^2x_n+s^2\mu}{s^2+\tau^2},
 \qquad H_n=1/s^2+1/\tau^2.
\]
Thus \(L_n(\theta)\pi(\theta)\) is exactly \(I_n\) times the normal
density with this mean and precision. Its value at the mode times
\(\sqrt{2\pi/H_n}\) is exactly \(I_n\).
The MLE is \(x_n\), with \(F_n=1/s^2\), and
\(\sqrt{2\pi s^2}L_n(x_n)\pi(x_n)=\pi(x_n)\).
Dividing this normal density by the exact convolution gives the ratio
in the theorem.

For bounded \(x_n\), the prior ordinate at the MLE is bounded away from
zero. Gaussian prior first and second derivatives are uniformly bounded.
Both exponents have zero third and fourth derivatives and curvatures
at least \(1/\sigma^2\) after division by \(n\).
Outside a fixed radius \(\rho\), their normalized Gaussian integrands
are bounded by a constant times
\(e^{-n\rho^2/(4\sigma^2)}e^{-n v^2/(4\sigma^2)}\).
The latter factor integrates to a bounded value for \(n\geq1\), proving
the required exponential-tail bound; the prior amplitude is bounded in
the MLE case. This verifies every Laplace hypothesis.
The logarithm of the explicit MLE ratio is \(O(s^2)=O(n^{-1})\)
for bounded \(x_n\), giving its claimed relative rate.

At \(x_n=\mu=0,\sigma^2=1,\tau^2=4\), divide
\(I_n=1/\sqrt{2\pi(4+1/n)}\) by the null density
\(\sqrt{n/(2\pi)}\). This gives \(1/\sqrt{1+4n}\).
The MLE approximation uses prior ordinate \(1/(2\sqrt{2\pi})\),
giving \(1/(2\sqrt n)\). The maximized alternative likelihood equals
the null likelihood here, so the one-parameter BIC penalty gives
\(1/\sqrt n\). Its ratio to the exact factor tends to two.

For the boundary case, substitution \(u=\sqrt n\theta\) yields
\[
 I_n=n^{-1/2}\int_0^{\sqrt n}e^{-u^2/2}du
 \sim\tfrac12\sqrt{2\pi/n}.
\]
There is no two-sided interior ball at zero. For the two-mode example,
the prior density is \(1/3\) on a total length three; symmetry gives
\[
 I_n=\frac23\int_{1/2}^{2}e^{-n(\theta-1)^2/2}d\theta
 =\frac2{3\sqrt n}\int_{-\sqrt n/2}^{\sqrt n}e^{-u^2/2}du
 \sim\frac23\sqrt{2\pi/n}.
\]
One mode's local contribution is only one half of this, and the other
mode violates the exponentially negligible outside-tail condition.
For the quartic case, symmetry and \(u=n^{1/4}\theta\) give
\[
 I_n=n^{-1/4}\int_0^{n^{1/4}}e^{-u^4}du.
\]
The limiting integral is positive and finite: above one,
\(e^{-u^4}\leq e^{-u}\). The Hessian at zero vanishes, so the positive
curvature premise fails. Finally, each exponential for one trial is in
\((0,1]\); taking it as a Bernoulli success probability and observing
\(n\) successes constructs the stated likelihoods under proper priors.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.direct-evidence-mc}{thm:p15-direct-evidence-mc}{The integrable prior average}
\begin{proof}
Since \(L\geq0\) and \(\int Ld\Pi=I<\infty\), the sampled likelihoods
are integrable identically distributed variables. Linearity gives
\(E\widehat I_M=I\). The i.i.d. sampling meets the stronger-than-needed
independence hypothesis of \cref{thm:p15-etemadi-strong-law}, so the
average converges almost surely to \(I\). If the second moment is finite,
independence eliminates cross-covariances, giving
\(\operatorname{Var}(\widehat I_M)=\operatorname{Var}_\Pi(L)/M\).
No finite second moment was used for almost-sure convergence.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.importance-evidence}{thm:p15-importance-evidence}{Domination and two different denominators}
\begin{proof}
Radon--Nikodym change of measure gives
\(\int V\,dQ=T(\Theta)=I\). Set \(V=0\) on its null exceptional set
to make it finite everywhere. The sampled variables are integrable
i.i.d.; the same strong law and variance calculation prove the ordinary
importance assertions.

If \(\Pi\ll Q\), choose \(W=d\Pi/dQ\), likewise finite everywhere
after a null-set assignment. Its expectation under \(Q\) is one, and
change of measure gives \(E_Q[WL]=I\), with \(WL\geq0\) integrable.
The two strong laws hold on a common probability-one event:
\[
 M^{-1}\sum_iW_i\to1,\qquad M^{-1}\sum_iW_iL_i\to I.
\]
The denominator is eventually positive and their ratio tends to \(I\).
If \(\pi,q\) are densities, \(\pi/q\) on \(q>0\), extended by zero
on the \(Q\)-null set, is the derivative when the stated domination
holds. The finite bias counterexample is proved in the boundary item.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.harmonic-evidence}{thm:p15-harmonic-evidence}{What posterior reciprocal averages estimate}
\begin{proof}
Since \(P=T/I\), integration of \(H=dG/dT\) gives
\(E_PH=I^{-1}\int H\,dT=1/I\). Squaring gives the stated second
moment, possibly infinite. Choose a finite version outside a \(P\)-null
set. Independent posterior draws therefore produce integrable i.i.d.
weights. The strong law gives \(A_M\to1/I>0\), so eventually the
mean is positive and its inverse tends to \(I\).
Linearity makes \(A_M\) unbiased for \(1/I\); it does not make its
nonlinear inverse unbiased.

The relation \(\Pi\ll T\) holds exactly when \(L>0\) \(\Pi\)-almost
everywhere. Indeed \(T\) vanishes on \(\{L=0\}\); on the positive set,
\(T(A)=0\) implies \(\Pi(A\cap\{L\geq1/j\})=0\) for every positive
integer \(j\), hence \(\Pi(A\cap\{L>0\})=0\). Under this relation,
\(d\Pi/dT=1/L\), and
\(E_P(1/L)^2=I^{-1}\int L^{-1}d\Pi\).
If instead \(s=\Pi(L>0)<1\), posterior draws still lie in \(\{L>0\}\)
almost surely, but
\[
 E_P(1/L)=\frac1I\int_{\{L>0\}}d\Pi=s/I.
\]
Here \(s>0\) because \(I>0\); the strong law gives the stated wrong
reciprocal limit \(I/s\). With densities \(g,h\), domination requires
\(g=0\) almost everywhere on \(h=0\), and \(H=g/h\) on \(h>0\).
Substitution gives \(\int H^2dT=\int g^2/h\), proving the explicit
variance requirement.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.evidence-mc-clt}{thm:p15-evidence-mc-clt}{The i.i.d. central limit theorem and denominator limits}
\begin{proof}
For an ordinary mean, apply \cref{thm:p15-clt-iid} to the integrable
i.i.d. weights with finite variance, including its zero-variance case.
For self-normalized importance put \(U_i=W_i(L_i-I)\). Its mean is
\(I-I=0\), and the assumed second moment is \(v_{\rm SN}\).
Where \(D_M=M^{-1}\sum_iW_i>0\),
\[
 \sqrt M(\widetilde I_M-I)=\frac{M^{-1/2}\sum_iU_i}{D_M}.
\]
The numerator has the asserted normal limit and \(D_M\to1\) almost
surely. The random-denominator result \cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation}
gives the limit without assuming independence between numerator and
denominator. For harmonic means apply the same CLT to
\(H_i-1/I\), and use the exact identity
\[
 \sqrt M(A_M^{-1}-I)=-\frac{I}{A_M}\sqrt M(A_M-1/I).
\]
Here \(I/A_M\to I^2\) in probability, so the same perturbation theorem
gives variance \(I^4v_H\). Positive limiting denominators imply that
zero-denominator events have probability tending to zero; a fixed default
on those events does not change the weak limits.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15.mc-evidence-boundaries}{thm:p15-mc-evidence-boundaries}{Support, reciprocal bias, infinite variance and dependence}
\begin{proof}
For the first example, direct likelihood draws are Bernoulli with mean
\(\varepsilon\) and variance \(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\).
Dividing the variance of their average by \(I^2\) gives
\((1-\varepsilon)/(M\varepsilon)\). Independence gives probability
\((1-\varepsilon)^M\) of all zero draws. The posterior is the first
point mass, where \(L=1\), so its harmonic estimate equals one.
The target measure \(T\) has mass \(\varepsilon\) on that point;
relative to \(Q\), its derivative is constant \(\varepsilon\), giving
ordinary importance with zero variance. However \(\Pi\not\ll Q\);
the attempted prior weight on the sampled point is \(\varepsilon\),
and self-normalization divides \(\varepsilon\) by itself.

In the second example \(I=(1+3)/2=2\). Proposal weights are \(2/3,2\),
and weighted likelihoods \(2/3,6\). Thus their expectation is 2 and
their second moment is
\((3/4)(4/9)+(1/4)36=28/3\), giving variance \(16/3\).
For one draw self-normalization returns \(L\), whose proposal mean is
\((3/4)1+(1/4)3=3/2\). The posterior is \((1/4,3/4)\);
its one-draw harmonic estimate also returns \(L\), now with expectation
\(1/4+9/4=5/2\). Its reciprocal has expectation
\(1/4+(3/4)(1/3)=1/2\).
To realize these likelihood values with normalized data laws, take the
two data models uniform on \([0,1]\) and \([0,1/3]\), respectively,
and observe an interior point common to both supports.

For the third example the normal density ratio is
\(W(\theta)=\tfrac12 e^{3\theta^2/2}\).
Both densities have full support and \(E_QW=1\), but \(W^2q\)
is a positive constant times \(e^{\theta^2}\), whose integral diverges.
A likelihood identically one can be the probability mass of a one-point
observation space, so this is a proper evidence problem.

For the fourth example, \(\int L^{-1}d\Pi\) is a positive constant times
\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}
 \exp\!\left\{\left(\frac1{2s^2}-\frac1{2\tau^2}\right)\theta^2
                       -\frac{x}{s^2}\theta+\frac{x^2}{2s^2}\right\}d\theta.
\]
If \(\tau^2>s^2\), the positive quadratic term makes it divergent.
At equality, a nonzero linear term diverges along one tail, while a zero
linear term leaves a positive constant over the line. For \(\tau^2<s^2\)
square completion gives a finite Gaussian integral. Evidence itself is
the positive finite normal convolution, so the reciprocal first moment
is finite in every case; only its second moment fails at the stated
threshold.

Finally, the identity transition kernel keeps its initialized posterior
state fixed and is stationary. The harmonic average is then \(1/L\) of
that same state at every simulation count, with inverse \(1/4\) or \(3/4\).
The true evidence is \((1/4+3/4)/2=1/2\). This proves failure of
convergence despite correct posterior marginals and identifies the missing
averaging assumption, without asserting a general MCMC theorem.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-infobound}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-information-replacement-boundary}{Logistic data with observed and expected information}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Logistic facts.} Here \(\log f(u)=-u-2\log(1+e^{-u})\), so
\((\log f)'(u)=-\tanh(u/2)\). Since
\(\operatorname{sech}^2(u/2)=4e^{-u}/(1+e^{-u})^2\),
\[
 (\log f)''(u)=-\tfrac12\operatorname{sech}^2(u/2)=-2f(u)<0 .
\] Also
\(f(u)=\tfrac14\operatorname{sech}^2(u/2)\) is even and decreasing in
\(|u|\), with \(f(0)=1/4\) and \(f(u)\to0\) as \(|u|\to\infty\). Every
derivative of \(\tanh\) is a polynomial in \(\tanh\), so every derivative
of \(\log f\) is bounded on \(\mathbb R\). For a location family the
information per observation at \(\theta\) is
\(E[-(\log f)''(X-\theta)]=2\int f^2\). The substitution
\(t=1/(1+e^{-u})\) has \(dt=f(u)\,du\) and \(f(u)=t(1-t)\), so
\(\int f^2\,du=\int_0^1t(1-t)\,dt=1/6\). The expected information per
observation is therefore \(1/3\) at every \(\theta\).

\emph{First item.} For \(m\) observations at each of \(\pm a\),
\(h_n(\theta)=\tfrac12\{\log f(a-\theta)+\log f(-a-\theta)\}\). Each
summand has second derivative \(-2f<0\), so \(h_n\) is strictly concave.
Also \(h_n'(\theta)=\tfrac12\{\tanh((a-\theta)/2)+\tanh((-a-\theta)/2)\}\)
vanishes at \(\theta=0\) because \(\tanh\) is odd. Hence
\(\widehat\theta_n=0\) is the unique maximizer, and
\(J_n=-h_n''(0)=f(a)+f(-a)=2f(a)\).

\emph{The Laplace hypotheses for \(0\leq a\leq\bar a\).} Take
\(\rho=1\), \(\Theta_n=\mathbb R\), and amplitude the standard normal
density \(\varphi\). The gradient vanishes at zero, and
\(J_n=2f(a)\in[2f(\bar a),1/2]\). The third and fourth derivatives of
\(h_n\) are averages of derivatives of \(\log f\), hence bounded. The
derivatives \(\varphi'\) and \(\varphi''\) are bounded, and
\(\varphi(0)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\). For \(|v|\leq1\),
\(-h_n''(v)=f(a-v)+f(a+v)\geq2f(\bar a+1)\), because
\(|a\pm v|\leq\bar a+1\). Taylor's theorem with \(h_n'(0)=0\) gives
\(h_n(v)-h_n(0)\leq-c_0v^2\) with \(c_0=f(\bar a+1)\). For
\(|\theta|\geq1\), concavity of \(t\mapsto h_n(t\theta/|\theta|)\)
gives
\(h_n(\theta)-h_n(0)\leq|\theta|\{h_n(\theta/|\theta|)-h_n(0)\}\leq-c_0|\theta|\).
Hence
\[
 \int_{|\theta|\geq1}e^{n(h_n(\theta)-h_n(0))}\varphi(\theta)\,d\theta
 \leq\frac2{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_1^\infty e^{-nc_0t}\,dt
 \leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,c_0}e^{-c_0n}.
\]
All the conditions of \cref{def:p15-laplace-family} hold with constants
depending only on \(\bar a\), so \cref{thm:p15-laplace-mode-variants}
gives \(I_n/\widehat I(F_n)=1+O(n^{-1})\). In the approximation only the
determinant changes when \(F_n\) is replaced by \(\widetilde F_n\), so
\(\widehat I(\widetilde F_n)/\widehat I(F_n)=(J_n/\widetilde J_n)^{1/2}\).

\emph{Second item.} The equation \(f(a)=1/6+n^{-1/2}/2\) has a solution
in \([0,\infty)\) exactly when \(1/6+n^{-1/2}/2\leq1/4\), that is when
\(n\geq36\), and the solution is unique by monotonicity. Since
\(f(a_n)>1/6=f(a^*)\) with \(a^*=\log(2+\sqrt3)\), it satisfies
\(a_n<a^*\). Here \(e^{-a^*}=2-\sqrt3\) is the root below one of
\(t^2-4t+1=0\), which is the equation \(t/(1+t)^2=1/6\). Take
\(\bar a=a^*\). Then \(J_n=1/3+n^{-1/2}\), and for \(n\geq38\),
\(\delta_n=n^{-1/2}\leq38^{-1/2}<1/6=c/2\). Thus
\[
 \log\frac{\widehat I(\widetilde F_n)}{I_n}
 =\tfrac12\log(3J_n)-\log\frac{I_n}{\widehat I(F_n)}
 =\tfrac12\log(1+3n^{-1/2})+O(n^{-1}).
\]
Write \(x=n^{-1/2}\). Then
\((1+3x)^{1/2}-1=3x/\{(1+3x)^{1/2}+1\}\), so
\(\sqrt n\{(1+3x)^{1/2}-1\}\to3/2\). Multiplying by \(1+O(n^{-1})\)
changes the scaled quantity by \(O(n^{-1/2})\), which proves the stated
limit.

\emph{Third item.} With \(t=e^{-a}\), \(f(a)=1/24\) is the equation
\(t^2-22t+1=0\). Its root in \((0,1)\) is \(t=11-\sqrt{120}\), so
\(a=-\log(11-\sqrt{120})=\log(11+\sqrt{120})\), because the product of
the two roots is one. Then \(J_n=1/12\) and
\(\|\widetilde J_n-J_n\|=1/3-1/12=1/4\). Any eigenvalue bound \(c\)
satisfies \(c\leq1/12\), so \(c/2\leq1/24<1/4\). With \(\bar a=a\), the
Laplace hypotheses hold as shown above, and
\(\widehat I(\widetilde F_n)/I_n=(1/4)^{1/2}(1+O(n^{-1}))\to1/2\).
The logistic density is positive everywhere, so these observations are
possible under every \(\theta\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-newton}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-one-step-newton}{Expansion identity and error of the one-step formula}
\begin{proof}
\emph{First item.} Put \(u=\bar\theta-\widehat\theta=(F+G)^{-1}\lambda'\),
writing \(\lambda'=\lambda'(\widehat\theta)\). Then
\(\ell^{(2)}(\bar\theta)=\ell(\widehat\theta)-\tfrac12u^TFu\) and
\(\lambda^{(1)}(\bar\theta)=\lambda(\widehat\theta)+\lambda'^Tu\).
Because \((F+G)^{-1}\) is symmetric,
\[
 2\lambda'^Tu-u^TFu
 =\lambda'^T(F+G)^{-1}\{2-F(F+G)^{-1}\}\lambda' .
\]
Adding \(2\ell(\widehat\theta)+2\lambda(\widehat\theta)+d\log(2\pi)-\log|F+G|\)
gives \(2\ell(\widehat\theta)+E\), as claimed. Formula (4) is
\((2\pi)^{d/2}|\widetilde\Sigma|^{1/2}\) times the likelihood and prior
at the posterior mode. Twice its logarithm, with the three stated
replacements, is the right side.

\emph{Second item.} Here \(\lambda'=D\pi/\pi\) at the mode. The amplitude
bounds give \(\|\lambda'\|\leq A/a_-\). The matrix \(G=W^{-1}\) is
positive definite, so \(F+G\succeq F\succeq ncI\). Hence
\(\|(F+G)^{-1}\|\leq1/(nc)\). Since \(\|F\|\leq nC\),
\(\|2I-F(F+G)^{-1}\|\leq2+C/c\). The quadratic term of \(E\) is
therefore at most \((A/a_-)^2(2+C/c)/(nc)\) in absolute value. Next,
\(\log|F+G|-\log|F|=\log\det(I+M)\) with
\(M=F^{-1/2}GF^{-1/2}\) symmetric, positive semidefinite and of norm at
most \(g/(nc)\). Its eigenvalues \(\mu_j\) lie in \([0,g/(nc)]\), and
\(0\leq\log(1+\mu_j)\leq\mu_j\), so this difference lies in
\([0,dg/(nc)]\). With
\(2\log\widehat I_{\rm MLE}=2\ell(\widehat\theta)+2\lambda(\widehat\theta)+d\log(2\pi)-\log|F|\),
the two bounds give
\(2\ell(\widehat\theta)+E-2\log\widehat I_{\rm MLE}=O(n^{-1})\).
\Cref{thm:p15-laplace-mode-variants} gives
\(2\log I_n-2\log\widehat I_{\rm MLE}=O(n^{-1})\). Adding the two
proves the claim for one model. Subtracting the claims for the two
models, \(2\ell_1(\widehat\theta_1)-2\ell_0(\widehat\theta_0)=\Lambda\),
gives the factor statement.

\emph{Third item.} Here \(\ell_n(\theta)=\log\phi_{\theta,s^2}(x_n)\)
is quadratic, with \(\widehat\theta=x_n\) and \(F=1/s^2\). The prior is
\(\lambda=\log\phi_{\mu,\tau^2}\), with \(G=1/\tau^2\) and
\(\lambda'(x_n)=-D_n/\tau^2\). Since \(\ell=\ell^{(2)}\) and
\(\lambda=\lambda^{(1)}-\tfrac12G(\theta-\widehat\theta)^2\), the
first item gives
\[
 \ell(\widehat\theta)+\tfrac12E
 =\ell(\bar\theta)+\lambda(\bar\theta)+\tfrac12Gu^2
 +\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log(F+G).
\]
The log posterior kernel \(\ell+\lambda\) is quadratic with curvature
\(F+G\), so one Newton step from any point lands at its maximizer, and
\(\bar\theta=\widetilde\theta_n\). The posterior-mode calculation is exact
by \cref{thm:p15-laplace-normal-example}, so the terms other than
\(\tfrac12Gu^2\) sum to \(\log I_n\). Finally
\(u=-(D_n/\tau^2)s^2\tau^2/(s^2+\tau^2)=-D_ns^2/(s^2+\tau^2)\), and
\(\tfrac12Gu^2=D_n^2s^4/\{2\tau^2(s^2+\tau^2)^2\}\). Since
\(s^4=\sigma^4/n^2\), this is \(O(n^{-2})\) for bounded \(D_n\). The
expression for approximation (5) is the logarithm of the ratio in
\cref{thm:p15-laplace-normal-example}. At \(D_n=0\) it equals
\(\tfrac12\log\{1+\sigma^2/(n\tau^2)\}\), and
\(n\) times this tends to \(\sigma^2/(2\tau^2)>0\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-ghexact}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-gauss-hermite-exactness}{Orthogonality, division and reflection}
\begin{proof}
Write \(m_j=\int_{\mathbb R}x^je^{-x^2/2}\,dx\). By
\cref{thm:p15-criteria-moment-identities}, applied to a standard normal
variable whose density is \(e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\), every absolute
moment is finite, \(m_j=0\) for odd \(j\), and
\(m_{2j}=(2j-1)!!\sqrt{2\pi}\). Thus \(m_0=m_2=\sqrt{2\pi}\),
\(m_4=3\sqrt{2\pi}\) and \(m_6=15\sqrt{2\pi}\).

\emph{Orthogonal polynomials.} All products of polynomials are integrable
against \(e^{-x^2/2}\). A nonzero polynomial vanishes at finitely many
points, so \(\langle P,P\rangle>0\) for \(P\neq0\), and
\(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\) is an inner product on polynomials. The
Gram--Schmidt process applied to \(1,x,\ldots,x^K\) yields monic
\(\mathrm{He}_k\) of degree \(k\), each orthogonal to
\(\mathrm{He}_0,\ldots,\mathrm{He}_{k-1}\), and hence to every polynomial
of degree less than \(k\), since those span that space. Such a polynomial
is unique: if \(P\) is monic of degree \(k\) and orthogonal to all
polynomials of lower degree, then \(P-\mathrm{He}_k\) has degree less than
\(k\) and is orthogonal to itself, so it is zero. The four displayed
polynomials are monic of the stated degrees. Against lower monomials of
opposite parity their integrals are integrals of odd polynomials, hence
zero. The remaining products give \(\langle x^2-1,1\rangle=m_2-m_0=0\),
\(\langle x^3-3x,x\rangle=m_4-3m_2=0\),
\(\langle x^4-6x^2+3,1\rangle=m_4-6m_2+3m_0=0\) and
\(\langle x^4-6x^2+3,x^2\rangle=m_6-6m_4+3m_2=0\). By uniqueness they are
\(\mathrm{He}_1,\ldots,\mathrm{He}_4\).

\emph{Zeros.} Let \(y_1<\cdots<y_s\) be the real points at which
\(\mathrm{He}_K\) changes sign, and suppose \(s<K\). The polynomial
\(u(x)=\prod_{i\leq s}(x-y_i)\), with \(u=1\) when \(s=0\), has degree
\(s<K\), so \(\langle\mathrm{He}_K,u\rangle=0\). But
\(\mathrm{He}_Ku\) has one sign off a finite set and is not identically
zero, so its integral against \(e^{-x^2/2}\) is not zero. Hence
\(s\geq K\). A polynomial of degree \(K\) has at most \(K\) zeros, so
\(s=K\), and these are all the zeros, each simple.

\emph{Exactness.} Let \(P\) have degree at most \(2K-1\). Division gives
\(P=\mathrm{He}_Ku+v\) with \(u\) and \(v\) of degree at most \(K-1\).
Then \(\int Pe^{-x^2/2}\,dx=\langle\mathrm{He}_K,u\rangle
+\int ve^{-x^2/2}\,dx=\int ve^{-x^2/2}\,dx\). The polynomials \(v\) and
\(\sum_kv(x_k)\ell_k\) have degree at most \(K-1\) and agree at the \(K\)
nodes, so they are equal, and
\(\int ve^{-x^2/2}\,dx=\sum_k\omega_kv(x_k)\). Since
\(\mathrm{He}_K(x_k)=0\), \(v(x_k)=P(x_k)\). For \(P=\mathrm{He}_K^2\) the
rule gives \(0\) and the integral is \(\langle\mathrm{He}_K,\mathrm{He}_K\rangle>0\).

\emph{Positivity.} \(\ell_k^2\) has degree \(2K-2\leq2K-1\), and
\(\ell_k(x_j)^2\) is \(1\) for \(j=k\) and \(0\) otherwise. Exactness gives
\(\omega_k=\sum_j\omega_j\ell_k(x_j)^2=\int\ell_k^2e^{-x^2/2}\,dx>0\).
With \(P=1\), \(\sum_k\omega_k=m_0=\sqrt{2\pi}\).

\emph{Reflection.} The polynomial \((-1)^K\mathrm{He}_K(-x)\) is monic of
degree \(K\). For \(u\) of degree less than \(K\), the substitution
\(x\mapsto-x\) and the evenness of \(e^{-x^2/2}\) give
\[
 \langle\mathrm{He}_K(-\cdot),u\rangle=\langle\mathrm{He}_K,u(-\cdot)\rangle=0 .
\]
By uniqueness \(\mathrm{He}_K(-x)=(-1)^K\mathrm{He}_K(x)\), so the node set
is symmetric and, in increasing order, \(x_{K+1-k}=-x_k\). The polynomial
\(\ell_k(-x)\) has degree \(K-1\), equals \(1\) at \(-x_k\) and vanishes at
the other nodes, so it is the Lagrange polynomial of \(x_{K+1-k}\). The
same substitution gives \(\omega_{K+1-k}=\omega_k\).

\emph{Product rule.} For a monomial \(z^\beta=\prod_iz_i^{\beta_i}\) with
every \(\beta_i\leq2K-1\),
\[
 \mathcal R_K(z^\beta)=\sum_{\mathbf k}\prod_{i=1}^d\omega_{k_i}x_{k_i}^{\beta_i}
 =\prod_{i=1}^d\sum_{k=1}^K\omega_kx_k^{\beta_i}
 =\prod_{i=1}^dm_{\beta_i}=\mathcal G(z^\beta),
\]
the last step by Fubini's theorem, since
\(|z^\beta|e^{-\|z\|^2/2}\) is a product of integrable one-variable
functions. Linearity gives the claim for every polynomial of degree at
most \(2K-1\) in each variable. A polynomial of total degree at most
\(2K-1\) is such a polynomial. For the odd case, the map
\(\mathbf k\mapsto\mathbf k'\) with \(k'_i=K+1-k_i\) is a bijection of the
index set with \(z_{\mathbf k'}=-z_{\mathbf k}\) and
\(\Omega_{\mathbf k'}=\Omega_{\mathbf k}\). Reindexing gives
\(\mathcal R_K(P)=\sum_{\mathbf k}\Omega_{\mathbf k}P(-z_{\mathbf k})
=-\mathcal R_K(P)\), so \(\mathcal R_K(P)=0\). The substitution
\(z\mapsto-z\) gives \(\mathcal G(P)=-\mathcal G(P)=0\).

\emph{Small rules.} For \(K=1\), the node is the zero of \(x\), and
\(\ell_1=1\) gives \(\omega_1=m_0\). For \(K=2\) the nodes are \(\pm1\),
and by reflection and \(\sum_k\omega_k=m_0\) each weight is \(m_0/2\). For
\(K=3\) the nodes are \(0,\pm\sqrt3\). Reflection gives
\(\omega_1=\omega_3\), and exactness for \(x^2\) gives
\(2\omega_1\cdot3=m_2=\sqrt{2\pi}\). So \(\omega_1=\sqrt{2\pi}/6\) and
\(\omega_2=\sqrt{2\pi}-2\omega_1=2\sqrt{2\pi}/3\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-agq}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-adaptive-quadrature}{A common expansion for the integral and the rule}
\begin{proof}
Put \(N_n=n^{d/2}(\det J_n)^{1/2}e^{-nh_n(\theta_n)}\). The substitution
\(\theta=\theta_n(z)\) has Jacobian
\(n^{-d/2}\det J_n^{-1/2}=n^{-d/2}(\det J_n)^{-1/2}\), so
\(N_nI_n=\mathcal G(g_n)\). By definition \(N_nQ_{n,K}=\mathcal R_K(g_n)\)
and \(N_n\widehat I_n=(2\pi)^{d/2}a_n(\theta_n)\).

\emph{First item.} The one-point product rule has the single node \(0\)
with weight \((2\pi)^{d/2}\), by
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-gauss-hermite-exactness}. Since
\(g_n(0)=a_n(\theta_n)\), \(N_nQ_{n,1}=(2\pi)^{d/2}a_n(\theta_n)=N_n\widehat I_n\).

\emph{Setting for the other items.} Let \(r=1\) for the second item and
\(r=2\) for the third. In both cases, on the ball, \(h_n\) is
\(C^{2r+2}\) and \(a_n\) is \(C^{2r}\), and the operator norms of
\(D^kh_n\) for \(3\leq k\leq2r+2\) and of \(D^ka_n\) for
\(1\leq k\leq2r\) are at most \(A\). Write \(\varepsilon=n^{-1/2}\),
\(\beta=c^{-1/2}\) and \(b_n=n^{1/24}\). The eigenvalues of \(J_n^{-1}\)
lie in \([C^{-1},c^{-1}]\), so
\(C^{-1/2}\|z\|\leq\|J_n^{-1/2}z\|\leq\beta\|z\|\). Let
\(Z_n=\{z:\varepsilon\|J_n^{-1/2}z\|<\rho\}\), the set where
\(\theta_n(z)\) lies in the ball. All constants \(C_1,C_2,\ldots\) below
depend only on \(r\), \(K\), \(d\) and the uniform bounds.

\emph{Expansion on \(Z_n\).} Since \(\nabla h_n(\theta_n)=0\) and
\(-D^2h_n(\theta_n)=J_n\), Taylor's theorem of order \(2r+1\) with
Lagrange remainder, applied along the segment from \(\theta_n\) to
\(\theta_n(z)\), gives for \(z\in Z_n\)
\[
 n\{h_n(\theta_n(z))-h_n(\theta_n)\}
 =-\tfrac12\|z\|^2+\mathcal E_n(z),
\]
\[
 \mathcal E_n(z)=\sum_{k=3}^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}(z)+\rho_{h}(z),
\]
with \(|\rho_h(z)|\leq\varepsilon^{2r}A\beta^{2r+2}\|z\|^{2r+2}/(2r+2)!\).
Here the quadratic term is
\[
 -\tfrac n2\varepsilon^2z^TJ_n^{-1/2}J_nJ_n^{-1/2}z=-\tfrac12\|z\|^2,
\]
the order-\(k\) term is \(n\varepsilon^kt_{n,k}(z)=\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}(z)\),
and the remainder carries the factor \(n\varepsilon^{2r+2}=\varepsilon^{2r}\).
In the same way
\[
 \frac{a_n(\theta_n(z))}{a_n(\theta_n)}
 =1+\sum_{k=1}^{2r-1}\varepsilon^kg_{n,k}(z)+\rho_a(z),\qquad
 |\rho_a(z)|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2r}A\beta^{2r}\|z\|^{2r}}{(2r)!\,a_-}.
\]
Also \(|t_{n,k}(z)|\leq A\beta^k\|z\|^k/k!\) and
\(|g_{n,k}(z)|\leq A\beta^k\|z\|^k/(k!\,a_-)\). Since
\(|Da_n|\leq A\) on the ball, \(a_n(\theta)\leq a_++A\rho\) there, and the
amplitude ratio is at most \((a_++A\rho)/a_-\). On \(Z_n\),
\(g_n(z)=a_n(\theta_n(z))e^{\mathcal E_n(z)}\).

\emph{The polynomials.} Let \(P_{n,j}\), \(1\leq j\leq2r-1\), be the
coefficient of \(\varepsilon^j\) in the product of
\(1+\sum_{k=1}^{2r-1}\varepsilon^kg_{n,k}\) and
\(\sum_{m=0}^{2r-1}\frac1{m!}\bigl(\sum_{k=3}^{2r+1}\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}\bigr)^m\),
expanded as a polynomial in \(\varepsilon\). Then
\(P_{n,1}=t_{n,3}+g_{n,1}\), and for \(r=2\), \(P_{n,2}\) is the
polynomial of the statement: \(\varepsilon^2\) arises from \(t_{n,4}\),
from \(\tfrac12t_{n,3}^2\), from \(g_{n,1}t_{n,3}\) and from \(g_{n,2}\).
The factor \(\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}\) has degree \(k\leq3(k-2)\) and
parity \((-1)^{k-2}\), and \(\varepsilon^kg_{n,k}\) has degree
\(k\leq3k\) and parity \((-1)^k\). Hence \(P_{n,j}\) is a polynomial of
degree at most \(3j\) with \(P_{n,j}(-z)=(-1)^jP_{n,j}(z)\), and its
coefficients are bounded in \(n\).

\emph{Local remainder.} For \(z\in Z_n\) with \(\|z\|\leq b_n\) and
\(\varepsilon\leq1\), every exponent piece \(\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}(z)\)
with \(3\leq k\leq2r+1\), and also \(\rho_h(z)\), is bounded by
\(C_1\varepsilon(1+\|z\|)^{2r+2}\). Hence
\(|\mathcal E_n(z)|\leq C_2\varepsilon(1+b_n)^6\leq2^6C_2n^{-1/4}\). Choose \(n_0\)
so that for \(n\geq n_0\) this is at most \(1\) and
\(\varepsilon\beta b_n<\rho\); then \(\|z\|\leq b_n\) implies \(z\in Z_n\).
For \(|E|\leq1\), Taylor's theorem for the exponential gives
\(|e^E-\sum_{m<2r}E^m/m!|\leq e|E|^{2r}/(2r)!\), which here is at most
\(C_3\varepsilon^{2r}(1+\|z\|)^{2r(2r+2)}\). Multiplied by the bounded
amplitude ratio, this error stays of that form. Now multiply out
\[
 \Bigl(1+\sum_k\varepsilon^kg_{n,k}+\rho_a\Bigr)
 \sum_{m<2r}\frac1{m!}\Bigl(\sum_k\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}+\rho_h\Bigr)^m .
\]
Each resulting product has one amplitude factor and at most \(2r-1\)
exponent factors. Give \(\varepsilon^kg_{n,k}\) the order \(k\),
\(\varepsilon^{k-2}t_{n,k}\) the order \(k-2\), and \(\rho_a,\rho_h\) the
order \(2r\). A factor of order \(o\) is at most
\(C_4\varepsilon^o(1+\|z\|)^{2r+2}\). The products of total order at most
\(2r-1\) contain neither \(\rho_a\) nor \(\rho_h\), and their sum is
\(1+\sum_{j\leq2r-1}\varepsilon^jP_{n,j}\). Every other product has total
order at least \(2r\) and at most \(2r\) factors, so it is at most
\(C_5\varepsilon^{2r}(1+\|z\|)^{2r(2r+2)}\). There are finitely many
products. Hence, for \(n\geq n_0\) and \(\|z\|\leq b_n\),
\[
 \Bigl|\frac{g_n(z)}{a_n(\theta_n)}-1-\sum_{j=1}^{2r-1}\varepsilon^jP_{n,j}(z)\Bigr|
 \leq C_6\varepsilon^{2r}(1+\|z\|)^{2r(2r+2)} .
\]

\emph{The integral.} Split \(\mathbb R^d\) into
\(Z^{(1)}=\{\|z\|\leq b_n\}\), \(Z^{(2)}=Z_n\setminus Z^{(1)}\) and
\(Z^{(3)}=\mathbb R^d\setminus Z_n\). On \(Z^{(1)}\) the local remainder
contributes at most
\(C_6a_+\varepsilon^{2r}\int(1+\|z\|)^{2r(2r+2)}e^{-\|z\|^2/2}\,dz
=O(\varepsilon^{2r})\). For a polynomial \(P\) with bounded coefficients,
\[
 \int_{\|z\|>b_n}|P(z)|e^{-\|z\|^2/2}\,dz
 \leq e^{-b_n^2/4}\int|P(z)|e^{-\|z\|^2/4}\,dz=O(e^{-n^{1/12}/4}),
\]
so the polynomial part over \(Z^{(1)}\) equals its integral over
\(\mathbb R^d\) up to \(o(\varepsilon^{2r})\). On \(Z^{(2)}\), by the
local quadratic condition,
\[
 g_n(z)e^{-\|z\|^2/2}=a_n(\theta_n(z))e^{n\{h_n(\theta_n(z))-h_n(\theta_n)\}}
 \leq(a_++A\rho)e^{-c_0\|z\|^2/C},
\]
because \(n\varepsilon^2\|J_n^{-1/2}z\|^2\geq\|z\|^2/C\). So the integral
over \(\|z\|>b_n\) is at most
\[
 (a_++A\rho)e^{-c_0b_n^2/(2C)}\int e^{-c_0\|z\|^2/(2C)}\,dz
 =o(\varepsilon^{2r}).
\]
On \(Z^{(3)}\), undoing the substitution and using the tail condition,
the integral is at most \(n^{d/2}C^{d/2}A_Te^{-\eta n}=o(\varepsilon^{2r})\).
Since \(\mathcal G(1)=(2\pi)^{d/2}\) and \(\mathcal G(P_{n,j})=0\) for odd
\(j\) by \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-gauss-hermite-exactness},
\[
 \mathcal G(g_n)=a_n(\theta_n)\Bigl\{(2\pi)^{d/2}
 +\sum_{\text{even }j\leq2r-1}\varepsilon^j\mathcal G(P_{n,j})\Bigr\}
 +O(\varepsilon^{2r}).
\]

\emph{The rule.} The nodes form a fixed finite set. For \(n\geq n_0\) so
large that every node has norm at most \(b_n\), the local remainder
bound holds at every node with the factor
\((1+\max_{\mathbf k}\|z_{\mathbf k}\|)^{2r(2r+2)}\), a constant. The
weights are positive with sum \((2\pi)^{d/2}\), and
\(\mathcal R_K(P_{n,j})=0\) for odd \(j\). Hence
\[
 \mathcal R_K(g_n)=a_n(\theta_n)\Bigl\{(2\pi)^{d/2}
 +\sum_{\text{even }j\leq2r-1}\varepsilon^j\mathcal R_K(P_{n,j})\Bigr\}
 +O(\varepsilon^{2r}).
\]

\emph{Second item.} With \(r=1\) there is no even \(j\leq1\). Both
\(\mathcal G(g_n)\) and \(\mathcal R_K(g_n)\) equal
\(D_n+O(n^{-1})\), where \(D_n=(2\pi)^{d/2}a_n(\theta_n)\geq(2\pi)^{d/2}a_->0\).
So \(\mathcal R_K(g_n)>0\) for large \(n\), hence \(Q_{n,K}>0\), and
\(I_n/Q_{n,K}=\mathcal G(g_n)/\mathcal R_K(g_n)=1+O(n^{-1})\).

\emph{Third item.} With \(r=2\) the only even \(j\) is \(2\). Put
\(x_n=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\mathcal G(P_{n,2})\) and
\(y_n=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\mathcal R_K(P_{n,2})\). Both are bounded, because the
coefficients of \(P_{n,2}\) are bounded, its Gaussian moments are finite
and the nodes are fixed. Dividing the two displays by \(D_n\),
\[
 \frac{I_n}{Q_{n,K}}=\frac{1+x_n/n+O(n^{-2})}{1+y_n/n+O(n^{-2})}
 =1+\frac{x_n-y_n}n+O(n^{-2}),
\]
and \(x_n-y_n=\Delta_{n,K}\). For \(K\geq4\), \(P_{n,2}\) has total degree
at most \(6\leq2K-1\), so
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-gauss-hermite-exactness} gives
\(\mathcal G(P_{n,2})=\mathcal R_K(P_{n,2})\).

\emph{Two integrals.} Write \(I_{k,n}=Q_{k,n,K}(1+u_k)\) with \(u_k\) of
the order just proved. Then
\(B_{10}=(I_{1,n}/I_{0,n})=(Q_{1,n,K}/Q_{0,n,K})(1+u_1)/(1+u_0)\), and
\((1+u_1)/(1+u_0)-1=(u_1-u_0)/(1+u_0)\) has the same order.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-agqex}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-adaptive-quadrature-example}{Stirling's correction and a second mode}
\begin{proof}
\emph{First item.} The substitution \(u=n\theta\) gives
\(I_n=n^{-n-1}\int_0^\infty u^ne^{-u}\,du=n!/n^{n+1}\). The derivatives
of \(h(\theta)=\log\theta-\theta\) are \(h'=1/\theta-1\),
\(h''=-1/\theta^2\) and \(h^{(k)}=(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!/\theta^k\) for
\(k\geq2\). So \(h'(1)=0\) and \(J_n=-h''(1)=1\), giving \(c=C=1\). On
\(B(1,1/2)\), \(\theta>1/2\), and \(|h^{(k)}|\leq(k-1)!\,2^k\), which is
\(16,96,768,7680\) for \(k=3,4,5,6\); so \(A=7680\) serves, and
\(a_n=1\) has \(a_-=a_+=1\) and zero derivatives. For \(|v|<1/2\) let
\(f(v)=\log(1+v)-v=-\int_0^vt/(1+t)\,dt\). If \(0<v<1/2\), then
\(t/(1+t)\geq2t/3\) on \([0,v]\), so \(f(v)\leq-v^2/3\). If
\(-1/2<v<0\), then \(-t/(1+t)\geq-t\) on \([v,0]\), so
\(f(v)=-\int_v^0(-t)/(1+t)\,dt\leq-v^2/2\). Thus \(c_0=1/3\) serves.

For the tail put \(\phi(\theta)=\log\theta-\theta+1=h(\theta)-h(1)\),
which is concave. Note that \(\eta=\tfrac12-\log\tfrac32>0\) because
\(\tfrac32<e^{1/2}\). On \((0,1/2]\), \(\phi\) is increasing, so
\(\phi\leq\phi(1/2)=\tfrac12-\log2\). This is at most \(-\eta\), because
\(\tfrac12-\log2+\eta=1-\log3<0\). That part of the integral is at most
\(\tfrac12e^{-\eta n}\). On \([3/2,\infty)\) concavity gives
\(\phi(\theta)\leq\phi(3/2)+\phi'(3/2)(\theta-3/2)
=-\eta-(\theta-3/2)/3\), so that part is at most
\(e^{-\eta n}\int_0^\infty e^{-nt/3}\,dt=3e^{-\eta n}/n\leq3e^{-\eta n}\).
So \(A_T=7/2\) serves.

Here \(t_{n,3}(z)=h'''(1)z^3/6=z^3/3\), \(t_{n,4}(z)=h''''(1)z^4/24=-z^4/4\)
and \(g_{n,k}=0\). So \(P_{n,2}=-z^4/4+z^6/18\), and
\((2\pi)^{-1/2}\mathcal G(P_{n,2})=-\tfrac34+\tfrac{15}{18}=\tfrac1{12}\),
using \(m_4=3\sqrt{2\pi}\) and \(m_6=15\sqrt{2\pi}\). With the rules of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-gauss-hermite-exactness}:
\(\mathcal R_1(P_{n,2})=\sqrt{2\pi}P_{n,2}(0)=0\), so
\(\Delta_{n,1}=\tfrac1{12}\);
\(\mathcal R_2(P_{n,2})=\sqrt{2\pi}P_{n,2}(1)=\sqrt{2\pi}(\tfrac1{18}-\tfrac14)
=-\tfrac7{36}\sqrt{2\pi}\), so \(\Delta_{n,2}=\tfrac3{36}+\tfrac7{36}=\tfrac5{18}\);
and
\(\mathcal R_3(P_{n,2})=\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot\tfrac13P_{n,2}(\sqrt3)
=\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot\tfrac13(\tfrac{27}{18}-\tfrac94)=-\tfrac14\sqrt{2\pi}\), so
\(\Delta_{n,3}=\tfrac1{12}+\tfrac14=\tfrac13\). For \(K\geq4\) the third
item of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-adaptive-quadrature} gives \(0\).
These three coefficients are nonzero constants, so the relative errors
are of exact order \(n^{-1}\). Finally \(Q_{n,1}=\widehat I_n=e^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi/n}\),
and \(I_n/\widehat I_n=n!/(n^ne^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n})\), which is therefore
\(1+1/(12n)+O(n^{-2})\).

\emph{Second item.} For \(\theta<3/2\), \(\theta^2<(\theta-3)^2\), so
\(h_n(\theta)=-\theta^2/2\) on \((-\infty,3/2)\), and by symmetry
\(h_n(\theta)=-(\theta-3)^2/2\) on \((3/2,\infty)\). On \(B(0,1)\),
\(h_n\) is a polynomial with \(\nabla h_n(0)=0\), \(J_n=1\), zero third
and fourth derivatives, and \(h_n(v)-h_n(0)=-v^2/2\), so the first three
conditions hold with \(c_0=1/2\). The tail condition fails, because the
integral over \(|\theta|\geq1\) is at least
\(\int_{5/2}^{7/2}e^{-n(\theta-3)^2/2}\,d\theta\), which is of order
\(n^{-1/2}\), not exponentially small. By the symmetry about \(3/2\),
\[
 I_n=2\int_{-\infty}^{3/2}e^{-n\theta^2/2}\,d\theta
 =2\sqrt{2\pi/n}\,\Phi(\tfrac32\sqrt n).
\]
For \(n>4x_K^2/9\) every node \(\theta_n(z_k)=x_k/\sqrt n\) satisfies
\(|x_k|/\sqrt n<3/2\), so \(g_n(x_k)=e^{-x_k^2/2}e^{x_k^2/2}=1\), and
\(Q_{n,K}=n^{-1/2}\sum_k\omega_k=\sqrt{2\pi/n}\). The ratio is
\(2\Phi(\tfrac32\sqrt n)\), which tends to \(2\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-unitinfo}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-unit-information-schwarz}{The constant-order terms under the unit-information prior}
\begin{proof}
By the first premise,
\(\log\pi_1(\widehat\beta,\widehat\psi)=\log\pi_0(\widehat\beta)
 -\tfrac p2\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\det W
 -\tfrac12(\widehat\psi-\psi_0)^TW^{-1}(\widehat\psi-\psi_0)\).
Substituting this into the definitions of \(c_{1,n}\) (dimension
\(d_0+p\)) and \(c_{0,n}\) (dimension \(d_0\)) gives
\[
\begin{split}
 c_{1,n}-c_{0,n}={}&\{\log\pi_0(\widehat\beta_n)-\log\pi_0(\widehat\beta^*_n)\}
 -\tfrac12(\widehat\psi_n-\psi_0)^TW^{-1}(\widehat\psi_n-\psi_0)\\
 &-\tfrac12\{\log\det J_{1,n}-\log\det J_{0,n}+\log\det W\}.
\end{split}
\]
By the second premise, the first brace is at most \(K^2n^{-1/2}\) in
absolute value, and the quadratic term is at most
\(\tfrac12\|W^{-1}\|K^2n^{-1}\).

For the determinants, \(A_n\) and \(D_n\) are principal blocks of
\(J_{1,n}\). Their Rayleigh quotients are Rayleigh quotients of
\(J_{1,n}\) on coordinate subspaces, so their eigenvalues lie in
\([c,C]\). Write \(\Sigma_n=D_n-B_n^TA_n^{-1}B_n\). The factorization
\[
 J_{1,n}=
 \begin{pmatrix}I&0\\B_n^TA_n^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}A_n&0\\0&\Sigma_n\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}I&A_n^{-1}B_n\\0&I\end{pmatrix}
\]
can be checked by multiplication. Its outer factors are unit triangular,
so \(\det J_{1,n}=\det A_n\det\Sigma_n\). The proof of
\cref{thm:p15-laplace-curvature-replacement} shows the following. If a
symmetric \(J\) has eigenvalues at least \(c\) and \(\|\widetilde J-J\|\leq\delta<c/2\),
then \(|\log\det\widetilde J-\log\det J|\leq2\dim(J)\delta/c\). Apply it
twice. First take \(J=J_{0,n}\) and \(\widetilde J=A_n\), with
\(\delta=Kn^{-1/2}\). Then take \(J=D_n\) and \(\widetilde J=\Sigma_n\),
with \(\delta=\|B_n^TA_n^{-1}B_n\|\leq K^2/(cn)\). For large \(n\) both
values of \(\delta\) are below \(c/2\), and
\[
\begin{split}
 &\log\det J_{1,n}-\log\det J_{0,n}+\log\det W\\
 &\quad=\{\log\det A_n-\log\det J_{0,n}\}\\
 &\qquad+\{\log\det\Sigma_n-\log\det D_n\}+\{\log\det D_n+\log\det W\}.
\end{split}
\]
The three braces are \(O(n^{-1/2})\), \(O(n^{-1})\) and, by the third
premise, \(O(n^{-1/2})\). Hence \(c_{1,n}-c_{0,n}=O(n^{-1/2})\). Since
\(\mathrm{BIC}_k=-2\ell_k+d_k\log n\) and \(d_1-d_0=p\),
\(-\tfrac12(\mathrm{BIC}_1-\mathrm{BIC}_0)=S_n\).
\Cref{thm:p15-bic-leading-order} gives
\(\log B_{10}=S_n+c_{1,n}-c_{0,n}+O(n^{-1})=S_n+O(n^{-1/2})\).

For the normal instance apply \cref{thm:p15-normal-point-null-factor}
with \(\theta_0=\mu_1=0\), \(s^2=\sigma^2/n\) and \(\tau^2=\sigma^2\),
so that \(r=n\) and \(z=\bar x/s\). This gives
\(\log B_{10}=-\tfrac12\log(1+n)+nz^2/\{2(n+1)\}\). The full-sample
log-likelihood ratio at the estimate \(\widehat\psi=\bar x\) is
\(n\bar x^2/(2\sigma^2)=z^2/2\), so \(S_n=z^2/2-\tfrac12\log n\).
Subtracting gives the displayed difference, which is at most
\((1+K^2)/(2n)\) in absolute value when \(|z|\leq K\). This instance
meets the premises: \(d_0=0\), \(J_{1,n}=D_n=1/\sigma^2=W^{-1}\), and
\(|\widehat\psi_n|=\sigma|z|n^{-1/2}\). The hypotheses of
\cref{thm:p15-bic-leading-order} for bounded \(\bar x\) were verified in
\cref{thm:p15-laplace-normal-example}. If \(\bar x\to\psi_1\neq0\), then
\(z^2/\{2(n+1)\}=n\bar x^2/\{2\sigma^2(n+1)\}\to\psi_1^2/(2\sigma^2)\),
and the logarithmic term tends to zero.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-mixture}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-mixture-estimators}{Monotone estimating equations}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Shape of \(\psi_p\).} For \(p>0\) the denominator of \(\psi_p\)
is positive. Differentiation gives
\[
 \partial_p\psi_p=\frac{L}{\{\delta p+(1-\delta)L\}^2}\geq0,
 \qquad
 \partial_p^2\psi_p=-\frac{2\delta L}{\{\delta p+(1-\delta)L\}^3}.
\]
If \(L=0\), then \(\psi_p=1/\delta\) for all \(p\). If \(L>0\), then
\(\psi_p\to-1/(1-\delta)\) as \(p\downarrow0\) and \(\psi_p\to1/\delta\)
as \(p\to\infty\), strictly increasing. Since \(p/(\delta p+(1-\delta)L)\leq1/\delta\)
and \(L/(\delta p+(1-\delta)L)\leq1/(1-\delta)\),
\(|\psi_p|\leq\max\{1/\delta,1/(1-\delta)\}\). With \(a=\delta p\) and
\(b=(1-\delta)L\), the inequalities \((a+b)^2\geq4ab\) and
\((a+b)^3\geq3a^2b\) give
\(0\leq\partial_p\psi_p\leq1/\{4\delta(1-\delta)p\}\) and
\(|\partial_p^2\psi_p|\leq2/\{3\delta(1-\delta)p^2\}\).

\emph{First item.} The stated limits and monotonicity of \(\Psi_m\)
follow by summation. If \(N_+=0\), then \(\Psi_m\equiv m/\delta>0\) has
no root. If \(N_+\geq1\), \(\Psi_m\) is continuous and strictly
increasing, so the intermediate value theorem gives a unique root exactly
when \(\Psi_m(0+)<0\). For the \(\widehat{\rm pr}_4\) equation, write
\(N(p)\) and \(D(p)\) for its numerator and denominator. Then \(p=N(p)/D(p)\)
if and only if \(pD(p)=N(p)\), that is
\(\sum_iw_p(\theta_i)\{p-L(\theta_i)\}=0\). The imagined terms cancel.
Since \(w_p(p-L)=p\,\psi_p\), this is \(\sum_i\psi_p(\theta_i)=0\). Each
posterior draw lies in \(\{L>0\}\) almost surely, because
\(P(L=0)=I^{-1}\int_{\{L=0\}}L\,d\Pi=0\). So \(N_0=0\), \(N_+=m\), and
the unique root exists.

\emph{Second item.} The measure \(Q_\delta\) has density
\(\delta+(1-\delta)L/I\) relative to \(\Pi\). Let
\(g(p)=E_{Q_\delta}\psi_p\). At \(p=I\),
\[
 g(I)=\int\frac{I-L}{\delta I+(1-\delta)L}\cdot
 \frac{\delta I+(1-\delta)L}{I}\,d\Pi=\frac{I-I}{I}=0 .
\]
Bounded convergence gives \(g'(p)=E_{Q_\delta}\partial_p\psi_p\). This
is positive, because \(Q_\delta(L>0)\geq(1-\delta)P(L>0)=1-\delta\). So
\(g\) is strictly increasing with unique root \(I\). Fix a rational
\(\epsilon\in(0,I)\). The bounded i.i.d. strong law gives
\(\Psi_m(I\pm\epsilon)/m\to g(I\pm\epsilon)\) almost surely, with
\(g(I-\epsilon)<0<g(I+\epsilon)\), and \(N_+/m\to Q_\delta(L>0)>0\).
Almost surely, therefore, eventually \(N_+\geq1\) and
\(\Psi_m(I-\epsilon)<0<\Psi_m(I+\epsilon)\). Then the root exists, is
unique, and lies in \((I-\epsilon,I+\epsilon)\). Intersecting over
rational \(\epsilon\) proves almost-sure convergence.

For the limit law, on the event that the root \(\widehat p\) exists and
lies in \([I/2,2I]\), the mean value theorem gives \(\bar p\) between
\(\widehat p\) and \(I\) with
\[
 \sqrt m(\widehat p-I)=-\frac{m^{-1/2}\Psi_m(I)}{m^{-1}\Psi_m'(\bar p)} .
\]
The numerator is a normalized sum of bounded i.i.d. variables with mean
\(g(I)=0\). By \cref{thm:p15-clt-iid} it converges to
\(\operatorname{normal}(0,E_{Q_\delta}\psi_I^2)\). On \([I/2,2I]\) the
second-derivative bound makes \(m^{-1}\Psi_m'\) Lipschitz with constant
\(8/\{3\delta(1-\delta)I^2\}\), uniformly in the sample. Hence
\(|m^{-1}\Psi_m'(\bar p)-m^{-1}\Psi_m'(I)|\to0\) almost surely. The strong
law gives \(m^{-1}\Psi_m'(I)\to g'(I)>0\). The complementary event has
probability tending to zero. \Cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation}, with a fixed
default value on that event, gives the normal limit with variance
\(E_{Q_\delta}\psi_I^2/g'(I)^2=v_3\).

\emph{Third item.} Let \(g_4(p)=E_P\psi_p\). Under \(P\), \(L>0\)
almost surely, so \(g_4\) is continuous and strictly increasing, with
limits \(-1/(1-\delta)\) and \(1/\delta\) by bounded convergence. It
therefore has a unique root \(p_\delta\). The argument of the second
item, with \(P\) in place of \(Q_\delta\), proves that the roots
converge almost surely to \(p_\delta\). Since \(g_4\) is strictly
increasing, \(p_\delta=I\) exactly when \(g_4(I)=0\). Because
\(dP/d\Pi=L/I\), that is the displayed integral condition. In the
example, \(P=(1/4,3/4)\) and, with \(\delta=1/2\),
\[
 g_4(p)=\frac12\cdot\frac{p-1}{p+1}+\frac32\cdot\frac{p-3}{p+3}.
\]
Clearing denominators gives \(2p^2-2p-6=0\), whose positive root is
\((1+\sqrt{13})/2\neq2\). For \(\widehat{\rm pr}_3\),
\(Q_{1/2}=(3/8,5/8)\), and
\(g(2)=\tfrac38\cdot\tfrac{1}{3/2}-\tfrac58\cdot\tfrac{1}{5/2}=0\),
in agreement with the second item.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-bridge}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-bridge-identity}{Two expectations}
\begin{proof}
The function \(\alpha q_1\) is \(p_2\)-integrable because
\[
 \int|\alpha q_1|\,p_2\,d\mu=c_2^{-1}\int|\alpha|q_1q_2\,d\mu<\infty .
\]
Its integral is \(\kappa/c_2\). In the same way \(E_{p_1}[\alpha q_2]=\kappa/c_1\).
Since \(\kappa\neq0\), dividing gives \(c_1/c_2\). The two sample means
are averages of integrable i.i.d. variables. By
\cref{thm:p15-etemadi-strong-law} they converge almost surely to
\(\kappa/c_2\) and \(\kappa/c_1\). The second limit is nonzero, so the
denominator is eventually nonzero and the ratio converges.

For the first special case,
\(E_f[\alpha q_1]=\int_{\{f>0\}}L\pi\,d\mu=I\,P(f>0)\). For the second,
\(E_f[\alpha q_1]=\int_{\{L\pi>0\}}f\,d\mu\) and
\(E_P[\alpha f]=E_P[f/(L\pi)]\). In the first case
\(\int|\alpha|q_1q_2\,d\mu=I\,P(f>0)\leq I\). In the second it is
\(\int_{\{L\pi>0\}}f\,d\mu\leq1\). The first display is the direct
computation. The identity applies to the second whenever the integral is
positive, and rearrangement gives the displayed form. For two models on
a common space, \(c_1=I_1\) and \(c_2=I_0\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-optbridge}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-optimal-bridge}{The delta method, an inequality and a contraction}
\begin{proof}
Throughout, \(w=s_1p_1+s_2p_2\), which is positive on \(S\), and
\(k(\alpha)=\int\alpha p_1p_2\,d\mu\), so that
\(\kappa=c_1c_2k(\alpha)\).

\emph{First item.} Let \(X_j=\alpha q_1(\theta'_j)\) and
\(Y_i=\alpha q_2(\theta_i)\), with means \(\mu_X=\kappa/c_2\) and
\(\mu_Y=\kappa/c_1\) by \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-bridge-identity}, so
\(r=\mu_X/\mu_Y\). Their second moments are
\(c_2^{-1}\int\alpha^2q_1^2q_2\,d\mu\) and
\(c_1^{-1}\int\alpha^2q_2^2q_1\,d\mu\), finite by square-integrability;
call the variances \(v_X,v_Y\). With \(\bar X,\bar Y\) the two sample
means, wherever \(\bar Y\neq0\),
\[
 \bar Y\cdot\sqrt M(\widehat r_\alpha-r)
 =\sqrt M(\bar X-\mu_X)-r\sqrt M(\bar Y-\mu_Y) .
\]
By \cref{thm:p15-clt-iid}, \(\sqrt{M_2}(\bar X-\mu_X)\Rightarrow
\operatorname{normal}(0,v_X)\), and since \(\sqrt{M/M_2}\to s_2^{-1/2}\),
\cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} gives
\(\sqrt M(\bar X-\mu_X)\Rightarrow\operatorname{normal}(0,v_X/s_2)\).
Likewise \(-r\sqrt M(\bar Y-\mu_Y)\Rightarrow
\operatorname{normal}(0,r^2v_Y/s_1)\). The two terms are independent
within each row, so by the second item of
\cref{thm:p15-criteria-limit-operations} their sum converges to the
normal law with variance \(v_X/s_2+r^2v_Y/s_1\). By the strong law
\(\bar Y\to\mu_Y\neq0\) almost surely, and
\cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} divides by it. After division by \(r\)
the limit variance is \(v_X/(s_2\mu_X^2)+v_Y/(s_1\mu_Y^2)\). In terms of
\(p_1,p_2\),
\[
 \frac{v_X}{\mu_X^2}=\frac{\int\alpha^2p_1^2p_2\,d\mu}{k(\alpha)^2}-1,
 \qquad
 \frac{v_Y}{\mu_Y^2}=\frac{\int\alpha^2p_1p_2^2\,d\mu}{k(\alpha)^2}-1,
\]
because the factors \(c_1^2c_2^2\) cancel. Since
\(1/s_1+1/s_2=1/(s_1s_2)\), the sum is \(\mathcal V(\alpha)\).

\emph{Second item.} The integrand of \(\mathcal H\) is positive on \(S\),
and \(\mu(S)>0\), so \(\mathcal H>0\). For \(a,b>0\), Cauchy--Schwarz in
\(\mathbb R^2\) gives \((s_1/b+s_2/a)(s_1b+s_2a)\geq(s_1+s_2)^2=1\), with
equality exactly when \(a=b\). With \(a=p_1\), \(b=p_2\) on \(S\),
\(p_1p_2/w=1/(s_1/p_2+s_2/p_1)\leq s_1p_2+s_2p_1\), so
\(\mathcal H\leq\int_S(s_1p_2+s_2p_1)\,d\mu\leq1\). Equality throughout
holds exactly when \(p_1=p_2\) \(\mu\)-almost everywhere on \(S\) and
\(\int_{S^c}(s_1p_2+s_2p_1)\,d\mu=0\), that is, exactly when
\(p_1=p_2\) \(\mu\)-almost everywhere.

Off \(S\) the integrands of \(k(\alpha)\) and of the numerator of
\(\mathcal V(\alpha)\) vanish. Almost surely \(q_2(\theta'_j)>0\) and
\(q_1(\theta_i)>0\), since for instance \(p_2(\{q_2=0\})=0\); so a
\(p_2\)-draw outside \(S\) has \(q_1=0\), a \(p_1\)-draw outside \(S\) has
\(q_2=0\), and the corresponding terms of \(\widehat r_\alpha\) are zero
whatever \(\alpha\) is there. On \(S\) write
\(\alpha p_1p_2=\bigl(\alpha\sqrt{p_1p_2w}\bigr)\bigl(\sqrt{p_1p_2/w}\bigr)\).
The Cauchy--Schwarz inequality in \(L^2(\mu|_S)\) gives
\[
 k(\alpha)^2\leq\int_S\alpha^2p_1p_2w\,d\mu\cdot\mathcal H,
\]
so \(\mathcal V(\alpha)\geq(1/\mathcal H-1)/(s_1s_2)\). Equality holds
exactly when the two factors are linearly dependent in \(L^2(\mu|_S)\).
The second is not zero, because \(\mathcal H>0\), so equality holds
exactly when \(\alpha\sqrt{p_1p_2w}=\lambda_0\sqrt{p_1p_2/w}\), that is
\(\alpha=\lambda_0/w\), \(\mu\)-almost everywhere on \(S\); and
\(\lambda_0\neq0\) because \(k(\alpha)\neq0\). On \(S\),
\(s_1q_1+s_2rq_2=c_1w\), so \(\alpha_r=1/(c_1w)\) there, and the
equality case is \(\alpha=\lambda_1\alpha_r\) with \(\lambda_1=c_1\lambda_0\).

\(\alpha_r\) satisfies the hypotheses: \(\int\alpha_rq_1q_2\,d\mu
=c_2\mathcal H\in(0,\infty)\), and since \(p_1/w\leq1/s_1\) and
\(p_2/w\leq1/s_2\),
\[
 \int\alpha_r^2q_1q_2(q_1+q_2)\,d\mu
 =\frac{c_2}{c_1}\int_S\frac{p_1p_2}w\cdot\frac{c_1p_1+c_2p_2}w\,d\mu
 \leq\frac{c_2}{c_1}\Bigl(\frac{c_1}{s_1}+\frac{c_2}{s_2}\Bigr)\mathcal H<\infty .
\]
For \(\alpha_r\), \(\int\alpha_r^2p_1p_2w\,d\mu=c_1^{-2}\mathcal H\) and
\(k(\alpha_r)=c_1^{-1}\mathcal H\), so the ratio in the criterion is
\(1/\mathcal H\) and \(\mathcal V(\alpha_r)=(1/\mathcal H-1)/(s_1s_2)\).

\emph{Third item.} For \(x>0\) put
\[
 A(x)=\frac1{M_2}\sum_j\frac{q_1(\theta'_j)}{s_1q_1(\theta'_j)+s_2xq_2(\theta'_j)},
\]
\[
 B(x)=\frac1{M_1}\sum_i\frac{q_2(\theta_i)}{s_1q_1(\theta_i)+s_2xq_2(\theta_i)} .
\]
Under the hypotheses every denominator is positive, \(A(x)>0\),
\(B(x)>0\), and \(\mathcal T_M(x)=A(x)/B(x)\). The equation
\(x=\mathcal T_M(x)\) is equivalent to \(xB(x)-A(x)=0\), and
\(xB(x)-A(x)=\Psi^{\rm b}_M(x)\). Each term \(xq_2/(s_1q_1+s_2xq_2)\) with
\(q_2>0\) is strictly increasing in \(x\) (its derivative is
\(s_1q_1q_2/(\cdot)^2>0\) because \(q_1(\theta_i)>0\)), and the others are
zero. Each term \(q_1/(s_1q_1+s_2xq_2)\) with \(q_1>0\) is strictly
decreasing, and the others are zero. Since at least one term of each kind
is not zero, \(\Psi^{\rm b}_M\) is continuous and strictly increasing. As
\(x\downarrow0\) its first sum tends to \(0\) and its second to
\(N'/(s_1M_2)>0\), with \(N'\) the number of \(j\) with
\(q_1(\theta'_j)>0\). As \(x\to\infty\) the first sum tends to
\(N/(s_2M_1)>0\), with \(N\) the number of \(i\) with \(q_2(\theta_i)>0\),
and the second to \(0\). So \(\Psi^{\rm b}_M\) has exactly one root
\(\widehat r_*\), and it is the unique fixed point.

Put \(\Gamma_M(y)=\log\mathcal T_M(e^y)\), a continuously differentiable
function on \(\mathbb R\), and \(x=e^y\). Then
\(\Gamma_M'(y)=xA'(x)/A(x)-xB'(x)/B(x)\). Differentiating term by term,
\[
 -\frac{xA'(x)}{A(x)}=\frac{\sum_ja_j\tau'_j}{\sum_ja_j},\qquad
 -\frac{xB'(x)}{B(x)}=\frac{\sum_ib_i\tau_i}{\sum_ib_i},
\]
where \(a_j\) and \(b_i\) are the summands of \(A(x)\) and \(B(x)\), and
\(\tau'_j\), \(\tau_i\) are the values of
\(s_2xq_2/(s_1q_1+s_2xq_2)\) at \(\theta'_j\) and \(\theta_i\). Where
\(a_j>0\), \(q_1(\theta'_j)>0\) and \(q_2(\theta'_j)>0\), so
\(\tau'_j\in(0,1)\). Where \(b_i>0\), \(q_2(\theta_i)>0\) and
\(q_1(\theta_i)>0\), so \(\tau_i\in(0,1)\). Both weighted averages
therefore lie in \((0,1)\), and \(|\Gamma_M'(y)|<1\) for every \(y\). Let
\(y_*=\log\widehat r_*\), \(y_0=\log\widehat r^{(0)}\),
\(D=|y_0-y_*|\), and let \(\lambda\) be the maximum of \(|\Gamma_M'|\) on
\([y_*-D,y_*+D]\); \(\lambda<1\) because \(\Gamma_M'\) is continuous. For
\(y\) in that interval the mean value theorem gives
\(|\Gamma_M(y)-y_*|=|\Gamma_M(y)-\Gamma_M(y_*)|\leq\lambda|y-y_*|\), so the
interval is mapped into itself. Induction gives the stated bound for
\(y_t=\log\widehat r^{(t)}\).

\emph{Fourth item.} Since \(p_1(\{q_1=0\})=0\) and \(p_2(\{q_2=0\})=0\),
almost surely all draws satisfy the first two conditions. Also
\(p_2(\{q_1>0\})=c_2^{-1}\int_Sq_2\,d\mu>0\) and similarly
\(p_1(\{q_2>0\})>0\), so almost surely the last two conditions hold once
\(M_1\) and \(M_2\) are large. Define, with value \(0\) where the
denominator vanishes,
\[
 \phi_1(\theta;x)=\frac{xq_2}{s_1q_1+s_2xq_2},\qquad
 \phi_2(\theta;x)=\frac{q_1}{s_1q_1+s_2xq_2},
\]
so that \(0\leq\phi_1\leq1/s_2\), \(0\leq\phi_2\leq1/s_1\), and
\(\Psi^{\rm b}_M(x)\) is the \(p_1\)-sample mean of \(\phi_1(\cdot;x)\)
minus the \(p_2\)-sample mean of \(\phi_2(\cdot;x)\). Its expectation is
\[
 \psi(x)=\int_S\frac{xq_2q_1/c_1-q_1q_2/c_2}{s_1q_1+s_2xq_2}\,d\mu
 =\Bigl(\frac x{c_1}-\frac1{c_2}\Bigr)G(x),
\]
where
\[
 G(x)=\int_S\frac{q_1q_2}{s_1q_1+s_2xq_2}\,d\mu .
\]
Here \(0<G(x)\leq c_1/(s_2x)\), so \(\psi\) has the sign of \(x-r\). For
rational \(\epsilon\in(0,r)\), the strong law for bounded variables gives
\(\Psi^{\rm b}_M(r\pm\epsilon)\to\psi(r\pm\epsilon)\) almost surely. So
almost surely, for all large \(M\),
\(\Psi^{\rm b}_M(r-\epsilon)<0<\Psi^{\rm b}_M(r+\epsilon)\), and the root
lies in \((r-\epsilon,r+\epsilon)\). Intersecting over rational
\(\epsilon\) gives \(\widehat r_*\to r\) almost surely.

For the limit law, put \(u=s_1q_1\) and \(v=s_2xq_2\). Then
\(\partial_x\phi_1=s_1q_1q_2/(u+v)^2\) and
\(\partial_x\phi_2=-s_2q_1q_2/(u+v)^2\), and
\[

\([r/2,2r]\) the second derivative of \(\Psi^{\rm b}_M\) is at most
\(32/(27r^2s_1s_2)\), uniformly in the sample. On the event that the
root lies in \([r/2,2r]\), the mean value theorem gives \(\bar x\) between
\(\widehat r_*\) and \(r\) with
\[

 =-\frac{\sqrt M\,\Psi^{\rm b}_M(r)}{(\Psi^{\rm b}_M)'(\bar x)} .
\]
Since \(\psi(r)=0\), the two sample means in \(\Psi^{\rm b}_M(r)\) have a

\[

\]

the consistency of \(\widehat r_*\) give

\(uv/(u+v)^2\leq1/4\). The strong law gives

\(\psi'(r)\), by differentiation under the integral, justified by the
derivative bound and bounded convergence. Since the factor
\(x/c_1-1/c_2\) vanishes at \(r\) and \(G\) is continuous by bounded
convergence, \(\psi'(r)=\lim_{x\to r}\psi(x)/(x-r)=G(r)/c_1\). The complementary event has probability tending to
zero, and \cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} gives the normal limit of
\(\sqrt M(\widehat r_*-r)\) with variance \(\sigma^2/\psi'(r)^2\).

At \(x=r\), on \(S\), \(s_1q_1+s_2rq_2=c_1w\), so
\(\phi_1(\cdot;r)=p_2/w\), \(\phi_2(\cdot;r)=p_1/w\) and
\(G(r)=c_2\mathcal H\); thus \(\psi'(r)=\mathcal H/r\). Also
\(E_{p_1}\phi_1=E_{p_2}\phi_2=\mathcal H\),
\(E_{p_1}\phi_1^2=\int_Sp_1p_2^2/w^2\,d\mu\) and

\[

So the limit variance of \(\sqrt M(\widehat r_*-r)/r\) is
\(\sigma^2/\mathcal H^2=(1/\mathcal H-1)/(s_1s_2)=\mathcal V(\alpha_r)\).
\end{proof}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-lapmet}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-laplace-metropolis}{Mode and covariance errors in the Laplace--Metropolis value}
\begin{proof}

\(\widehat I_{\rm LM}\) and of
\(\widehat I_{\rm post}=(2\pi)^{d/2}|H_n|^{-1/2}e^{q_n(\widetilde\theta_n)}\)
differ by the displayed amount. Put
\(v=\theta^*_n-\widetilde\theta_n\). The third premise of
\cref{def:p15-laplace-family} gives
\(q_n(\widetilde\theta_n)-q_n(\theta^*_n)\geq nc_0\|v\|^2\). For the upper
bound, the gradient at the mode is zero, and Taylor's theorem with
integral remainder gives
\[
 q_n(\widetilde\theta_n+v)-q_n(\widetilde\theta_n)
 =n\int_0^1(1-t)\,D^2h_n(\widetilde\theta_n+tv)[v,v]\,dt .
\]
On the ball,
\(\|D^2h_n(\xi)\|\leq\|J_n\|+\sup\|D^3h_n\|\,\|\xi-\widetilde\theta_n\|\leq C+A\rho\).
Hence the absolute difference is at most \(\tfrac12n(C+A\rho)\|v\|^2\).
The eigenvalues of \(E_n\) lie in \([-e_n,e_n]\subset(-1/2,1/2)\), so
\(\mathrm{I}+E_n\) is positive definite. Since \(|\log(1+t)|\leq2|t|\)
there, \(|\tfrac12\log\det(\mathrm{I}+E_n)|\leq de_n\).

\Cref{thm:p15-laplace-mode-variants} gives
\(\log I_n-\log\widehat I_{\rm post}=O(n^{-1})\). Adding the bounds
proves the stated orders and the inheritance condition. If \(e_n\to0\)
and \(\liminf n\varepsilon_n^2>0\), then
\(\log\widehat I_{\rm post}-\log\widehat I_{\rm LM}\geq nc_0\varepsilon_n^2-de_n\).
This has positive \(\liminf\), and so does \(\log I_n-\log\widehat I_{\rm LM}\).

In the normal instance, the log posterior kernel is
\(q(\theta)=q(\widetilde\theta)-\tfrac12H(\theta-\widetilde\theta)^2\)
with \(H=1/s^2+1/\tau^2\), and the posterior is
\(\operatorname{normal}(\widetilde\theta,1/H)\). Exact draws are
\(\theta_i=\widetilde\theta+Z_iH^{-1/2}\). The maximizer of \(q\) among
them minimizes \(|Z_i|\); ties have probability zero. Hence
\(q(\widetilde\theta)-q(\theta^*)=\tfrac12\min_iZ_i^2\), and with
\(\Sigma^*=1/H\), \(E_n=0\). The posterior-mode calculation is exact by
\cref{thm:p15-laplace-normal-example}, so
\(\log I_n-\log\widehat I_{\rm LM}=\tfrac12\min_iZ_i^2\). Independence
gives \(\mathbb P(\tfrac12\min_iZ_i^2>t)=\mathbb P(|Z_1|>\sqrt{2t})^M
=\{2-2\Phi(\sqrt{2t})\}^M\). This law does not involve \(n\), and the
error is positive almost surely because \(\mathbb P(Z_i=0)=0\).
\end{proof}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-sdratio}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-savage-dickey}{Evaluating the posterior marginal at the null}
\begin{proof}
By definition of the conditional version,
\[
 p_1(\psi_0\mid D)=\frac{\pi_1(\psi_0)}{I_1}
 \int L(\beta,\psi_0)\pi_1(\beta\mid\psi_0)\,d\beta .
\]
\emph{First item.} Under the condition, the integral is \(I_0\). Thus
\(p_1(\psi_0\mid D)=\pi_1(\psi_0)I_0/I_1>0\), and
\(\pi_1(\psi_0)/p_1(\psi_0\mid D)=I_1/I_0=B_{10}\).

\emph{Second item.} The support hypothesis lets the integral be taken
over \(\{\pi_0>0\}\):
\[
\begin{split}
 \int L(\beta,\psi_0)\pi_1(\beta\mid\psi_0)\,d\beta
 &=\int_{\{\pi_0>0\}}\frac{\pi_1(\beta\mid\psi_0)}{\pi_0(\beta)}
 L(\beta,\psi_0)\pi_0(\beta)\,d\beta\\
 &=I_0E_{P_0}\!\left[\frac{\pi_1(\beta\mid\psi_0)}{\pi_0(\beta)}\right],
\end{split}
\]
because \(P_0(d\beta)=L(\beta,\psi_0)\pi_0(\beta)\,d\beta/I_0\) gives
\(\{\pi_0=0\}\) mass zero. The equality holds in \([0,\infty]\).
Dividing by \(I_1\) gives the identity.

\emph{Third item.} Since \(p\geq1\), the set \(\{\psi=\psi_0\}\) is
Lebesgue-null in \(\mathbb R^{d_0+p}\). Changing \(\pi_1\) there changes
no integral over that space, so \(I_1\) is unchanged. \(I_0\) does not
involve \(\pi_1\). The new marginal value is \(\int g\), and the new
posterior marginal value is \(I_1^{-1}\int L(\beta,\psi_0)g(\beta)\,d\beta\).

\emph{Fourth item.} With \(d_0=0\) the conditional condition is vacuous.
Here \(I_0=L(\psi_0)\) and \(p_1(\psi_0\mid x)=L(\psi_0)\pi_1(\psi_0)/I_1\),
so the ratio is \(I_1/L(\psi_0)=m_1(x)/m_0(x)\), the reciprocal of the
factor in \cref{thm:p15-normal-point-null-factor}. In the two-coordinate
example, \(\pi_1(\beta\mid0)=\phi_{0,1}(\beta)\neq\phi_{0,4}(\beta)=\pi_0(\beta)\),
and both are positive. The posterior under \(H_0\) at \(x_1=0\) is
\(\operatorname{normal}(0,4/5)\), since precisions add:
\(1/4+1=5/4\). Here \(\phi_{0,1}/\phi_{0,4}=2e^{-3\beta^2/8}\). For
\(\beta\sim\operatorname{normal}(0,v)\) and \(a>0\), completing the
Gaussian integral gives \(Ee^{-a\beta^2}=(1+2av)^{-1/2}\). With
\(a=3/8\) and \(v=4/5\) this equals \((5/8)^{1/2}\), so the correction
factor is \(2(5/8)^{1/2}=\sqrt{5/2}\).
\end{proof}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin}{Oscillation contraction and the residual kernel}
\begin{proof}
Two facts about the canonical chain are used throughout. Under
\(\mathbb P_\mu\), \(X_t\) has law \(\mu K^t\), by the finite-dimensional
formula defining \(\mathbb P_\mu\) \cite[Sec.~5.2, p.~273]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}.
For bounded measurable \(Y\) on the path space and \(m\geq0\),
\(\mathbb E_\mu(Y\circ\vartheta^m\mid\mathcal F_m)=\mathbb E_{X_m}Y\),
where \(\mathcal F_m=\sigma(X_0,\ldots,X_m)\) and \(x\mapsto\mathbb E_xY\)
is measurable \cite[Theorem~5.2.3, p.~275]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}.
Taking expectations, \(\mathbb P_\mu(\vartheta^{-m}E)=\int\mathbb P_x(E)\,\mu K^m(dx)\).

\emph{Third item.} For \(A\in\mathcal A\),
\(\gamma(A)=\gamma K(A)=\int K(x,A)\,\gamma(dx)\geq\varepsilon\zeta(A)\).

\emph{First item.} If \(\varepsilon=1\), then \(K(x,A)\geq\zeta(A)\) and
\(K(x,A^c)\geq\zeta(A^c)\); since both are probabilities, \(K(x,A)=\zeta(A)\)
for all \(x\) and \(A\). Then \(b=Kb=\zeta(b)\) is constant. If
\(\varepsilon<1\), put
\[
 R(x,A)=\frac{K(x,A)-\varepsilon\zeta(A)}{1-\varepsilon}.
\]
By (D) it is nonnegative; it is countably additive with \(R(x,\Theta)=1\),
and measurable in \(x\). So \(R\) is a transition probability and
\(K=(1-\varepsilon)R+\varepsilon\zeta\) row by row. Let \(b\) be bounded
and harmonic, with \(s=\sup b\) and \(i=\inf b\). For any \(x,y\),
\[
 b(x)-b(y)=Kb(x)-Kb(y)=(1-\varepsilon)\{Rb(x)-Rb(y)\}
 \leq(1-\varepsilon)(s-i),
\]
because the terms \(\varepsilon\zeta(b)\) cancel and \(Rb\) takes values in
\([i,s]\). Taking the supremum over \(x\) and \(y\) gives
\(s-i\leq(1-\varepsilon)(s-i)\), so \(s=i\).

\emph{Second item.} Let \(E\) be shift-invariant and \(b(x)=\mathbb P_x(E)\),
bounded and measurable. The Markov property with \(m=1\) gives
\[
 Kb(x)=\mathbb E_x\,b(X_1)=\mathbb E_x\,\mathbb E_x(\mathbf 1_E\circ\vartheta\mid\mathcal F_1)
 =\mathbb P_x(\vartheta^{-1}E)=\mathbb P_x(E)=b(x).
\]
By the first item \(b\) equals a constant \(c\), and
\(\mathbb P_\gamma(E)=\int b\,d\gamma=c\). Fix \(m\geq0\) and
\(B\in\mathcal F_m\). Since \(\vartheta^{-m}E=E\), the Markov property at
time \(m\) gives
\[
 \mathbb P_\gamma(B\cap E)=\mathbb E_\gamma\bigl[\mathbf 1_B\,
 \mathbb E_\gamma(\mathbf 1_E\circ\vartheta^m\mid\mathcal F_m)\bigr]
 =\mathbb E_\gamma[\mathbf 1_Bb(X_m)]=c\,\mathbb P_\gamma(B).
\]
The \(\mathcal F_m\) increase and generate the path sigma-algebra. By
\cref{thm:p15-finite-coordinate-approximation} there are
\(B_j\in\mathcal F_{m_j}\) with \(\mathbb P_\gamma(B_j\triangle E)\to0\).
Then \(\mathbb P_\gamma(B_j)\to\mathbb P_\gamma(E)=c\) and
\(\mathbb P_\gamma(B_j\cap E)\to\mathbb P_\gamma(E)=c\), since each
difference is at most \(\mathbb P_\gamma(B_j\triangle E)\). Passing to the
limit in \(\mathbb P_\gamma(B_j\cap E)=c\,\mathbb P_\gamma(B_j)\) gives
\(c=c^2\), so \(c\in\{0,1\}\). This is the first clause of (E), and the
second clause is the assumption \(\mu\ll\gamma\).

\emph{Fourth item.} If \(\varepsilon=1\), the first item gives
\(K(x,\cdot)=\zeta\) for every \(x\), hence \(\gamma=\gamma K=\zeta\) and
\(K^kf(x)=\gamma(K^{k-1}f)=\gamma f=0\) for \(k\geq1\); the bound holds
because its right side is zero for \(k\geq1\) and equality holds at
\(k=0\). Let \(\varepsilon<1\) and use \(R\) from the first item.

Step 1. For all \(k\), \(x\) and \(A\),
\((1-\varepsilon)^kR^k(x,A)\leq K^k(x,A)\). This holds at \(k=0\). If it
holds at \(k\), then
\[
\begin{split}
 (1-\varepsilon)^{k+1}R^{k+1}(x,A)
 &=\int(1-\varepsilon)R(x,dy)\,(1-\varepsilon)^kR^k(y,A)\\
 &\leq\int K(x,dy)\,K^k(y,A),
\end{split}
\]
because \((1-\varepsilon)R(x,\cdot)\leq K(x,\cdot)\) as measures. Hence
\(\gamma R^k\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-k}\gamma K^k=(1-\varepsilon)^{-k}\gamma\),
and by monotone convergence through simple functions,
\(\gamma(R^kv)\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{-k}\gamma(v)\) for measurable
\(v\geq0\).

Step 2. Let \(v\geq0\) be measurable with \(\gamma v<\infty\). There are
finite constants \(c_k(v)\geq0\) with
\(K^kv(x)=(1-\varepsilon)^kR^kv(x)+c_k(v)\) for every \(x\), as an
identity in \([0,\infty]\). At \(k=0\), \(c_0=0\). If it holds at \(k\),
the row decomposition of \(K\) gives
\[
\begin{split}
 K^{k+1}v(x)&=(1-\varepsilon)\int R(x,dy)K^kv(y)+\varepsilon\zeta(K^kv)\\
 &=(1-\varepsilon)^{k+1}R^{k+1}v(x)+(1-\varepsilon)c_k(v)+\varepsilon\zeta(K^kv).
\end{split}
\]
Set \(c_{k+1}(v)=(1-\varepsilon)c_k(v)+\varepsilon\zeta(K^kv)\). It is
finite because, by the third item and invariance,
\(\varepsilon\zeta(K^kv)\leq\gamma(K^kv)=\gamma(v)\).

Step 3. Let \(f\in L^2(\gamma)\subseteq L^1(\gamma)\) with \(\gamma f=0\).
Apply Step 2 to \(f^+\) and \(f^-\). By Step 1 and invariance,
\(R^kf^\pm\) and \(K^kf^\pm\) are \(\gamma\)-integrable, hence finite off a
\(\gamma\)-null set. There
\(K^kf=(1-\varepsilon)^kR^kf+c_k\) with
\(c_k=c_k(f^+)-c_k(f^-)\). Integrating against \(\gamma\) and using
\(\gamma(K^kf)=\gamma f=0\) gives
\(c_k=-(1-\varepsilon)^k\gamma(R^kf)\), so
\[
 K^kf=(1-\varepsilon)^k\bigl\{R^kf-\gamma(R^kf)\bigr\}
 \qquad\gamma\text{-almost everywhere.}
\]

Step 4. For \(v\in L^2(\gamma)\),
\(\gamma\{(v-\gamma v)^2\}=\gamma(v^2)-(\gamma v)^2\leq\gamma(v^2)\).
For each \(x\),
the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality for \(R^k(x,\cdot)\) gives
\((R^kf)^2\leq R^k(f^2)\). With Step 1,
\[
 \|K^kf\|^2_{L^2(\gamma)}\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}\gamma\bigl(R^k(f^2)\bigr)
 \leq(1-\varepsilon)^{2k}(1-\varepsilon)^{-k}\gamma(f^2)
 =(1-\varepsilon)^k\|f\|^2_{L^2(\gamma)} .
\]
Taking square roots proves the fourth item.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-ergodic}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-ergodic-averages}{The maximal inequality, the ergodic theorem and the transfer}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 0: the shift preserves \(\mathbb P_\gamma\).} By the Markov
property recorded at the start of
\cref{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin}, for every path event \(A\),
\[
 \mathbb P_\gamma(\vartheta^{-1}A)=\int\mathbb P_x(A)\,\gamma K(dx)
 =\int\mathbb P_x(A)\,\gamma(dx)=\mathbb P_\gamma(A).
\]

\emph{Step 1: maximal inequality.} Let \(\vartheta\) preserve a probability
\(\mathbb P\) on \((\Omega,\mathcal F)\), let \(G\) be integrable and real,
\(G_j=G\circ\vartheta^j\), \(S_k=G_0+\cdots+G_{k-1}\) and
\(M_k=\max(0,S_1,\ldots,S_k)\). We show \(\mathbb E(G;M_k>0)\geq0\). For
\(1\leq j\leq k\), \(M_k\circ\vartheta\geq S_j\circ\vartheta\), and
\(G+S_j\circ\vartheta=S_{j+1}\). So \(G\geq S_{j+1}-M_k\circ\vartheta\) for
\(j=1,\ldots,k-1\), and \(G=S_1\geq S_1-M_k\circ\vartheta\) because
\(M_k\circ\vartheta\geq0\). Hence
\(G\geq\max(S_1,\ldots,S_k)-M_k\circ\vartheta\). On \(\{M_k>0\}\) the
maximum equals \(M_k\), so
\[
 \mathbb E(G;M_k>0)\geq\mathbb E(M_k-M_k\circ\vartheta;M_k>0)
 \geq\mathbb E(M_k-M_k\circ\vartheta)=0 .
\]
The second inequality holds because \(M_k-M_k\circ\vartheta=-M_k\circ\vartheta\leq0\)
on \(\{M_k=0\}\). The final equality holds because
\(0\leq M_k\leq\sum_{j<k}|G_j|\) is integrable and \(\vartheta\) preserves
\(\mathbb P\). This is the route of
\cite[Lemma~6.2.2, p.~335]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}.

\emph{Step 2: the ergodic case.} Suppose in addition that every
\(A\in\mathcal F\) with \(\vartheta^{-1}A=A\) has probability \(0\) or
\(1\). We show \(S_n/n\to\mathbb EG\) almost surely. Replacing \(G\) by
\(G-\mathbb EG\), assume \(\mathbb EG=0\). Let
\(\bar G=\limsup_nS_n/n\in[-\infty,\infty]\). Since
\(S_n\circ\vartheta=S_{n+1}-G\), \(G\) is finite and \((n+1)/n\to1\),
\(\bar G\circ\vartheta=\limsup_n(S_{n+1}-G)/n=\bar G\). Fix \(\epsilon>0\)
and put \(D=\{\bar G>\epsilon\}\); then \(\vartheta^{-1}D=D\). Let
\(G'=(G-\epsilon)\mathbf 1_D\). As \(\mathbf 1_D\circ\vartheta^j=\mathbf 1_D\),
the partial sums of \(G'\) are \(S'_k=(S_k-k\epsilon)\mathbf 1_D\). With
\(M'_k\) formed from them, the events \(F_k=\{M'_k>0\}\) increase to
\(D\cap\{\sup_k(S_k/k-\epsilon)>0\}=D\), because on \(D\) infinitely many
\(S_k/k\) exceed \(\epsilon\). Step 1 gives \(\mathbb E(G';F_k)\geq0\).
Since \(|G'|\leq|G|+\epsilon\), dominated convergence gives
\(\mathbb E(G';D)\geq0\), that is
\(\mathbb E(G;D)\geq\epsilon\,\mathbb P(D)\). If \(\mathbb P(D)=1\), this
reads \(0=\mathbb EG\geq\epsilon\), which is false; so \(\mathbb P(D)=0\).
Letting \(\epsilon\downarrow0\) through rationals,
\(\limsup S_n/n\leq0\) almost surely. Applied to \(-G\), this gives
\(\liminf S_n/n\geq0\). Compare
\cite[Theorem~6.2.1, pp.~335--336]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019}, whose proof
reduces to this one when the invariant sigma-algebra is trivial.

\emph{Step 3: the stationary chain.} Take the path space,
\(\mathbb P=\mathbb P_\gamma\) (preserved by Step 0) and \(G=F\). The
first clause of (E) is the hypothesis of Step 2, so the event
\[
 E_F=\Bigl\{M^{-1}\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{M-1}F\circ\vartheta^t\to\mathbb E_\gamma F\Bigr\}
\]
has \(\mathbb P_\gamma(E_F)=1\). With \(F(\omega)=f(\omega_0)\),
\(\mathbb E_\gamma|F|=\gamma|f|\) and \(F\circ\vartheta^t=f(X_t)\), which
gives the statement for \(\bar f_M\).

\emph{Step 4: transfer under (E).} Let \(b(x)=\mathbb P_x(E_F)\in[0,1]\).
Since \(\int(1-b)\,d\gamma=1-\mathbb P_\gamma(E_F)=0\), \(b=1\) off a
\(\gamma\)-null set \(N\). As \(\mu\ll\gamma\), \(\mu(N)=0\) and
\(\mathbb P_\mu(E_F)=\int b\,d\mu=1\). This proves the first item.

\emph{Step 5: every initial law under (D).} The event \(E_F\) is
shift-invariant. Indeed, with \(a_M(\omega)=\sum_{t<M}F(\vartheta^t\omega)\)
and \(c=\mathbb E_\gamma F\), \(\vartheta\omega\in E_F\) means
\((a_{M+1}(\omega)-F(\omega))/M\to c\). Since \(F(\omega)\) is finite and
\((M+1)/M\to1\), this holds exactly when \(a_{M+1}(\omega)/(M+1)\to c\),
that is, when \(\omega\in E_F\). Under (D),
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin} gives the first clause of (E), so
Step 3 gives \(\mathbb P_\gamma(E_F)=1\), and its second item gives
\(\mathbb P_x(E_F)=\mathbb P_\gamma(E_F)=1\) for every \(x\). Therefore
\(\mathbb P_\mu(E_F)=\int\mathbb P_x(E_F)\,\mu(dx)=1\) for every
\(\mu\).

\emph{Step 6: stationary expectations.} Under \(\mathbb P_\gamma\), \(X_t\)
has law \(\gamma K^t=\gamma\), so \(\mathbb E_\gamma f(X_t)=\gamma f\) for
each \(t\), and averaging gives the third item. No step used a second
moment.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-mgclt}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-martingale-clt}{Truncated products of characteristic factors}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: a factorization.} For complex \(z\) with \(|z|\leq1/2\), let
\(\operatorname{Log}(1+z)=\sum_{k\geq1}(-1)^{k+1}z^k/k\); then
\(\exp\{\operatorname{Log}(1+z)\}=1+z\) by
\cref{thm:p15-criteria-complex-log}. For real \(x\) with
\(|x|\leq1/2\), \(z=ix\) has \(z^2=-x^2\), so
\(\operatorname{Log}(1+ix)=ix+x^2/2-r(x)\) with
\(r(x)=-\sum_{k\geq3}(-1)^{k+1}(ix)^k/k\). Hence
\[
 e^{ix}=(1+ix)\exp\{-x^2/2+r(x)\},\qquad
 |r(x)|\leq\sum_{k\geq3}\frac{|x|^k}3=\frac{|x|^3}{3(1-|x|)}\leq|x|^3 .
\]

\emph{Step 2: truncation.} Put
\(Y'_{n,j}=Y_{n,j}\mathbf 1\{\sum_{i<j}Y_{n,i}^2\leq2\}\). The indicator is
\(\mathcal F_{n,j-1}\)-measurable, so the \(Y'_{n,j}\) are square-integrable
martingale differences for the same sigma-algebras. If
\(\sum_jY_{n,j}^2\leq2\), then \(Y'_{n,j}=Y_{n,j}\) for every \(j\); by the
third premise, \(\mathbb P(Y'_{n,j}\neq Y_{n,j}\text{ for some }j)\to0\).
Hence, with \(S_n=\sum_jY_{n,j}\) and \(S'_n=\sum_jY'_{n,j}\),
\(S_n-S'_n\to0\) in probability, \(\max_j|Y'_{n,j}|\to0\) in probability
and \(\sum_jY'^2_{n,j}\to1\) in probability.

\emph{Step 3: the product.} Fix real \(t\) and put
\(T_n=\prod_j(1+itY'_{n,j})\). The set of \(j\) with
\(\sum_{i<j}Y_{n,i}^2\leq2\) is an initial segment \(\{1,\ldots,J\}\),
with \(J\geq1\). So \(Y'_{n,j}=Y_{n,j}\) for \(j\leq J\) and \(0\)
otherwise, and since \(1+a\leq e^a\),
\[
 |T_n|^2=\prod_{j\leq J}(1+t^2Y_{n,j}^2)
 \leq e^{t^2\sum_{j<J}Y_{n,j}^2}(1+t^2Y_{n,J}^2)
 \leq e^{2t^2}\bigl(1+t^2\max_jY_{n,j}^2\bigr).
\]
By the first premise, \(\sup_n\mathbb E|T_n|^2\leq C_t<\infty\). Next let
\(P_j=\prod_{i\leq j}(1+itY'_{n,i})\), \(P_0=1\). Then
\(P_j-P_{j-1}=it\,V_{j-1}Y_{n,j}\), where
\(V_{j-1}=P_{j-1}\mathbf 1\{\sum_{i<j}Y_{n,i}^2\leq2\}\) is
\(\mathcal F_{n,j-1}\)-measurable. On the event in that indicator,
\(Y'_{n,i}=Y_{n,i}\) for \(i<j\) and
\(|P_{j-1}|^2\leq e^{t^2\sum_{i<j}Y_{n,i}^2}\leq e^{2t^2}\), so
\(V_{j-1}\) is bounded. Taking real and imaginary parts,
\(\mathbb E[V_{j-1}Y_{n,j}]=\mathbb E[V_{j-1}\mathbb E(Y_{n,j}\mid\mathcal F_{n,j-1})]=0\).
Each \(P_j\) is integrable by the same bound as \(T_n\). Hence
\(\mathbb EP_j=\mathbb EP_{j-1}\), and \(\mathbb ET_n=1\).

\emph{Step 4: the remaining factor.} Since \(|1+itY'_{n,j}|\geq1\),
\(T_n\neq0\). Put \(U_n=e^{itS'_n}/T_n\), so \(|T_nU_n|=1\). On
\(G_n=\{\max_j|tY'_{n,j}|\leq1/2\}\), Step 1 gives
\[
\begin{gathered}
 U_n=\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{t^2}2\sum_jY'^2_{n,j}+\sum_jr(tY'_{n,j})\Bigr\},\\
 \Bigl|\sum_jr(tY'_{n,j})\Bigr|\leq|t|^3\max_j|Y'_{n,j}|\sum_jY'^2_{n,j}.
\end{gathered}
\]
By Step 2, \(\mathbb P(G_n)\to1\), the sum of squares tends to one and the
remainder to zero in probability. Continuity of the exponential gives
\(U_n\to e^{-t^2/2}\) in probability.

\emph{Step 5: the limit.} Write
\(\mathbb Ee^{itS'_n}=\mathbb E[T_nU_n]=e^{-t^2/2}\mathbb ET_n+\mathbb EV_n
=e^{-t^2/2}+\mathbb EV_n\), where \(V_n=T_n(U_n-e^{-t^2/2})\) satisfies
\(|V_n|\leq1+|T_n|\). For \(\kappa,\eta>0\),
\(\mathbb P(|V_n|>\eta)\leq C_t/\kappa^2+\mathbb P(|U_n-e^{-t^2/2}|>\eta/\kappa)\),
so \(V_n\to0\) in probability. Then, by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality,
\[
 \mathbb E|V_n|\leq\eta+\mathbb E\bigl[(1+|T_n|)\mathbf 1\{|V_n|>\eta\}\bigr]
 \leq\eta+\mathbb P(|V_n|>\eta)+C_t^{1/2}\mathbb P(|V_n|>\eta)^{1/2},
\]
whose upper limit is \(\eta\). As \(\eta\) is arbitrary,
\(\mathbb Ee^{itS'_n}\to e^{-t^2/2}\), the standard normal transform of
\cref{thm:p15-characteristic-normal-transform}. The continuity theorem
\cref{thm:p15-characteristic-continuity-theorem} gives
\(S'_n\Rightarrow\operatorname{normal}(0,1)\), and
\cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} with \(S_n-S'_n\to0\) in probability gives
the same limit for \(S_n\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-chainclt}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-chain-clt}{Poisson equation, martingale part and an arbitrary start}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: \(K\) on \(L^2(\gamma)\).} For \(v\in L^2(\gamma)\),
\(\gamma(K|v|)=\gamma|v|<\infty\), so \(Kv\) is finite off a
\(\gamma\)-null set; set it to zero there. A \(\gamma\)-null set \(N\) has
\(\int K(x,N)\gamma(dx)=\gamma(N)=0\), so \(K\) respects
\(\gamma\)-almost-everywhere equality. Cauchy--Schwarz for
\(K(x,\cdot)\) gives \((Kv)^2\leq K(v^2)\), so
\(\gamma((Kv)^2)\leq\gamma(K(v^2))=\gamma(v^2)\): \(K\) is a linear
contraction of \(L^2(\gamma)\), and \(\gamma(Kv)=\gamma v\).

\emph{Step 2: the Poisson equation.} Let \(r=(1-\varepsilon)^{1/2}<1\).
By \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin},
\(\|K^kf^{\rm c}\|\leq r^k\|f^{\rm c}\|\), so the series \(u_f\) converges
absolutely in the complete space \(L^2(\gamma)\). As \(K\) is continuous
and linear, \(Ku_f=\sum_{k\geq1}K^kf^{\rm c}\), hence
\(u_f-Ku_f=f^{\rm c}\).

\emph{Step 3: the variance.} From \(u_f=f^{\rm c}+Ku_f\),
\(\gamma(u_f^2)=\gamma((f^{\rm c})^2)+2\gamma(f^{\rm c}Ku_f)+\gamma((Ku_f)^2)\),
so
\(\sigma^2_K(f)=\operatorname{Var}_\gamma f+2\sum_{k\geq1}\gamma(f^{\rm c}K^kf^{\rm c})\)
by continuity of the inner product. Each term satisfies
\(|\gamma(f^{\rm c}K^kf^{\rm c})|\leq\|f^{\rm c}\|\,\|K^kf^{\rm c}\|\leq r^k\operatorname{Var}_\gamma f\),
which proves absolute convergence and the bound
\(1+2r/(1-r)=(1+r)/(1-r)\). Step 1 gives \(\sigma^2_K(f)\geq0\). If \(v(X_t)\) is
\(\mathbb P_\mu\)-integrable, then
\(\mathbb E_\mu(v(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=Kv(X_{t-1})\): this is
\cite[Theorem~5.2.1, p.~274]{DurrettPTEAuthor2019} for indicators, extended
to nonnegative \(v\) by monotone convergence and then to \(v^\pm\).
Iterating, \(\mathbb E_\mu(v(X_k)\mid\mathcal F_0)=K^kv(X_0)\). With Cauchy--Schwarz for integrability,
\(\operatorname{Cov}_{\mathbb P_\gamma}(f(X_0),f(X_k))
=\mathbb E_\gamma[f^{\rm c}(X_0)K^kf^{\rm c}(X_0)]=\gamma(f^{\rm c}K^kf^{\rm c})\).

\emph{Step 4: initial laws with \(\mu\leq c\gamma\).} Write \(u=u_f\), with
the versions of Step 1. Let \(N\) be the \(\gamma\)-null set where
\(u-Ku\neq f^{\rm c}\). Under \(\mathbb P_\mu\), \(X_t\) has law
\(\mu K^t\leq c\gamma K^t=c\gamma\), so almost surely no \(X_t\) lies in
\(N\). For \(t\geq1\) put \(\xi_t=u(X_t)-Ku(X_{t-1})\). Summation by
parts gives, almost surely,
\[
 \sum_{t=0}^{M-1}f^{\rm c}(X_t)=u(X_0)-Ku(X_{M-1})+\sum_{t=1}^{M-1}\xi_t .
\]
The pair \((X_{t-1},X_t)\) has law \(\mu K^{t-1}(dx)K(x,dy)\leq
c\,\gamma(dx)K(x,dy)\), the \(\mathbb P_\gamma\)-law of \((X_0,X_1)\). So
for measurable \(\phi\geq0\),
\(\mathbb E_\mu\phi(X_{t-1},X_t)\leq c\,\mathbb E_\gamma\phi(X_0,X_1)\);
in particular \(\xi_t\) is square-integrable. By the conditional
expectation rule of Step 3, \(\mathbb E_\mu(u(X_t)\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=Ku(X_{t-1})\),
so \(\mathbb E_\mu(\xi_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})=0\). Also
\(\mathbb E_\gamma[u(X_1)Ku(X_0)]=\gamma((Ku)^2)\), so
\(\mathbb E_\gamma\xi_1^2=\gamma(u^2)-\gamma((Ku)^2)=\sigma^2\), where
\(\sigma^2=\sigma^2_K(f)\).

The boundary terms are negligible. First, \(u(X_0)/\sqrt M\to0\)
surely. Second,
\[
 M^{-1}\mathbb E_\mu\bigl(Ku(X_{M-1})\bigr)^2\leq c\gamma(u^2)/M\to0,
\]
so \(Ku(X_{M-1})/\sqrt M\to0\) in probability by Chebyshev's inequality.

If \(\sigma^2=0\), then \(\xi_1=0\) \(\mathbb P_\gamma\)-almost surely and
\(\mathbb P_\mu(\xi_t\neq0)\leq c\,\mathbb P_\gamma(\xi_1\neq0)=0\), so
\(\sqrt M(\bar f_M-\gamma f)\) consists of the boundary terms and tends to
zero in probability, the law \(\operatorname{normal}(0,0)\).

If \(\sigma^2>0\), apply \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-martingale-clt} to
\(Y_{M,j}=\xi_j/(\sigma\sqrt M)\), \(1\leq j\leq M-1\), with
\(\mathcal F_{M,j}=\mathcal F_j\). First premise:
\(\mathbb E_\mu\max_jY_{M,j}^2\leq\sum_j\mathbb E_\mu Y_{M,j}^2\leq c\).
Second premise: for \(\eta>0\),
\[
\begin{split}
 \mathbb P_\mu\bigl(\max_jY_{M,j}^2>\eta\bigr)
 &\leq\sum_{j<M}\mathbb P_\mu(\xi_j^2>\eta\sigma^2M)
 \leq cM\,\mathbb P_\gamma(\xi_1^2>\eta\sigma^2M)\\
 &\leq\frac c{\eta\sigma^2}\mathbb E_\gamma\bigl[\xi_1^2;\xi_1^2>\eta\sigma^2M\bigr],
\end{split}
\]
which tends to zero by dominated convergence. Third premise: the path
function \(\Phi(\omega)=(u(\omega_1)-Ku(\omega_0))^2\) is finite,
\(\mathbb E_\gamma\Phi=\sigma^2\), and \(\xi_j^2=\Phi\circ\vartheta^{j-1}\).
By \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin}, (D) gives (E) for
\(\mu\ll\gamma\); \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-ergodic-averages} then gives
\(\sum_jY_{M,j}^2=\{(M-1)/M\}\{\sigma^2(M-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\xi_j^2\to1\)
almost surely. Hence \(\sum_jY_{M,j}\Rightarrow\operatorname{normal}(0,1)\);
multiplying by \(\sigma\) and adding the boundary terms,
\cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} gives
\(\sqrt M(\bar f_M-\gamma f)\Rightarrow\operatorname{normal}(0,\sigma^2)\).

\emph{Step 5: an arbitrary initial law.} For \(k\geq1\) we write
\(\mu K^k=(1-\varepsilon)^k\rho_k+\lambda_k\), with \(\rho_k\) a probability
and \(\lambda_k\) a measure of mass \(1-(1-\varepsilon)^k\) satisfying
\(\lambda_k\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\gamma\). If \(\varepsilon=1\), take
\(\lambda_k=\gamma\), since \(K(x,\cdot)=\gamma\) for every \(x\) by the
proof of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin}. If \(\varepsilon<1\), take
\(\rho_k=\mu R^k\), \(\lambda_1=\varepsilon\zeta\) and
\(\lambda_{k+1}=\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)^k\zeta+\lambda_kK\). The identity
holds at \(k=1\) because \(K=(1-\varepsilon)R+\varepsilon\zeta\) row by row.
If it holds at \(k\), then, using
\(\mu R^kK=(1-\varepsilon)\mu R^{k+1}+\varepsilon\zeta\),
\[
 \mu K^{k+1}=(1-\varepsilon)^k\mu R^kK+\lambda_kK
 =(1-\varepsilon)^{k+1}\mu R^{k+1}+\lambda_{k+1}.
\]
The mass of \(\lambda_k\) follows by comparing total masses. For the bound,
\(\lambda_k\leq a_k\gamma\) with \(a_1=1\) by the third item of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-doeblin}; and
\(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)^k\zeta\leq(1-\varepsilon)^k\gamma\),
\(\lambda_kK\leq a_k\gamma K=a_k\gamma\), so one may take
\(a_{k+1}=a_k+(1-\varepsilon)^k\), giving
\(a_k=\sum_{j<k}(1-\varepsilon)^j\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\).

Fix real \(t\) and \(k\geq1\). For \(M>k\) put
\[
 \varphi_M(\omega)=\exp\Bigl\{itM^{-1/2}\sum_{s=0}^{M-k-1}f^{\rm c}(\omega_s)\Bigr\},
\]
so that
\[
 e^{it\sqrt M(\bar f_M-\gamma f)}=e^{itM^{-1/2}\sum_{s<k}f^{\rm c}(X_s)}\,
 \varphi_M\circ\vartheta^k,
\]
and \(|e^{ia}-1|\leq\min(2,|a|)\) gives
\[
\begin{split}
 &\Bigl|\mathbb E_\mu e^{it\sqrt M(\bar f_M-\gamma f)}-\mathbb E_\mu[\varphi_M\circ\vartheta^k]\Bigr|\\
 &\qquad\leq\mathbb E_\mu\min\Bigl\{2,|t|M^{-1/2}\Bigl|\sum_{s<k}f^{\rm c}(X_s)\Bigr|\Bigr\}\to0
\end{split}
\]
by dominated convergence, since \(f^{\rm c}\) is finite. The Markov
property gives
\(\mathbb E_\mu[\varphi_M\circ\vartheta^k]=\int\mathbb E_x\varphi_M\,\mu K^k(dx)
=(1-\varepsilon)^k\int\mathbb E_x\varphi_M\,\rho_k(dx)+\int\mathbb E_x\varphi_M\,\lambda_k(dx)\).
The normalized law \(\hat\lambda_k=\lambda_k/\{1-(1-\varepsilon)^k\}\)
satisfies \(\hat\lambda_k\leq\gamma/\{\varepsilon(1-(1-\varepsilon)^k)\}\),
so Step 4, applied with \(M-k\) terms and followed by multiplication by
\(\sqrt{(M-k)/M}\to1\) through \cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation}, gives
\(\int\mathbb E_x\varphi_M\,\hat\lambda_k(dx)\to e^{-\sigma^2t^2/2}\) by
\cref{thm:p15-characteristic-continuity-theorem,thm:p15-characteristic-normal-transform}.
Since \(|\varphi_M|=1\),
\[
 \limsup_{M\to\infty}\Bigl|\mathbb E_\mu e^{it\sqrt M(\bar f_M-\gamma f)}-e^{-\sigma^2t^2/2}\Bigr|
 \leq(1-\varepsilon)^k+(1-\varepsilon)^ke^{-\sigma^2t^2/2}\leq2(1-\varepsilon)^k .
\]
Letting \(k\to\infty\) makes the right side zero when \(\varepsilon<1\); it
is zero already when \(\varepsilon=1\). The continuity theorem gives the
normal limit for every initial law.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-depest}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-dependent-estimators}{Replacing each strong law and each central limit step}
\begin{proof}
\emph{First item.} The independent proofs use the strong law only for the
following functions, each integrable under its sampled law by those same
proofs: \(L\) under \(\Pi\)
(\cref{annex:p15.direct-evidence-mc}); \(V\), \(W\) and \(WL\) under \(Q\)
(\cref{annex:p15.importance-evidence}); \(H\) under \(P\), with mean
\(1/I\), or \(s/I\) for \(1/L\) when \(\Pi(L>0)=s\)
(\cref{annex:p15.harmonic-evidence}); the bounded functions
\(\psi_{I\pm\epsilon}\), \(\epsilon\) rational, and \(\mathbf 1\{L>0\}\)
under \(Q_\delta\), and \(\psi_{p_\delta\pm\epsilon}\) under \(P\)
(\cref{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-mixture}); \(\alpha q_1\) under \(p_2\) and
\(\alpha q_2\) under \(p_1\) (\cref{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-bridge}); and
\(B_{10}(\cdot)\) under \(P^z_0\) (\cref{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-latent}).
For each, \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-ergodic-averages} gives almost-sure
convergence of the chain average to the same mean, under (E) by its first
item and under (D) by its second. Countably many functions are involved,
so the probability-one events intersect in a probability-one event. The
remaining steps of those proofs are deterministic consequences of these
limits: a denominator with positive limit is eventually positive and the
ratio converges; for \(\widehat{\rm pr}_3\) and \(\widehat{\rm pr}_4\), the
monotone function \(\Psi_m\) changes sign between \(I-\epsilon\) and
\(I+\epsilon\), respectively \(p_\delta\pm\epsilon\), for all large \(m\),
and the existence and uniqueness of the root follow as there, with
\(N_+/m\to Q_\delta(L>0)>0\) for \(\widehat{\rm pr}_3\). For
\(\widehat{\rm pr}_4\) the chain may start at a value with \(L=0\); the
first item applied to \(\mathbf 1\{L>0\}\) under \(P\) gives
\(N_+/m\to1\) and \(N_0/m\to0\), so \((1-\delta)N_0<\delta N_+\) for all
large \(m\), and the first item of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-mixture-estimators} gives a unique root.
Functions are taken in the finite versions fixed in those proofs, with
value zero off the sets where they were defined. For the bridge, each average converges as
its own run length tends to infinity on a probability-one event of the
space carrying both chains, whatever their joint law; on the intersection
the ratio converges as \(M_1,M_2\to\infty\), because its denominator
limit \(\kappa/c_1\) is nonzero.

\emph{Second item.} Under (D) apply
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-chain-clt} with the invariant law of the
corresponding chain. For the ordinary averages take \(f=Z\in L^2(\gamma)\),
of mean \(I\). For self-normalized importance, \(U=W(L-I)\) lies in
\(L^2(Q)\) because \(v_{\rm SN}<\infty\), and
\(Q(U)=E_Q[WL]-IE_QW=0\). On \(\{D_M>0\}\), with
\(D_M=M^{-1}\sum_tW(X_t)\),
\(\sqrt M(\widetilde I_M-I)=M^{-1/2}\sum_tU(X_t)/D_M\). The numerator
tends weakly to \(\operatorname{normal}(0,\sigma^2_K(U))\) and \(D_M\to1\)
almost surely by the first item, so \cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} gives
the limit, with a fixed default where \(D_M=0\). For the reciprocal
weight, \(v_H<\infty\) puts \(H\) in \(L^2(P)\), so
\(\sqrt M(A_M-1/I)\Rightarrow\operatorname{normal}(0,\sigma^2_K(H))\); the
identity \(\sqrt M(A_M^{-1}-I)=-(I/A_M)\sqrt M(A_M-1/I)\) and
\(I/A_M\to I^2\) almost surely give variance \(I^4\sigma^2_K(H)\).

For \(\widehat{\rm pr}_3\), now the root \(\widehat p\) of
the chain sum \(\Psi_m(p)=\sum_{t<m}\psi_p(X_t)\), follow
\cref{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-mixture}. On the event that
\(\widehat p\) exists and lies in \([I/2,2I]\), whose probability tends to
one by the first item, the mean value theorem gives \(\bar p\) between
\(\widehat p\) and \(I\) with
\(\sqrt m(\widehat p-I)=-m^{-1/2}\Psi_m(I)/\{m^{-1}\Psi_m'(\bar p)\}\).
The numerator is \(\sqrt m\) times the chain average of the bounded
function \(\psi_I\), of mean \(g(I)=0\) under \(Q_\delta\), so it tends
weakly to \(\operatorname{normal}(0,\sigma^2_K(\psi_I))\). The
second-derivative bound proved there is deterministic, so
\(|m^{-1}\Psi_m'(\bar p)-m^{-1}\Psi_m'(I)|\leq8|\widehat p-I|/\{3\delta(1-\delta)I^2\}\to0\)
almost surely, and \(m^{-1}\Psi_m'(I)\to g'(I)>0\) almost surely by the
first item applied to the bounded function \(\partial_p\psi_p\) at
\(p=I\). \Cref{thm:p15-clt-perturbation} gives variance
\(\sigma^2_K(\psi_I)/g'(I)^2\). For the latent-data average, the support
condition makes the mean \(B_{10}\), and the variance criterion of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-latent-ratio} puts \(B_{10}(\cdot)\) in
\(L^2(P^z_0)\).

\emph{Third item.} Under \(\mathbb P_\gamma\) every \(X_t\) has law
\(\gamma\) (\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-ergodic-averages}, third item), so
each linear average has the expectation computed in the independent
proofs. The first item of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-two-state-chain}
gives an initial law for which the reciprocal average is biased at every
\(M\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-twostate}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-two-state-chain}{Eigenfunction calculation, independence sampler and the identity kernel}
\begin{proof}
\emph{First item.} The Metropolis acceptance probability for a move from
\(x\) to \(y\) is \(\min\{1,P(y)/P(x)\}\): it is \(1\) from the first value
to the second and \(1/3\) from the second to the first, which gives the
displayed kernel. Invariance: \(PK(\{1\})=\tfrac14\cdot0+\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac14\)
and \(PK(\{2\})=\tfrac14\cdot1+\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac34\). For (D),
every set containing the second value has \(K(x,A)\geq K(x,\{2\})\geq2/3\)
for both \(x\), and every other set has \(\zeta(A)=0\).

Here \(PH=\tfrac14\cdot4+\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac43=2=1/I\), and
\(H^{\rm c}=H-2=(2,-2/3)\). Directly,
\(KH^{\rm c}(1)=H^{\rm c}(2)=-2/3\) and
\(KH^{\rm c}(2)=\tfrac13\cdot2+\tfrac23\cdot(-\tfrac23)=\tfrac29\), so
\(KH^{\rm c}=-\tfrac13H^{\rm c}\) and \(K^kH^{\rm c}=(-1/3)^kH^{\rm c}\).
Then \(\operatorname{Var}_PH=\tfrac14\cdot4+\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac49=\tfrac43\),
and by Step 3 of \cref{annex:p15-w02b-p15-056-chainclt} the covariance at
lag \(k\) is \(P(H^{\rm c}K^kH^{\rm c})=(4/3)(-1/3)^k\). Summing,
\[
 \sigma^2_K(H)=\frac43+2\cdot\frac43\sum_{k\geq1}\Bigl(-\frac13\Bigr)^k
 =\frac43-\frac23=\frac23 .
\]
\Cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-056-dependent-estimators} gives variance
\(I^4\sigma^2_K(H)=\tfrac1{16}\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac1{24}\), and
\cref{thm:p15-evidence-mc-clt} gives
\(I^4\operatorname{Var}_PH=\tfrac1{16}\cdot\tfrac43=\tfrac1{12}\) for
independent draws. Started at the first value, \(X_t\) has law
\(\delta_1K^t\), so
\(\mathbb E_1H(X_t)=K^tH(1)=2+K^tH^{\rm c}(1)=2+2(-1/3)^t\). Averaging,
\[
 \mathbb E_1A_M=2+\frac2M\sum_{t<M}\Bigl(-\frac13\Bigr)^t
 =2+\frac3{2M}\Bigl\{1-\Bigl(-\frac13\Bigr)^M\Bigr\},
\]
which exceeds \(2\) because \(|(-1/3)^M|<1\).

\emph{Second item.} Since \(0\leq\alpha\leq1\) and \(\int q=1\),
\(\rho(x)\in[0,1]\) and \(K(x,\cdot)\) is a probability. As
\(p\leq w^*q\), \(p=0\) where \(q=0\), \(w\leq w^*\) everywhere, and
\(w^*\geq\int p=1\). Minorization: if \(w(x)=0\), then
\(K(x,A)\geq\int_Aq\geq\int_Ap/w^*\). If \(w(x)>0\) and \(q(y)>0\), then
\(\alpha(x,y)\geq\min\{1,w(y)/w^*\}=w(y)/w^*\), so
\(q(y)\alpha(x,y)\geq p(y)/w^*\); where \(q(y)=0\) also \(p(y)=0\). Hence
\(K(x,A)\geq w^{*-1}\int_Ap\) for every \(x\).

Invariance: put \(m(x,y)=\min\{p(x)q(y),p(y)q(x)\}\), symmetric. If
\(p(x)>0\), then \(q(x)>0\), and \(p(x)q(y)\alpha(x,y)=m(x,y)\): for
\(q(y)>0\) because \(w(y)/w(x)=p(y)q(x)/\{q(y)p(x)\}\), and for \(q(y)=0\)
because both sides vanish. Points with \(p(x)=0\) carry no mass, and there
\(m(x,y)=0\). By Tonelli's theorem,
\[
 \int p(x)K(x,A)\,dx=\int_A\int m(x,y)\,dx\,dy+\int_Ap(x)\rho(x)\,dx .
\]
For \(p(y)>0\), symmetry gives
\(\int m(x,y)\,dx=p(y)\int q(x)\alpha(y,x)\,dx=p(y)\{1-\rho(y)\}\); for
\(p(y)=0\) both sides are zero. The total is \(\int_Ap\).

\emph{Third item.} Under the identity kernel \(K(x,A)=\mathbf 1_A(x)\),
(D) would give \(\varepsilon\zeta(\{1\})\leq K(2,\{1\})=0\) and
\(\varepsilon\zeta(\{2\})\leq K(1,\{2\})=0\), so \(\varepsilon\zeta(\Theta)=0\),
contradicting \(\varepsilon>0\). The event
\(E=\{\omega_t=1\text{ for all large }t\}\) satisfies
\(\vartheta^{-1}E=E\). The finite-dimensional distributions make the path
constant \(\mathbb P_x\)-almost surely, so
\(\mathbb P_P(E)=P(\{1\})=1/4\), and (E) fails.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}
