\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-finite-calculus}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-finite-calculus}{The finite frequency calculus}
\begin{proof}
Put \(n=N(\alpha)>0\). All classes below are subclasses of the finite
class \(\alpha\), so their numbers are finite, and product classes obey
\((\alpha.\beta).\gamma=\alpha.(\beta.\gamma)\).

Item~1. Every element of \(\alpha\) lies in exactly one of
\(\alpha.\beta\) and \(\alpha.\bar\beta\), so
\(N(\alpha.\beta)+N(\alpha.\bar\beta)=n\). Dividing by \(n\) gives the claim.

Item~2. If \(N(\alpha.\beta)>0\), then
\[
 {}_{\alpha}F''(\beta)\cdot{}_{\alpha.\beta}F''(\gamma)
 =\frac{N(\alpha.\beta)}{n}\cdot\frac{N(\alpha.\beta.\gamma)}{N(\alpha.\beta)}
 =\frac{N(\alpha.\beta.\gamma)}{n}={}_{\alpha}F''(\beta.\gamma).
\]
The second form is the same computation with \(\gamma\) in place of
\(\beta\), using \(N(\alpha.\gamma)>0\) and \(\gamma.\beta=\beta.\gamma\).

Item~3. Both forms of item~2 apply. Suppose \(\gamma\) is independent of
\(\beta\) within \(\alpha\), that is
\({}_{\alpha.\beta}F''(\gamma)={}_{\alpha}F''(\gamma)\). Substituting in the
first form of item~2 gives the special multiplication theorem.
Conversely, from the special multiplication theorem and the first form,
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\beta)\cdot{}_{\alpha.\beta}F''(\gamma)
={}_{\alpha}F''(\beta)\cdot{}_{\alpha}F''(\gamma)\); since
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\beta)=N(\alpha.\beta)/n>0\), cancelling gives
independence of \(\gamma\) from \(\beta\). The same two steps with the
second form of item~2, cancelling \({}_{\alpha}F''(\gamma)>0\), show that
independence of \(\beta\) from \(\gamma\) is also equivalent to the special
multiplication theorem. Hence all three conditions are equivalent.

Item~4. Put \(X=\alpha.\beta\) and \(Y=\alpha.\gamma\). Then
\(\alpha.(\beta+\gamma)=X\cup Y\) and \(X\cap Y=\alpha.\beta.\gamma\).
The set \(X\cup Y\) is the disjoint union of \(X\) and \(Y\setminus X\), and
\(Y\) is the disjoint union of \(Y\setminus X\) and \(X\cap Y\). Hence
\(N(X\cup Y)=N(X)+N(Y)-N(X\cap Y)\). Dividing by \(n\) gives the general
addition theorem, and \(N(\alpha.\beta.\gamma)=0\) removes the last term.

Item~5. Divide the first form of item~2 by
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\beta)>0\).

Item~6. Since \(N(\alpha.\gamma)>0\), item~5 with the roles of \(\beta\)
and \(\gamma\) exchanged gives
\({}_{\alpha.\gamma}F''(\beta_i)={}_{\alpha}F''(\beta_i.\gamma)/{}_{\alpha}F''(\gamma)\).
Since \(N(\alpha.\beta_i)>0\), item~2 gives
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\beta_i.\gamma)={}_{\alpha}F''(\beta_i)\cdot{}_{\alpha.\beta_i}F''(\gamma)\)
for the numerator. For the denominator, \(\alpha.\gamma\subset\Sigma\beta_i\)
means every element of \(\alpha.\gamma\) belongs to some \(\beta_j\), so
\(\alpha.\gamma\) is the union of the classes \(\alpha.\beta_j.\gamma\).
For \(i\neq j\) the classes \(\alpha.\beta_i.\gamma\) and
\(\alpha.\beta_j.\gamma\) are disjoint, because their intersection lies in
the empty class \(\alpha.\beta_i.\beta_j\). Hence
\(N(\alpha.\gamma)=\sum_jN(\alpha.\beta_j.\gamma)\), so
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\gamma)=\sum_j{}_{\alpha}F''(\beta_j.\gamma)
=\sum_j{}_{\alpha.\beta_j}F''(\gamma)\cdot{}_{\alpha}F''(\beta_j)\),
by item~2 for each \(j\). Substituting both expressions gives the formula.

Item~7. If \(\gamma\subset\beta\), then \(\alpha.\beta.\gamma=\alpha.\gamma\)
and \(\alpha.\beta\) is the disjoint union of \(\alpha.\gamma\) and
\(\alpha.\beta.\bar\gamma\). Hence
\(N(\alpha.\beta)=N(\alpha.\gamma)+N(\alpha.\beta.\bar\gamma)\geq N(\alpha.\gamma)\),
with equality exactly when \(N(\alpha.\beta.\bar\gamma)=0\). Divide by \(n\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-sym}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-triple-symmetry}{Five buttons}
\begin{proof}
The description fixes the buttons up to naming. Call the round ones
\(b_1\) (black, not large), \(b_2\) (black, large), \(b_3\) (not black,
large) and \(b_4\) (neither). These are the four round buttons, with two
black, two large and one both. Call the square one \(b_5\) (black,
large). Then
\(\alpha=\{b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4\}\), \(\beta=\{b_1,b_2,b_5\}\) and
\(\gamma=\{b_2,b_3,b_5\}\). So \(\alpha.\beta=\{b_1,b_2\}\),
\(\alpha.\gamma=\{b_2,b_3\}\), \(\alpha.\beta.\gamma=\{b_2\}\) and
\(\beta.\gamma=\{b_2,b_5\}\).

Within \(\alpha\):
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\gamma)=2/4=1/2\) and
\({}_{\alpha.\beta}F''(\gamma)=N(\{b_2\})/N(\{b_1,b_2\})=1/2\). So
\(\gamma\) is independent of \(\beta\) within \(\alpha\). Because
\(N(\alpha.\beta)\) and \(N(\alpha.\gamma)\) are positive, item~3 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-finite-calculus} gives mutual independence.

Within \(\beta\): \(\beta.\alpha=\alpha.\beta\), so
\({}_{\beta.\alpha}F''(\gamma)={}_{\alpha.\beta}F''(\gamma)=1/2\), while
\({}_{\beta}F''(\gamma)=N(\beta.\gamma)/N(\beta)=2/3\). The two differ, so
\(\gamma\) is not insensitive within \(\beta\) to selection of
\(\alpha\)-elements.

Finally, if \(\beta\subset\alpha\) then \(\beta.\alpha=\beta\), and the two
sides of \({}_{\beta.\alpha}F''(\gamma)={}_{\beta}F''(\gamma)\) are the same
quotient \(N(\beta.\gamma)/N(\beta)\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial}{Cyclic counts, Schiff's reduction and the binomial formula}
\begin{proof}
Two counting identities are used. Let \(\tau\) be a word of length
\(k\geq1\) and \(r\geq1\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(L)] \(c_a(\tau)=\sum_{\pi}c_a(\pi\tau)\), the sum over all words
  \(\pi\) of length \(r\).
\item[(R)] \(c_a(\tau)=\sum_{\pi}c_a(\tau\pi)\), the sum over all words
  \(\pi\) of length \(r\).
\end{enumerate}
For (L), an occurrence of \(\tau\) starting at position \(i\) determines
the word \(\pi=a_{i-r}\cdots a_{i-1}\) (indices modulo \(N\)), and then
\(\pi\tau\) occurs at \(i-r\). Conversely an occurrence of \(\pi\tau\) at
\(j\) gives an occurrence of \(\tau\) at \(j+r\). These maps are mutually
inverse, so occurrences of \(\tau\) correspond one to one with pairs of a
word \(\pi\) and an occurrence of \(\pi\tau\). (R) is proved in the same
way with the \(r\) terms after the occurrence. In particular (R) with
\(r=1\) gives \(c_a(\tau)=c_a(\tau0)+c_a(\tau1)\), so \(c_a(\tau b)\leq
c_a(\tau)\) for \(b\in\{0,1\}\). Also \(c_a(1)+c_a(0)=N\), so \(p+q=1\).

Item~1. The hypothesis is the defining condition for words of length
\(n\). Let \(\tau\) have length \(j<n\) and \(c_a(\tau)>0\). By (L) with
\(r=n-j\),
\(c_a(\tau)=\sum_\pi c_a(\pi\tau)\) and
\(c_a(\tau1)=\sum_\pi c_a(\pi\tau1)\), both sums over words \(\pi\) of
length \(n-j\). Each \(\pi\tau\) has length \(n\). If
\(c_a(\pi\tau)>0\), the hypothesis gives
\(c_a(\pi\tau1)=p\,c_a(\pi\tau)\). If \(c_a(\pi\tau)=0\), then
\(c_a(\pi\tau1)\leq c_a(\pi\tau)=0\), so again
\(c_a(\pi\tau1)=p\,c_a(\pi\tau)\). Summing over \(\pi\) gives
\(c_a(\tau1)=p\,c_a(\tau)\). Hence \(a\) is cyclically \(n\)-free.

Item~2. We show by induction on \(k\), for \(1\leq k\leq n\), that every
word \(w\) of length \(k\) with \(m\) ones has \(c_a(w)=Np^mq^{k-m}\).
For \(k=1\), \(c_a(1)=Np\) and \(c_a(0)=Nq\) by the definition of \(p,q\).
Let \(1\leq k\leq n-1\), assume the claim for length \(k\), and let
\(w=\tau b\) with \(\tau\) of length \(k\) having \(m'\) ones. Since
\(k\leq n-1\) and \(a\) is cyclically \((n-1)\)-free,
\(c_a(\tau)>0\) gives \(c_a(\tau1)=p\,c_a(\tau)\) and then, by (R),
\(c_a(\tau0)=c_a(\tau)-c_a(\tau1)=q\,c_a(\tau)\). If \(c_a(\tau)=0\), then
\(c_a(\tau b)=0=c_a(\tau)\cdot p\) and \(=c_a(\tau)\cdot q\). In either
case \(c_a(\tau1)=Np^{m'+1}q^{k-m'}\) and \(c_a(\tau0)=Np^{m'}q^{k+1-m'}\),
which is the claim for length \(k+1\). (For \(n=1\) only the base case is
needed.) Taking \(k=n\) gives
\({}_{a_{(n)}}F''(\sigma_m)=c_a(\sigma_m)/N=p^mq^{n-m}\). Each of the \(N\)
cyclic windows of length \(n\) is exactly one word, and the words of
length \(n\) with \(m\) ones are \(\binom{n}{m}\) in number, each with
\(c_a\)-value \(Np^mq^{n-m}\). So the number of windows with exactly
\(m\) ones is \(\binom{n}{m}Np^mq^{n-m}\), and dividing by \(N\) gives
the binomial formula.

Item~3. Cyclic \(n\)-freedom includes cyclic \(((n+1)-1)\)-freedom, so
item~2 with \(n+1\) in place of \(n\) and \(p=q=1/2\) gives
\(c_a(w)=N/2^{n+1}\) for every word \(w\) of length \(n+1\). This is a
nonnegative integer and positive because \(N\geq1\), so it is at least 1
and \(2^{n+1}\) divides \(N\); in particular \(N\geq2^{n+1}>n\). Let
\(b=a_0\cdots a_{N-1}a_0\cdots a_{n-1}\), so \(b_t=a_{t\bmod N}\) for
\(0\leq t\leq N+n-1\). The cyclic window of length \(n+1\) starting at
\(i\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\) consists of \(a_{(i+s)\bmod N}=b_{i+s}\) for
\(s=0,\ldots,n\), since \(i+s\leq N+n-1\). So every cyclic occurrence is
a block of \(b\), and every word of length \(n+1\), occurring cyclically
at least once, is a block of \(b\).

Item~4. Let \(N=2^{n+1}\) and each word of length \(n+1\) occur exactly
once. For a word \(w\) of length \(k\leq n+1\), (R) with \(r=n+1-k\)
(nothing to do when \(k=n+1\)) gives \(c_a(w)=2^{n+1-k}\), the number
of words \(\pi\) of length \(n+1-k\). Hence \(c_a(1)=2^n=N/2\), so
\(p=q=1/2\). For \(\tau\) of length \(j\leq n\),
\(c_a(\tau1)=2^{n-j}=\tfrac12\cdot2^{n+1-j}=p\,c_a(\tau)\), so \(a\) is
cyclically \(n\)-free.

The four periods. Their cyclic windows, starting at the first term, are
as follows. For (A), with length 2: 11, 10, 00, 01. For (B), with
length 3: 101, 011, 111, 110, 100, 000, 001, 010. For (C), with
length 4: 1011, 0110, 1100, 1000, 0000, 0001, 0011, 0111, 1111, 1110,
1101, 1010, 0100, 1001, 0010, 0101. For (D), with length 5: 01100,
11000, 10001, 00011, 00111, 01110, 11101, 11010, 10101, 01010, 10100,
01001, 10010, 00100, 01000, 10000, 00000, 00001, 00010, 00101, 01011,
10111, 01111, 11111, 11110, 11100, 11001, 10011, 00110, 01101, 11011,
10110.
In each list the words are pairwise distinct and their number is
\(2^{n+1}\) for \(n=1,2,3,4\) respectively, which is the number of all
words of that length. So each occurs exactly once, and item~4 gives
cyclic 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-freedom with \(p=1/2\). For (A) and the pair
\(\tau=11\): \(c_a(11)=1\), and its only occurrence, starting at the
first term, is followed by \(0\), so \(c_a(111)=0\neq\tfrac12\cdot1\);
(A) is not cyclically 2-free. For (B), item~2 with \(n=3\) gives
\(c_a(w)=8\cdot2^{-3}=1\) for each word of length 3, so
\({}_{a_{(3)}}F''(m)=\binom3m/8\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-bernoulli-bound}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-bernoulli-bound}{Chebyshev's bound for the binomial weights}
\begin{proof}
Write \(B(m)=\binom nm p^mq^{n-m}\) for \(0\leq m\leq n\). By the binomial
theorem \(\sum_mB(m)=(p+q)^n=1\).

First moment. For \(m\geq1\),
\(m\binom nm=n\binom{n-1}{m-1}\). Hence
\[
 \sum_{m=0}^nmB(m)=np\sum_{m=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{m-1}p^{m-1}q^{n-m}
 =np(p+q)^{n-1}=np.
\]
Second factorial moment. For \(n\geq2\) and \(m\geq2\),
\(m(m-1)\binom nm=n(n-1)\binom{n-2}{m-2}\), so
\(\sum_mm(m-1)B(m)=n(n-1)p^2(p+q)^{n-2}=n(n-1)p^2\). For \(n=1\) every
term has \(m(m-1)=0\) and the value \(n(n-1)p^2=0\) agrees.
Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \sum_m(m-np)^2B(m)
 &=\sum_m m(m-1)B(m)+\sum_m mB(m)\\
 &\qquad-2np\sum_m mB(m)+n^2p^2\sum_mB(m)\\
 &=n(n-1)p^2+np-2n^2p^2+n^2p^2=npq.
\end{aligned}
\]
If \(|m/n-p|\geq\delta\) then \((m-np)^2\geq n^2\delta^2\). All terms
being nonnegative,
\[
 n^2\delta^2\sum_{|m/n-p|\geq\delta}B(m)\leq\sum_m(m-np)^2B(m)=npq ,
\]
which is the displayed inequality after division by \(n^2\delta^2\).

Item~1. By item~2 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial}, the
proportion of cyclic \(n\)-segments with exactly \(m\) ones is \(B(m)\).
A segment is not \(\Delta p\) exactly when its \(m\) satisfies
\(|m/n-p|\geq\delta\). Summing over those \(m\) gives a proportion
\(\sum_{|m/n-p|\geq\delta}B(m)\leq pq/(n\delta^2)\).

Item~2. The properties \(m=0,\ldots,n\) of the segments are mutually
exclusive and exhaust them, and \(\Delta p\) is the union of those
\(m\) with \(|m/n-p|<\delta\). On a finite initial part of the segment
sequence, the relative frequency of \(\Delta p\) is the sum of the
relative frequencies of those \(m\). Formula (3) asserts that each
summand converges to \(B(m)\), so the sum converges to
\(\sum_{|m/n-p|<\delta}B(m)=1-\sum_{|m/n-p|\geq\delta}B(m)\geq1-pq/(n\delta^2)\).
A relative frequency is at most 1, so
\(1-pq/(n\delta^2)\leq{}_{\alpha_n}F(\Delta p)\leq1\) for every \(n\).
Since \(pq/(n\delta^2)\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\), the limit is 1.

Item~3. With \(0<p<1\) we have \(pq>0\), so
\(\delta_\varepsilon=\sqrt{pq/(n\varepsilon)}>0\) and the main inequality
with \(\delta=\delta_\varepsilon\) bounds the sum by
\(pq/(n\delta_\varepsilon^2)=\varepsilon\). If
\(n\geq pq/(\varepsilon_0\varphi^2)\) and \(\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_0\),
then \(\delta_\varepsilon^2=pq/(n\varepsilon)\leq pq/(n\varepsilon_0)\leq\varphi^2\),
so \(\delta_\varepsilon\leq\varphi\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-middle-frequencies}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-middle-frequencies}{Accumulation points of a frequency sequence}
\begin{proof}
Write \(S_n=a_1+\cdots+a_n\), so \(h_n=S_n/n\in[0,1]\). A number \(c\)
is an accumulation point (middle frequency) when for every
\(\varepsilon>0\) and every \(K\) there is \(n>K\) with
\(|h_n-c|<\varepsilon\). Two facts are used.
First, \(h_{n+1}-h_n=(nS_n+na_{n+1}-(n+1)S_n)/(n(n+1))=(a_{n+1}-h_n)/(n+1)\),
and \(|a_{n+1}-h_n|\leq1\), so \(|h_{n+1}-h_n|\leq1/(n+1)\).
Second, let \(L=\liminf_nh_n\) and \(U=\limsup_nh_n\), where
\(U=\lim_{K\to\infty}u_K\) with \(u_K=\sup_{n>K}h_n\) nonincreasing, and
\(L\) is defined dually.

Item~1. The sequence \((h_n)\) is bounded, so by the Bolzano--Weierstrass
theorem a subsequence converges to some \(c\). Every neighbourhood of
\(c\) then contains \(h_n\) for infinitely many \(n\), so \(c\) is an
accumulation point. If \(c\notin[0,1]\), then the positive distance from
\(c\) to \([0,1]\) bounds \(|h_n-c|\) from below, so no point outside
\([0,1]\) is an accumulation point.

Item~2. \(U\) is an accumulation point: given \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(K\),
choose \(K'\geq K\) with \(u_{K'}<U+\varepsilon\); since
\(u_{K'}\geq U\), there is \(n>K'\) with \(h_n>u_{K'}-\varepsilon\geq U-\varepsilon\),
and \(h_n\leq u_{K'}<U+\varepsilon\). The same argument for lower bounds
shows that \(L\) is an accumulation point. No accumulation point exceeds
\(U\): if \(c>U\), put \(\varepsilon=(c-U)/2\) and choose \(K\) with
\(u_K<U+\varepsilon\); then \(h_n<c-\varepsilon\) for every \(n>K\).
Dually none is below \(L\). So the set \(M\) lies in \([L,U]\) and
contains both endpoints.
Now let \(L<c<U\), \(\varepsilon>0\) and \(K\) be given. Choose
\(K_1\geq K\) with \(1/(K_1+1)<\varepsilon\). As \(U\) is an
accumulation point and \(U-c>0\), there is \(n_1>K_1\) with
\(h_{n_1}>c\). As \(L\) is an accumulation point and \(c-L>0\), there is
\(n_2>n_1\) with \(h_{n_2}<c\). Let \(n^*\) be the least \(n\) with
\(n_1<n\leq n_2\) and \(h_n\leq c\); it exists since \(n_2\) qualifies.
Then \(h_{n^*-1}>c\geq h_{n^*}\), whence
\(0\leq c-h_{n^*}<h_{n^*-1}-h_{n^*}\leq1/n^*\leq1/(K_1+1)<\varepsilon\),
with \(n^*>K\). So \(c\in M\), and \(M=[L,U]\). If \(M\) has more than one
element then \(L<U\) and \(M\) is a nondegenerate interval.

Item~3. If \(h_n\to l\), every convergent subsequence has limit \(l\),
and an accumulation point is the limit of a subsequence chosen with
\(|h_{n_j}-c|<1/j\) and increasing \(n_j\); so \(M=\{l\}\). Conversely,
if \(M=\{l\}\), item~2 gives \(L=U=l\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\) choose
\(K\) with \(u_K<l+\varepsilon\) and, dually,
\(\inf_{n>K}h_n>l-\varepsilon\); then \(|h_n-l|<\varepsilon\) for
\(n>K\), so \(h_n\to l\), and \(l\) is the frequency limit.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-fwin}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows}{Freedom and windows}
\begin{proof}
Write \(C_L(u)\) as in \cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-027-free-along}, and let
\(\tau\) have length \(k\). A position \(t\) lies in \(S_\tau\) exactly
when \(t>k\) and \(\tau\) starts at \(s=t-k\). Hence, for \(L>k\) and
\(x\in\{0,1\}\),
\[
 s_\tau(L)=C_{L-k}(\tau),\qquad
 \#\{t\in S_\tau:t\leq L,\ a_t=x\}=C_{L-k}(\tau x). \tag{a}
\]
Moving \(L\) by \(k\) changes any count \(C_L(u)\) by at most \(k\), so
(a) gives \(|s_\tau(L)-C_L(\tau)|\leq k\) and
\(|\#\{t\in S_\tau:t\leq L,\ a_t=x\}-C_L(\tau x)|\leq k\).

Item~1, by induction on the length of \(u\). For the empty word
\(C_L(\varnothing)=L\) and \(\mu_p(\varnothing)=1\). Suppose
\(C_{L_i}(\tau)/L_i\to\mu_p(\tau)\), and let \(x\in\{0,1\}\), with
\(p_1=p\) and \(p_0=q\). If \(S_\tau\) is infinite, the frequency of
\(x\) among the selected positions up to \(L_i\) tends to \(p_x\): for
\(x=1\) by hypothesis, and for \(x=0\) because the two frequencies add
to \(1\). By the bounds after (a),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{C_{L_i}(\tau x)}{L_i}
 &=\frac{s_\tau(L_i)}{L_i}\cdot
  \frac{\#\{t\in S_\tau:t\leq L_i,\ a_t=x\}}{s_\tau(L_i)}+O\!\left(\frac{k}{L_i}\right)\\
 &\longrightarrow\mu_p(\tau)\,p_x=\mu_p(\tau x),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the first factor tends to \(\mu_p(\tau)\) because it differs from
\(C_{L_i}(\tau)/L_i\) by at most \(k/L_i\); the finitely many \(i\) with
\(s_\tau(L_i)=0\) do not affect the limit. If \(S_\tau\) is finite, then
by (a) \(C_{L}(\tau x)\leq C_L(\tau)\leq|S_\tau|\) for all \(L\), so both
\(C_{L_i}(\tau)/L_i\) and \(C_{L_i}(\tau x)/L_i\) tend to \(0\). Then
\(\mu_p(\tau)=0\), and \(\mu_p(\tau x)=\mu_p(\tau)p_x=0\) is again the
limit.

Item~2. Let \(0<p<1\) and fix \(\tau\) of length \(k\). Then
\(\mu_p(\tau)>0\), and \(C_{L_i}(\tau)\geq\tfrac12\mu_p(\tau)L_i\) for
large \(i\), so \(C_L(\tau)\) is unbounded and, by (a), \(S_\tau\) is
infinite. By (a) and the bounds after it,
\[
 \frac{o_\tau(L_i)}{s_\tau(L_i)}
 =\frac{C_{L_i-k}(\tau1)}{C_{L_i-k}(\tau)}
 =\frac{C_{L_i}(\tau1)/L_i+O(k/L_i)}{C_{L_i}(\tau)/L_i+O(k/L_i)}
 \longrightarrow\frac{\mu_p(\tau)p}{\mu_p(\tau)}=p .
\]
For the empty word, \(o_\varnothing(L_i)/s_\varnothing(L_i)=C_{L_i}(1)/L_i\to p\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-aligned-windows}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-aligned-windows}{Normal ordinal selection of windows}
\begin{proof}
Let \(w\) have length \(\ell\), and put \(\mu=\mu_p\).

Step~1: a variance bound. For \(k\geq1\) let \(\Lambda=(k-1)d+\ell\), and
for a word \(v\) of length \(\Lambda\) let \(N(v)\) be the number of
\(i\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\) such that \(w\) starts in \(v\) at offset
\(id\). Give each word \(v\) of length \(\Lambda\) the weight
\(\mu(v)\); the weights sum to \((p+q)^\Lambda=1\). Summing the product
weights one coordinate at a time shows two facts: the weighted sum of a
function of a set \(Q\) of coordinates equals its weighted sum over words
of length \(|Q|\), and two functions of disjoint coordinate sets have a
weighted sum of products equal to the product of their weighted sums.
Let \(I_i(v)=1\) when \(w\) starts at offset \(id\), else \(0\). Then
\(\sum_v\mu(v)I_i(v)=\mu(w)\). When \(|i-i'|d\geq\ell\), the coordinate
sets of \(I_i\) and \(I_{i'}\) are disjoint, so
\(\sum_v\mu(v)(I_i-\mu(w))(I_{i'}-\mu(w))=0\). For every pair the same
sum has absolute value at most \(1/4\), by the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality
and \(\mu(w)(1-\mu(w))\leq1/4\). There are at most \(k(2c-1)\) ordered
pairs with \(|i-i'|<c\), where \(c=\lceil\ell/d\rceil\). Hence
\[
 \sum_v\mu(v)\bigl(N(v)-k\mu(w)\bigr)^2\leq\frac{k(2c-1)}{4},
\]
and for \(\varepsilon>0\) the set \(\mathrm{Bad}\) of words \(v\) with
\(N(v)\geq k(\mu(w)+\varepsilon)\), on which
\((N(v)-k\mu(w))^2\geq k^2\varepsilon^2\), has
\[
 \sum_{v\in\mathrm{Bad}}\mu(v)\leq\frac{2c-1}{4k\varepsilon^2}. \tag{b}
\]

Step~2: a counting identity. For \(s\equiv j\pmod d\) let \(v_s\) be the
word of length \(\Lambda\) starting at \(s\). Then \(N(v_s)\) counts the
\(i<k\) with \(w\) starting at \(s+id\), and each such start is again
\(\equiv j\pmod d\). Summing over the \(s\leq L\) with \(s\equiv j\pmod
d\), every start \(s'\leq L\) of \(w\) with \(s'\equiv j\pmod d\) and
\(s'>(k-1)d\) is counted exactly \(k\) times, from \(s=s'-id\),
\(i=0,\ldots,k-1\). There are at most \(k-1\) positions \(s'\leq(k-1)d\)
in the class. Therefore
\[
 A_L(w)\leq k+\frac1k\sum_{\substack{s\leq L\\ s\equiv j}}N(v_s)
 =k+\frac1k\sum_{|v|=\Lambda}B_L(v)N(v),
\]
where \(B_L(v)\) is the number of \(s\leq L\) in the class at which \(v\)
starts. Splitting into \(v\notin\mathrm{Bad}\), where
\(N(v)<k(\mu(w)+\varepsilon)\), and \(v\in\mathrm{Bad}\), where
\(N(v)\leq k\), and using \(\sum_vB_L(v)=M_L\) and
\(B_L(v)\leq C_L(v)\),
\[
 A_L(w)\leq k+(\mu(w)+\varepsilon)M_L+\sum_{v\in\mathrm{Bad}}C_L(v). \tag{c}
\]

Step~3: the upper bound. Since \(|M_L-L/d|\leq1\), \(L/M_{L}\to d\).
By hypothesis \(C_{L_i}(v)/L_i\to\mu(v)\) for each of the finitely many
\(v\) of length \(\Lambda\). Dividing (c) by \(M_{L_i}\) and using (b),
\[
 \limsup_{i\to\infty}\frac{A_{L_i}(w)}{M_{L_i}}
 \leq\mu(w)+\varepsilon+d\,\frac{2c-1}{4k\varepsilon^2}.
\]
Choosing \(k\geq d(2c-1)/(4\varepsilon^3)\) gives the bound
\(\mu(w)+2\varepsilon\). As \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary,
\(\limsup_iA_{L_i}(w)/M_{L_i}\leq\mu(w)\), for every word \(w\) of
every length.

Step~4: the lower bound. Every \(s\leq L\) in the class starts exactly
one word of length \(\ell\), so \(\sum_{|w'|=\ell}A_L(w')=M_L\), and
\(\sum_{|w'|=\ell}\mu(w')=1\). Hence
\[
 \frac{A_{L_i}(w)}{M_{L_i}}=1-\sum_{w'\neq w}\frac{A_{L_i}(w')}{M_{L_i}},
 \qquad
 \liminf_i\frac{A_{L_i}(w)}{M_{L_i}}\geq1-\sum_{w'\neq w}\mu(w')=\mu(w),
\]
by Step~3 applied to the finitely many \(w'\neq w\) of length \(\ell\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-third-binomial}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-third-binomial}{The third binomial formula}
\begin{proof}
By item~1 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows}, \(a\) has
binomial window frequencies \(p\) along \((L_i)\), so
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-aligned-windows} applies.

Item~1. Take \(d=n\). The segments starting at positions \(\leq L_i\) are
\(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{R_i}\), and \(R_i=M_{L_i}\). The index \(t\)
has \((\sigma_t,\ldots,\sigma_{t+r-1})=(\sigma'_1,\ldots,\sigma'_r)\)
exactly when the concatenation \(w=\sigma'_1\cdots\sigma'_r\), of length
\(rn\), starts at \(j+(t-1)n\). So the proportion is
\(A_{L_i}(w)/M_{L_i}\to\mu_p(w)=\mu_p(\sigma'_1)\cdots\mu_p(\sigma'_r)\).
For the property \(m\), take \(r=1\) and sum over the \(\binom nm\)
words with \(m\) ones, each of weight \(p^mq^{n-m}\).

Item~2. For a word \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_r)\) of zeros and ones, \(v\) starts
in \(b\) at \(t\) exactly when \((\sigma_t,\ldots,\sigma_{t+r-1})\) lies in
the finite set of segment words with \(\sigma'_l\in\Pi\) where \(v_l=1\)
and \(\sigma'_l\notin\Pi\) where \(v_l=0\). Summing item~1 over that set,
the number of such \(t\leq R_i\), divided by \(R_i\), tends to the
product over \(l\) of \(\pi\) where \(v_l=1\) and \(1-\pi\) where
\(v_l=0\), that is, to \(\mu_\pi(v)\). So \(b\) has binomial window
frequencies \(\pi\) along \((R_i)\). If \(\Pi\) is empty or contains
every word, \(b\) is constant with value \(\pi\in\{0,1\}\), and every
infinite selection has frequency \(\pi\). Otherwise \(0<\pi<1\), because
\(0<p<1\) gives every word positive weight, and item~2 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows} shows that \(\pi\) is free
along \((R_i)\). With \(L_i=i\), \(R_i\) runs through all positive
integers, so \(b\) is absolutely free with probability \(\pi\).

Item~3. Take \(\Pi=\Delta p\) in item~1, summing over the values of \(m\)
with \(|m/n-p|<\delta\). The bound on \(B_n(\delta)\) is the displayed
inequality of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-bernoulli-bound}, since
\(B_n(\delta)\) is \(1\) minus the sum there.

Item~4. Let \(k=|\tau|\), \(k'=|\tau'|\). A position \(t>k\) with
\(t\equiv j\pmod d\) is selected with \(a_t=x\) exactly when the word
\(\tau x\tau'\) starts at \(s=t-k\), and \(s\equiv j-k\pmod d\). Let
\(A\) and \(M\) denote the counts of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-aligned-windows} for that residue class, with
\(L_i=i\). The selected positions up to \(L\) with value \(x\) number
\(A_{L-k}(\tau x\tau')\), so the frequency of ones among the selected
positions up to \(L\) is
\[
 \frac{A_{L-k}(\tau1\tau')}{A_{L-k}(\tau0\tau')+A_{L-k}(\tau1\tau')}
 \longrightarrow
 \frac{\mu_p(\tau)\,p\,\mu_p(\tau')}{\mu_p(\tau)\,(q+p)\,\mu_p(\tau')}=p,
\]
after dividing numerator and denominator by \(M_{L-k}\); the limit of the
denominator is positive because \(0<p<1\), so the selection is infinite.
As after \cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-027-free-along}, convergence along all
\(L\) is convergence of the relative frequencies of the selected
subsequence.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-bmid}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-bernoulli-middle}{Common lengths}
\begin{proof}
Let \(h_R\) be the relative frequency of segments with property
\(\Delta p\) among \(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_R\) of \(\alpha_n^{(j)}\). By
item~3 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-third-binomial},
\(h_{R_i}\to B_n(\delta)\) along the increasing sequence \(R_i\), so for
every \(\varepsilon>0\) and every \(R_0\) some \(R_i>R_0\) has
\(|h_{R_i}-B_n(\delta)|<\varepsilon\): \(B_n(\delta)\) is an accumulation
point of \((h_R)\), that is, a middle frequency. The same argument with
item~1 of that theorem gives the property \(m\). Item~2 is item~2 of
that theorem, and item~3 is item~3 of it. Uniqueness was nowhere used.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-finite-records}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-finite-records}{Altering a finite initial record; bounded and unbounded existentials}
\begin{proof}
Item~1. Let \(a\) be absolutely free with probability \(p\) and let \(w\)
have length \(\ell\). Define \(a'\) by \(a'_t=w_t\) for \(t\leq\ell\) and
\(a'_t=a_t\) for \(t>\ell\). The numbers of ones among the first \(n\)
terms of \(a\) and \(a'\) differ by at most \(\ell\), so their relative
frequencies differ by at most \(\ell/n\), and \(a'\) has the frequency
limit \(p\).

Fix a word \(\tau\) of length \(k\). A position \(t>\ell+k\) has
\(t-k>\ell\), so \(a'\) and \(a\) agree at \(t-k,\ldots,t\). Hence
\(t\in S'_\tau\) iff \(t\in S_\tau\) (primes for \(a'\)), and the term at
\(t\) is the same. Let \(s(n),s'(n)\) be the numbers of selected
positions up to \(n\), and \(o(n),o'(n)\) the numbers of selected ones.
Then \(|s(n)-s'(n)|\leq\ell+k\) and \(|o(n)-o'(n)|\leq\ell+k\). If
\(S'_\tau\) is infinite, so is \(S_\tau\), since the two differ only at
positions up to \(\ell+k\). For \(n\) with \(s(n),s'(n)>0\), using
\(o(n)\leq s(n)\),
\[
 \Bigl|\frac{o'(n)}{s'(n)}-\frac{o(n)}{s(n)}\Bigr|
 \leq\frac{|o'(n)-o(n)|}{s'(n)}+o(n)\frac{|s(n)-s'(n)|}{s(n)s'(n)}
 \leq\frac{2(\ell+k)}{s'(n)} .
\]
Taking \(n\) as the position of the \(r\)th element of \(S'_\tau\), the
left fraction \(o'(n)/s'(n)\) is the relative frequency of ones among the
first \(r\) selected elements of \(a'\). As \(r\to\infty\), \(s'(n)=r\to\infty\)
and \(s(n)\to\infty\). By absolute freedom of \(a\),
\(o(n)/s(n)\to p\) along these \(n\), because these are relative
frequencies of the selected subsequence of \(a\) at increasing lengths.
The bound then gives \(o'(n)/s'(n)\to p\). So \(a'\) is absolutely free
with probability \(p\) and begins with \(w\).

Item~2. Let \(w\) have length \(\ell\). The alternative \(w\) followed
by zeros satisfies \(S_\infty\): for any \(x\) take
\(y=\max(x,\ell)+1\), so \(y>x\) and \(a_y=0\). The alternative \(w\)
followed by ones violates \(S_\infty\): for \(x=\ell\) every \(y>x\)
has \(a_y=1\). If \(a_1=a_2=1\), then \(x=1\) gives \(a_x=1\) and
\(a_{x+1}\neq0\), so \(S_b\) fails.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-refs}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-reference-class}{Reference classes}
\begin{proof}
Item~1. Since \(\alpha.\beta.\beta=\alpha.\beta\) and
\(N(\alpha.\beta)>0\), \({}_{\alpha.\beta}F''(\beta)=1\). By item~1 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-finite-calculus},
\(N(\alpha.\bar\beta)=N(\alpha)-N(\alpha.\beta)>0\), and
\(\alpha.\bar\beta.\beta\) is empty, so
\({}_{\alpha.\bar\beta}F''(\beta)=0\). The value
\({}_{\alpha}F''(\beta)=N(\alpha.\beta)/N(\alpha)\) lies strictly between
0 and 1 by the hypotheses, and its definition mentions no particular
element of \(\alpha\). Both \(\alpha.\beta\) and \(\alpha.\bar\beta\) are
nonempty.

Item~2. The element \(1\) belongs to \(\alpha.\beta\) and not to
\(\gamma\), so the universal law fails. Among the first \(n\) positive
integers all belong to \(\beta\) and \(n-1\) to \(\gamma\); the relative
frequencies \(1\) and \((n-1)/n\) both converge to \(1\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-greedy-rule}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-greedy-rule}{Construction rule}
\begin{proof}
A \emph{window} of a linear word \(g\) is a block of \(x\) consecutive
terms of \(g\). By construction the first window is \(1^x\) and every
appended term creates a window that has not occurred before, so at every
stage the windows of \(g\) are pairwise distinct. Each step adds one
window, and there are \(2^x\) words of length \(x\), so the procedure
stops after finitely many steps.

Case \(x=1\). The rule writes \(1\), then appends \(0\) (the word \(0\)
is new), giving \(10\); both words of length~1 have now occurred, so it
stops. The length is \(2=2^1+1-1\), item~2 is vacuous, and the period
\(10\) contains each word of length~1 once, so item~3 follows from
item~4 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial} with \(n=0\).

Case \(x\geq2\). Call a word of length \(x-1\) a \emph{state}. Let the
current word \(g\) have length \(\ell\geq x\) and windows
\(w_1,\ldots,w_{\ell-x+1}\) in order. Put \(s_0\) equal to the first
\(x-1\) terms of \(w_1\), so \(s_0=1^{x-1}\); for
\(1\leq j\leq\ell-x+1\) put \(s_j\) equal to the last \(x-1\) terms of
\(w_j\). Consecutive windows overlap in \(x-1\) terms, so \(s_j\) is
also the first \(x-1\) terms of \(w_{j+1}\) for \(j\leq\ell-x\). The
final state \(f=s_{\ell-x+1}\) consists of the last \(x-1\) terms of
\(g\). For a state \(u\) let \(\mathrm{out}(u)\) be the number of windows
whose first \(x-1\) terms are \(u\), and \(\mathrm{in}(u)\) the number of
windows whose last \(x-1\) terms are \(u\). Then
\(\mathrm{out}(u)=\#\{j\in[0,\ell-x]:s_j=u\}\) and
\(\mathrm{in}(u)=\#\{j\in[1,\ell-x+1]:s_j=u\}\), so
\[
 \mathrm{in}(u)-\mathrm{out}(u)=[u=f]-[u=s_0],
\]
where \([\,\cdot\,]\) is \(1\) for a true and \(0\) for a false condition.
Since windows are distinct, \(\mathrm{out}(u)\leq2\) (only \(u0,u1\)) and
\(\mathrm{in}(u)\leq2\) (only \(0u,1u\)).

The procedure stops at \(g\) exactly when \(f0\) and \(f1\) are both
windows, that is \(\mathrm{out}(f)=2\). Then
\(\mathrm{in}(f)=3-[f=s_0]\), and \(\mathrm{in}(f)\leq2\) forces
\(f=s_0=1^{x-1}\). This proves item~2. At the stop, therefore,
\(\mathrm{in}(u)=\mathrm{out}(u)\) for every state \(u\).

The rule appends a one after state \(u\) only when \(u0\) is already a
window. Every window \(u1\) other than the first window \(1^x\) arose
by such an appending, so if \(u1\) is a window then \(u0\) is a window,
for \(u\neq1^{x-1}\). For \(u=1^{x-1}=f\) we have \(\mathrm{out}(u)=2\)
at the stop. Hence at the stop:
\[
 u1\text{ is a window}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathrm{out}(u)=2.\tag{\(*\)}
\]
Let \(u=c_1\cdots c_{x-1}\) be any state and, for \(0\leq j\leq x-1\),
put \(u^{(j)}=c_{j+1}\cdots c_{x-1}1^j\), so \(u^{(0)}=u\) and
\(u^{(x-1)}=1^{x-1}\), with \(\mathrm{out}(u^{(x-1)})=2\). Suppose
\(\mathrm{out}(u^{(j+1)})=2\). Then \(\mathrm{in}(u^{(j+1)})=2\), so both
\(0u^{(j+1)}\) and \(1u^{(j+1)}\) are windows. One of them is
\(c_{j+1}u^{(j+1)}=c_{j+1}c_{j+2}\cdots c_{x-1}1^{j}1=u^{(j)}1\), so
\(\mathrm{out}(u^{(j)})=2\) by (\(*\)). Descending from \(j=x-2\) to
\(j=0\) gives \(\mathrm{out}(u)=2\). Thus both \(u0\) and \(u1\) are
windows for each of the \(2^{x-1}\) states, so all \(2^x\) words of
length \(x\) are windows, each once. The number of windows is
\(\ell-x+1=2^x\), so \(\ell=2^x+x-1\). This proves item~1.

Item~3. Write \(g=g_1\cdots g_\ell\) and \(G=g_1\cdots g_{2^x}\). The
first \(x\) terms of \(g\) are ones, and by item~2 so are its last
\(x-1\) terms \(g_{2^x+1},\ldots,g_{2^x+x-1}\). Hence
\(g_{2^x+j}=g_j\) for \(1\leq j\leq x-1\), and the cyclic window of
\(G\) starting at position \(i\) (\(1\leq i\leq2^x\)), with indices
modulo \(2^x\), equals the linear window \(w_i\) of \(g\). The \(2^x\)
cyclic windows are therefore the \(2^x\) distinct windows of \(g\), and
every word of length \(x\) occurs exactly once cyclically in \(G\). With
\(n=x-1\) and \(N=2^{n+1}\), item~4 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial} gives \(p=q=1/2\) and cyclic
\(n\)-freedom.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-shortest-sequence}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-shortest-sequence}{Nesting}
\begin{proof}
Item~1. By item~3 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-greedy-rule} with
\(x=n_i\), every word of length \(n_i\) occurs exactly once among the
cyclic windows of \(G_{i+1}\), which has length \(2^{n_i}=n_{i+1}\). In
particular \(P_i\), of length \(n_i\), occurs at exactly one cyclic
position \(c\). The rotation of \(G_{i+1}\) starting at \(c\) begins with
\(P_i\); a rotation starting at \(c'\neq c\) that began with \(P_i\)
would give a second cyclic occurrence. So \(P_{i+1}\) exists, is unique
and has \(P_i\) as initial segment. Since \(n_{i+1}=2^{n_i}>n_i\), the
lengths increase without bound, and \(a_t\) is well defined as the
\(t\)-th term of any \(P_i\) with \(n_i\geq t\); every sequence with all
\(P_i\) as initial segments has these terms.

Item~2. For \(i\geq1\), the initial segment of length \(n_i\) is
\(P_i\), a rotation of \(G_i\). Cyclic counts are unchanged by rotation,
so by item~3 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-greedy-rule} with
\(x=n_{i-1}\), \(P_i\) is cyclically \((n_{i-1}-1)\)-free with
\(p=q=1/2\), and \(n_{i-1}=\log_2n_i\).

Item~3. Let \(n_{i-1}\geq k\). By item~2 and item~2 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial} with \(n=n_{i-1}\), every word
\(\tau\) of length \(k\) has cyclic count \(c_{P_i}(\tau)=n_i2^{-k}\).
For \(s\leq n_i-k+1\) the window \((a_s,\ldots,a_{s+k-1})\) lies inside
\(P_i\) and coincides with the cyclic window at \(s\). For the remaining
\(k-1\) values \(s\leq n_i\), the linear and cyclic windows may differ,
so \(|C_{n_i}(\tau)-n_i2^{-k}|\leq k-1\).
With \(k=1\) and \(\tau=1\), the relative frequency of ones at length
\(n_i\) is exactly \(1/2\) once \(i\geq1\). For a predecessor word
\(\tau\) of length \(k\), a position \(t\) belongs to \(S_\tau\) exactly
when \(t>k\) and the window at \(s=t-k\) is \(\tau\); so the number of
positions of \(S_\tau\) not exceeding \(n_i+k\) is \(C_{n_i}(\tau)\), and
the number of those carrying a one is \(C_{n_i}(\tau1)\). For \(i\) with
\(n_{i-1}\geq k+1\) and \(n_i2^{-k}>k-1\),
\[
 \frac{C_{n_i}(\tau1)}{C_{n_i}(\tau)}
 \in\left[\frac{n_i2^{-k-1}-k}{n_i2^{-k}+k-1},
 \frac{n_i2^{-k-1}+k}{n_i2^{-k}-(k-1)}\right],
\]
and both ends tend to \(1/2\) as \(n_i\to\infty\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-free-exists}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-free-exists}{An absolutely free alternative with probability one half}
\begin{proof}
By item~3 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-greedy-rule}, \(D_K\) is a cyclic
period of length \(2^K\) that is cyclically \((K-1)\)-free with
\(p=q=1/2\). By item~2 of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-binomial} with
\(n=K\), every word of length \(k\leq K\) has cyclic count
\(c_{D_K}(\tau)=2^{K-k}\).

Let \(B_K\) be the block of \(2^K\) copies of \(D_K\), of length
\(4^K\), and let \(T_J=\sum_{K=1}^J4^K\) (\(T_0=0\)). Then
\(a=B_1B_2B_3\cdots\), and \(B_K\) occupies the positions
\(T_{K-1}<s\leq T_K\).

Step~1: the count inside one block. Fix \(\tau\) of length \(k\leq K\),
and let \(E_K(\tau)\) be the number of positions \(s\) in \(B_K\) at which
the window \((a_s,\ldots,a_{s+k-1})\) of \(a\) equals \(\tau\). Take \(s\)
in the \(j\)-th copy of \(D_K\) at offset \(r\) (\(0\leq r<2^K\)). If
\(r\leq2^K-k\), the window lies in the copy and equals the cyclic window
of \(D_K\) at \(r\). If \(r>2^K-k\) and \(j<2^K\), the window runs into the
next copy of \(D_K\), and again equals the cyclic window at \(r\). So each
of the first \(2^K-1\) copies contributes exactly \(2^{K-k}\), and the last
copy contributes \(2^{K-k}\) up to an error of at most \(k-1\), from its
last \(k-1\) offsets. Hence \(|E_K(\tau)-4^K2^{-k}|\leq k-1\).

Step~2: an arbitrary length. Let \(L\) satisfy \(T_{J-1}<L\leq T_J\) with
\(J\geq k+1\), and write \(L=T_{J-1}+j2^J+i\) with \(0\leq j\leq2^J\),
\(0\leq i<2^J\), and \(i=0\) when \(j=2^J\). Split the positions
\(s\leq L\) into four groups.
(i)~\(s\leq T_{k-1}\): the count lies in \([0,T_{k-1}]\), and differs from
\(2^{-k}T_{k-1}\) by at most \(T_{k-1}\).
(ii)~The blocks \(B_K\), \(k\leq K\leq J-1\): by Step~1 their counts
together differ from \(2^{-k}(T_{J-1}-T_{k-1})\) by at most
\((J-k)(k-1)\).
(iii)~The first \(j\) copies of \(D_J\) in \(B_J\): if \(j<2^J\), each is
followed by another copy of \(D_J\), so by the argument of Step~1 each
contributes exactly \(2^{J-k}\); if \(j=2^J\), Step~1 applies to the
whole block with error at most \(k-1\).
(iv)~The last \(i\) positions: the count lies in \([0,i]\), and differs
from \(2^{-k}i\) by at most \(i<2^J\).
Adding the four deviations,
\[
 |C_L(\tau)-2^{-k}L|\leq T_{k-1}+J(k-1)+2^J .
\]
Since \(L>T_{J-1}\geq4^{J-1}\),
\[
 \left|\frac{C_L(\tau)}{L}-2^{-k}\right|
 \leq\frac{T_{k-1}+J(k-1)+2^J}{4^{J-1}},
\]
and the right side tends to \(0\) as \(J\to\infty\). As \(L\to\infty\),
\(J\to\infty\), so \(C_L(\tau)/L\to2^{-k}\).

Step~3: frequencies and selections. With \(k=1\) and \(\tau=1\),
\(C_L(1)\) is the number of ones among \(a_1,\ldots,a_L\), so the
relative frequency of ones tends to \(1/2\) and \({}_{a}F'(1)=1/2\).
For a predecessor word \(\tau\) of length \(k\), a position \(t\) lies in
\(S_\tau\) exactly when \(t>k\) and the window at \(s=t-k\) is \(\tau\).
Since \(C_L(\tau)/L\to2^{-k}>0\), \(C_L(\tau)\) is unbounded, so
\(S_\tau\) is infinite; list it as \(t_1<t_2<\cdots\). For each \(N\) take
\(L=t_N-k\): the members of \(S_\tau\) not exceeding \(t_N\) are
\(t_1,\ldots,t_N\), so \(N=C_L(\tau)\), and the ones among
\(a_{t_1},\ldots,a_{t_N}\) number \(C_L(\tau1)\). As \(N\to\infty\),
\(L\to\infty\), and the relative frequency of ones among the first \(N\)
selected positions is
\[
 \frac{C_L(\tau1)}{C_L(\tau)}
 =\frac{C_L(\tau1)/L}{C_L(\tau)/L}\longrightarrow\frac{2^{-k-1}}{2^{-k}}=\frac12 .
\]
This holds for every \(k\) and \(\tau\), so \(a\) is absolutely free in the
sense of \cref{def:p15-w02b-p15-027-absolute-freedom}, with
\({}_{a}F(1)=1/2\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-concatenation}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-concatenation}{Counting windows in a concatenation}
\begin{proof}
A window starting in \(U_i\) that ends in \(U_i\) is the window of the
repetition \(V_iV_iV_i\cdots\) at the offset of its start within its
copy of \(V_i\). Only starts among the last \(k-1\) positions of \(U_i\)
can end outside \(U_i\). Let \(i<j\). The \(r_i|V_i|\) starts in \(U_i\)
run over each offset \(r_i\) times, so the count from \(U_i\) differs from
\(r_i|V_i|\kappa_{V_i}(u)=|U_i|\kappa_{V_i}(u)\) by at most \(k-1\).
In \(U_j\), write \(L-T_{j-1}=c|V_j|+e\) with \(0\leq e<|V_j|\) and
\(c\leq r_j\). The starts in the first \(c\) copies run over each offset
\(c\) times, and by the same argument their count differs from
\(c|V_j|\kappa_{V_j}(u)\) by at most \(k-1\). The remaining \(e\) starts
contribute between \(0\) and \(e\), and \(e\kappa_{V_j}(u)\) also lies in
\([0,e]\), so the difference is at most \(e<|V_j|\). Adding the \(j\)
deviations of at most \(k-1\) and the deviation \(|V_j|\) gives the
bound.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-constructions}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-constructions}{Models}
\begin{proof}
Step~1: consequences of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-concatenation}, whose error is at most
\(j(k-1)+|V_j|\) when \(T_{j-1}<L\leq T_j\) and \(u\) has length \(k\).
Suppose
\[
 \frac{j}{T_{j-1}}\to0,\qquad\frac{|V_j|}{T_{j-1}}\to0. \tag{G}
\]
Put \(\omega_{L,i}=|U_i|/L\) for \(i<j\), \(\omega_{L,j}=(L-T_{j-1})/L\),
and \(\omega_{L,i}=0\) for \(i>j\); these weights sum to \(1\), and
\[
 \frac{C_L(u)}{L}=\sum_i\omega_{L,i}\,\kappa_{V_i}(u)+\eta_L(u),\qquad
 \eta_L(u)\to0 . \tag{W}
\]
(i) If \(\kappa_{V_i}(u)\to\kappa(u)\) as \(i\to\infty\), then
\(C_L(u)/L\to\kappa(u)\): given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(i_1\) with
\(|\kappa_{V_i}(u)-\kappa(u)|<\varepsilon\) for \(i>i_1\); the weights
with \(i\leq i_1\) sum to at most \(T_{i_1}/L\to0\), and \(\kappa\)-values
lie in \([0,1]\).
(ii) If \(J\) is an infinite set of indices with \(|U_j|/T_j\to1\) along
\(J\), then \(C_{T_j}(u)/T_j-\kappa_{V_j}(u)\to0\) along \(J\), because
at \(L=T_j\) the weight \(\omega_{L,j}=|U_j|/T_j\) tends to \(1\).

A choice of repetition numbers that gives (G) and \(|U_j|/T_j\to1\) for
every \(j\): once \(V_1,V_2,\ldots\) are fixed, choose \(r_j\) so that
\[
 |U_j|\geq j\,(T_{j-1}+|V_{j+1}|+1). \tag{R}
\]
Then \(T_{j-1}/T_j\leq1/(j+1)\), \(|V_j|/T_{j-1}\leq|V_j|/|U_{j-1}|\leq
1/(j-1)\), and \(T_{j-1}\geq\sum_{i<j}i=j(j-1)/2\), so (G) holds.

Step~2: good finite words. Let \(0<p<1\), \(K\geq1\), \(\varepsilon>0\)
and \(\ell\geq\max(K,K2^K/\varepsilon^2)\). Weight each word \(V\) of
length \(\ell\) by \(\mu_p(V)\). For a word \(u\) of length \(k\leq K\),
the cyclic windows of \(V\) at offsets \(r\) and \(r'\) use \(k\) distinct
coordinates each, and disjoint ones unless the cyclic distance of \(r\)
and \(r'\) is less than \(k\), which happens for at most \(2k-1\) values of
\(r'\). The argument of Step~1 in the proof of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-aligned-windows} gives
\[
 \sum_V\mu_p(V)\bigl(\ell\kappa_V(u)-\ell\mu_p(u)\bigr)^2\leq\frac{\ell(2k-1)}{4},
\]
and therefore
\[
 \sum_{V:\ |\kappa_V(u)-\mu_p(u)|\geq\varepsilon}\mu_p(V)\leq\frac{2k-1}{4\ell\varepsilon^2}.
\]
Summing over the \(2^k\) words of each length \(k\leq K\), the total
weight of words \(V\) that fail for some \(u\) is at most
\(\sum_{k=1}^K2^k(2k-1)/(4\ell\varepsilon^2)<K2^{K+2}/(4\ell\varepsilon^2)\leq1\).
The weights sum to \(1\), so some word \(W\) of length \(\ell\) has
\(|\kappa_W(u)-\mu_p(u)|<\varepsilon\) for every \(u\) with
\(|u|\leq K\). Write \(W_K(p)\) for such a word with \(\varepsilon=1/K\).

Item~1. Let \(V_i=W_i(p)\) and choose \(r_i\) by (R). For each \(u\),
\(|\kappa_{V_i}(u)-\mu_p(u)|<1/i\) once \(i\geq|u|\), so by (i)
\(C_L(u)/L\to\mu_p(u)\) along all \(L\). Item~2 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows} with \(L_i=i\) gives
absolute freedom with probability \(p\).

Item~2. Let \(1/2<p<1\). Build \(a\) in stages \(K=1,2,\ldots\): the
block \(U_{2K-1}\) consists of \(r_K\) copies of \(D_K\), with \(r_K\)
chosen after the earlier blocks so that
\(|U_{2K-1}|\geq2K(T_{2K-2}+2^{K+1}+1)\); then \(U_{2K}\) consists of
\(b_K\) ones, \(b_K\) the least \(b\geq1\) for which the relative frequency
of ones in \(U_1\cdots U_{2K-1}1^b\) is at least \(p\) (it exists, since
the frequency tends to \(1\) as \(b\) grows). Take \(V_{2K}=1\). Then
(G) holds: for a block of ones \(|V_j|=1\), and for \(j=2K+1\),
\(|V_j|=2^{K+1}\leq|U_{2K-1}|/(2K)\leq T_{j-1}/(2K)\); and for
\(j\in\{2K,2K+1\}\) with \(K\geq2\),
\(T_{j-1}\geq|U_{2K-1}|\geq2K\,|U_{2K-3}|\geq4K(K-1)\), so
\(j/T_{j-1}\to0\).

(2a) Along \(J=\{1,3,5,\ldots\}\), \(|U_j|/T_j\to1\), and
\(\kappa_{D_K}(u)=2^{-|u|}\) once \(K\geq|u|\). By (ii),
\(C_{T_{2K-1}}(u)/T_{2K-1}\to2^{-|u|}=\mu_{1/2}(u)\), and by item~2 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows}, \(1/2\) is free along
\((T_{2K-1})\), every \(S_\tau\) being infinite. In particular \(1/2\) is
a middle frequency of the ones.

(2b) By (2a) the frequency at \(T_{2K-1}\) tends to \(1/2<p\), so for
large \(K\) adding a single one does not reach \(p\) and \(b_K\geq2\).
Then the frequency just before the last one of \(U_{2K}\) is below \(p\):
writing \(N_L\) for the number of ones among the first \(L\) terms,
\(N_{T_{2K}}-1<p(T_{2K}-1)\), so
\(p\leq N_{T_{2K}}/T_{2K}<p+1/T_{2K}\). Hence \(p\) is a middle frequency,
and since \(p\neq1/2\) the frequencies have no limit
(\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-middle-frequencies}).

(2c) Let \(Z_L\) be the number of positions \(s\leq L\) lying in blocks of
copies of some \(D_K\), and \(Y_L=L-Z_L\) the number lying in blocks of
ones. In (W), \(\kappa_{D_K}(1)=1/2\), \(\kappa_{D_K}(0)=1/2\),
\(\kappa_{D_K}(01)=1/4\) for \(K\geq2\), and for the word \(1\)
\(\kappa_1(1)=1\), \(\kappa_1(0)=\kappa_1(01)=0\). The block with \(K=1\)
contributes a bounded amount. So
\[
 N_L=\tfrac12Z_L+Y_L+o(L),\qquad C_L(0)=\tfrac12Z_L+o(L),\qquad
 C_L(01)=\tfrac14Z_L+o(L).
\]
The first relation gives \(Y_L=2N_L-L+o(L)\). We show
\(N_L\leq pL+o(L)\) for all \(L\). For large \(K\) and \(L=T_{2K}\) this is
(2b). If \(L\) lies in the block \(U_{2K}\), then
\(N_L=N_{T_{2K}}-(T_{2K}-L)\), and removing the same number from the
numerator and denominator of a fraction at most \(1\) does not increase
it, so \(N_L/L\leq N_{T_{2K}}/T_{2K}<p+1/T_{2K}\). If \(L\) lies in the
block \(U_{2K+1}\), then \(Y_L=Y_{T_{2K}}\leq(2p-1)T_{2K}+o(T_{2K})\leq
(2p-1)L+o(L)\) directly. In all cases \(Y_L\leq(2p-1)L+o(L)\), so
\(Z_L\geq2(1-p)L-o(L)\), with \(2(1-p)>0\). The frequency of ones among
the members of \(S_0\) up to \(L\) is \(C_{L-1}(01)/C_{L-1}(0)\), by (a) in
the proof of \cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows}, and
\[
 \frac{C_{L}(01)}{C_{L}(0)}=\frac{\tfrac14Z_L/L+o(1)}{\tfrac12Z_L/L+o(1)}\longrightarrow\frac12,
\]
because \(Z_L/L\) stays above a positive constant for large \(L\). So
\(S_0\) is infinite and has the frequency limit \(1/2\). A value that is
a middle frequency of every infinite predecessor selection is in
particular one of \(S_0\), so it equals \(1/2\).

Item~3. Let \(V_{2i-1}=W_i(p)\), \(V_{2i}=W_i(p')\), and choose \(r_j\) by
(R). Along the odd indices \(\kappa_{V_j}(u)\to\mu_p(u)\), along the even
ones \(\kappa_{V_j}(u)\to\mu_{p'}(u)\), and \(|U_j|/T_j\to1\) along both.
By (ii), \(a\) has binomial window frequencies \(p\) along
\((T_{2i-1})\) and \(p'\) along \((T_{2i})\), and by item~2 of
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-freedom-windows} \(p\) is free along the
first sequence and \(p'\) along the second. Each is then a middle
frequency of \(a\) and of every infinite predecessor selection, so the
requirement of randomness holds and that of uniqueness fails.

Item~4. Let \(V_{2K-1}=D_K\) and \(V_{2K}=1^K0^K\), and choose \(r_j\) by
(R). The cyclic counts of \(1^K0^K\), of length \(2K\), are
\(\kappa(1)=\kappa(0)=1/2\), \(\kappa(11)=\kappa(00)=(K-1)/(2K)\) and
\(\kappa(10)=\kappa(01)=1/(2K)\). Since \(\kappa(1)=1/2\) for every
block, (i) gives \(N_L/L\to1/2\): the ones have the frequency limit
\(1/2\). As in (2a), \(1/2\) is free along \((T_{2K-1})\). For \(K\geq2\)
both kinds of block have \(\kappa(11)\geq1/4\) and \(\kappa(01)\leq1/4\),
since \((K-1)/(2K)\geq1/4\) and \(1/(2K)\leq1/4\); the blocks with \(K=1\)
contribute a bounded amount. By (W), \(C_L(11)\geq L/4-o(L)\) and
\(C_L(01)\leq L/4+o(L)\), while \(C_L(1)=L/2+o(L)\) and
\(C_L(0)=L/2+o(L)\). So the frequency of ones along \(S_1\) is at least
\(1/2-o(1)\), and along \(S_0\) at most \(1/2+o(1)\): every middle
frequency of \(S_1\) is at least \(1/2\), and every middle frequency of
\(S_0\) at most \(1/2\).

Fix \(n\geq1\), an offset \(j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\) and
\(0<\delta\leq1/2\). A constant \(n\)-segment has \(m/n\in\{0,1\}\), so
\(|m/n-1/2|=1/2\geq\delta\) and it is not a fair sample. Take \(K\geq n\)
and count the fair samples among the segments starting at positions
\(s\leq T_{2K}\), \(s\equiv j\pmod n\). Those starting at \(s\leq
T_{2K-1}\) number at most \(T_{2K-1}/n+1\). A segment starting in
\(U_{2K}\) is fair only if it is not constant, so it either contains a
change point, a position \(c\) in \(U_{2K}\) with \(a_c\neq a_{c+1}\), or
starts among the last \(n-1\) positions of \(U_{2K}\). The block
\(U_{2K}\) has at most \(|U_{2K}|/K\) change points. The segments
containing a given change point \(c\) start at one of the \(n-1\)
consecutive positions \(c-n+2,\ldots,c\), at most one of which is
\(\equiv j\pmod n\); likewise at most one start among the last \(n-1\)
positions is in the class. The number of segments is at least
\(T_{2K}/n-1\). Hence the relative frequency of fair samples among them is
at most
\[
 \frac{T_{2K-1}/n+|U_{2K}|/K+2}{T_{2K}/n-1}
 \longrightarrow0\qquad(K\to\infty),
\]
because \(T_{2K-1}/T_{2K}\to0\) by (R) and \(n/K\to0\). So the liminf of
the relative frequency of fair samples is \(0\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-characteristic}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-characteristic}{Insensitivity of the Bernoulli half-width}
\begin{proof}
Write \(b(m)=\binom{n}{m}p^mq^{n-m}\), with \(0^0=1\).

Item~1. If \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), all weight sits at \(m=np\), where
\(|m/n-p|=0<\delta\), so \(T_n(\delta)=0\). Let \(0<p<1\). Since
\(|m/n-p|\geq\delta\) holds exactly when \(m\geq n(p+\delta)\) or
\(m\leq n(p-\delta)\), \(T_n(\delta)\) is at most the sum \(U\) of the
weights with \(m\geq n(p+\delta)\) plus the sum \(\Lambda\) of those with
\(m\leq n(p-\delta)\). For \(s>0\) every \(m\) in \(U\) has
\(e^{s(m-np-n\delta)}\geq1\). Using \(sm-snp=sqm-sp(n-m)\) and the binomial
theorem,
\[
 U\leq e^{-sn\delta}\sum_{m=0}^n\binom{n}{m}(pe^{sq})^m(qe^{-sp})^{n-m}
 =e^{-sn\delta}\bigl(pe^{sq}+qe^{-sp}\bigr)^n .
\]
Put \(\psi(s)=\ln(pe^{sq}+qe^{-sp})=-sp+\ln(q+pe^s)\). Then
\(\psi(0)=0\), \(\psi'(s)=-p+u(s)\) with \(u(s)=pe^s/(q+pe^s)\), so
\(\psi'(0)=0\), and \(\psi''(s)=u(s)(1-u(s))\leq1/4\) because
\(0\leq u(s)\leq1\). By Taylor's theorem with Lagrange remainder,
\(\psi(s)=\psi''(\xi)s^2/2\leq s^2/8\) for some \(\xi\) between \(0\) and
\(s\). This is the centered exponential bound of
\cref{thm:p15-conc-exponential} for a variable with values \(0\) and
\(1\). Hence \(U\leq\exp(-sn\delta+ns^2/8)\), and \(s=4\delta\) gives
\(U\leq e^{-2n\delta^2}\). For \(\Lambda\), substitute \(m'=n-m\): the
condition becomes \(m'\geq n(q+\delta)\) and the weight becomes
\(\binom{n}{m'}q^{m'}p^{n-m'}\), so \(\Lambda\) is the upper sum with
\(p\) and \(q\) exchanged, and the same argument gives
\(\Lambda\leq e^{-2n\delta^2}\). So \(T_n(\delta)\leq2e^{-2n\delta^2}\).

Item~2. The set of \(m\) with \(|m/n-p|\geq\delta\) shrinks as \(\delta\)
grows, so \(T_n\) is nonincreasing. Put
\(A(\varepsilon)=\{\delta>0:T_n(\delta)\leq\varepsilon\}\). It is
nonempty, since \(T_n(\delta)=0\) for \(\delta>1\). If
\(\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon'\) then \(A(\varepsilon)\subseteq A(\varepsilon')\),
so \(\delta_n(\varepsilon')=\inf A(\varepsilon')\leq\inf A(\varepsilon)=\delta_n(\varepsilon)\).
Clearly \(\delta_n(\varepsilon)\geq0\). Let
\(\delta^*=\sqrt{\ln(2/\varepsilon)/(2n)}\), which is positive because
\(\varepsilon\leq1<2\). By item~1,
\(T_n(\delta^*)\leq2e^{-\ln(2/\varepsilon)}=\varepsilon\), so
\(\delta^*\in A(\varepsilon)\) and \(\delta_n(\varepsilon)\leq\delta^*\).

Item~3. By item~2,
\(0\leq\delta_n(\varepsilon_1)-\delta_n(\varepsilon_2)\leq\delta_n(\varepsilon_1)
\leq\sqrt{\ln(2/\varepsilon_1)/(2n)}\), and the last expression tends to
\(0\) as \(n\to\infty\).

Item~4. For \(\varepsilon\in[\varepsilon_1,1]\), item~2 gives
\(\delta_n(\varepsilon)\leq\delta_n(\varepsilon_1)\leq\sqrt{\ln(2/\varepsilon_1)/(2n)}\),
which is at most \(\varphi\) exactly when
\(n\geq\ln(2/\varepsilon_1)/(2\varphi^2)\). Replacing \(\varepsilon_1\) by
\(\varepsilon_1/10\) changes the threshold to
\(\ln(20/\varepsilon_1)/(2\varphi^2)=\ln(2/\varepsilon_1)/(2\varphi^2)+\ln10/(2\varphi^2)\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-absolute-equalities}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-absolute-equalities}{Commutation, idempotence and association in the absolute system}
\begin{proof}
Item~1. A1 for \(x,y\) gives \(p(xy)\geqslant p(yx)\), and A1 for \(y,x\)
gives \(p(yx)\geqslant p(xy)\). Hence \(p(xy)=p(yx)\).

Item~2. A3 gives \(p(xx)\geqslant p(x)\). B1 with \(y:=x\) gives
\(p(x)\geqslant p(xx)\). Hence \(p(xx)=p(x)\).

Item~3. A2 gives \(p((xy)z)\geqslant p(x(yz))\). For the reverse
inequality, apply item~1 and A2 alternately:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 p(x(yz))&=p((yz)x) &&\text{item~1 with the elements }x,\ yz\\
 &\geqslant p(y(zx)) &&\text{A2 with }y,z,x\\
 &=p((zx)y) &&\text{item~1 with }y,\ zx\\
 &\geqslant p(z(xy)) &&\text{A2 with }z,x,y\\
 &=p((xy)z) &&\text{item~1 with }z,\ xy.
\end{aligned}
\]
So \(p(x(yz))\geqslant p((xy)z)\), and with the first inequality the two
are equal. Only A1, A2, A3 and B1 were used.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-association}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-association}{Absolute association from the two multiplication laws}
\begin{proof}
The steps are Popper's (appendix~*iv, note~3); the justifications are
spelled out.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(p((ab)c)=p(ab,c)\,p(c)\): (d) with the element \(ab\) in place of
  \(a\) and \(c\) in place of \(b\).
\item \(p((ab)c)=p(a,bc)\,p(b,c)\,p(c)\): substitute the value of
  \(p(ab,c)\) given by (e) into step~1.
\item \(p(a(bc))=p(a,bc)\,p(bc)\): (d) with \(bc\) in place of \(b\).
\item \(p(a(bc))=p(a,bc)\,p(b,c)\,p(c)\): (d) with \(b,c\) in place of
  \(a,b\) gives \(p(bc)=p(b,c)\,p(c)\); substitute into step~3.
\item The right sides of steps~2 and~4 are the same product of real
  numbers, so \(p((ab)c)=p(a(bc))\).
\end{enumerate}
Steps~2 and~4 replace a probability by an equal number, which is the
substitutivity of equal probabilities assumed in the statement; no
algebraic law for the elements is used.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-cplus-reflexivity}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-cplus-reflexivity}{C\texorpdfstring{\({}^{+}\)}{+} gives A3 and C}
\begin{proof}
Popper's five steps (appendix~*iv, note~8), with each inference made
explicit.

Step~1. Suppose \(p(c,b)+p(\bar c,b)\neq p(b,b)\). C\({}^{+}\) with the
elements \(c,b,b\) in the places of \(a,b,c\) gives \(p(b,b)=p(d,b)\) for
every \(d\); taking \(d=c\) and \(d=\bar c\) gives
\(p(b,b)=p(c,b)=p(\bar c,b)\).

Step~2. Suppose \(p(a,a)\neq p(b,b)\). If
\(p(c,b)+p(\bar c,b)\neq p(a,a)\), then C\({}^{+}\) with \(c,b,a\) in the
places of \(a,b,c\) gives \(p(a,a)=p(d,b)\) for every \(d\), in particular
\(p(a,a)=p(b,b)\), contrary to the supposition. Hence
\(p(c,b)+p(\bar c,b)=p(a,a)\neq p(b,b)\), and step~1 applies:
\(p(b,b)=p(c,b)=p(\bar c,b)\).

Step~3. Under the supposition of step~2,
\(p(a,a)=p(c,b)+p(\bar c,b)=2p(b,b)\).

Step~4. Suppose \(p(b,b)\neq p(a,a)\). Step~3 with \(a\) and \(b\)
interchanged gives \(p(b,b)=2p(a,a)\), and step~3 itself gives
\(p(a,a)=2p(b,b)\). So \(p(b,b)=4p(b,b)\), whence \(p(b,b)=0\) and then
\(p(a,a)=0=p(b,b)\), contradicting the supposition.

Step~5. Hence \(p(a,a)=p(b,b)\) for all \(a,b\).

For C: suppose it is not the case that \(p(b,b)=p(c,b)\) for every \(c\),
and suppose \(p(a,b)+p(\bar a,b)\neq p(b,b)\). C\({}^{+}\) with \(a,b,b\)
gives \(p(b,b)=p(d,b)\) for every \(d\), a contradiction. So
\(p(a,b)+p(\bar a,b)=p(b,b)\) whenever the exception of C does not apply,
which is C.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-consistency}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-consistency}{Consistency models and the assertion (E)}
\begin{proof}
Item~1. Write \(t(b)=\operatorname{top}(b)\) for nonempty \(b\). All values
of \(p\) lie in \(\{0,1\}\), and \(S\) is closed under \(\cap\) and
complement, so the closure parts of postulates~3 and~4 hold; \(S\) is
finite, so postulate~1 holds.

A1: \(\Omega\neq\varnothing\), \(t(\Omega)\notin\varnothing\) and
\(t(\Omega)\in\Omega\), so \(p(\varnothing,\Omega)=0\neq1=p(\Omega,\Omega)\);
hence for every \(a,b\) one of these two pairs has a different value.

A2: if \(p(a,c)=p(b,c)\) for every \(c\), take \(c=\{\omega\}\) for each
\(\omega\in\Omega\); then \(t(c)=\omega\), so \(\omega\in a\) if and only if
\(\omega\in b\). Thus \(a=b\), and the conclusion of A2 is immediate.

A3: \(p(\varnothing,\varnothing)=1\), and for nonempty \(a\),
\(t(a)\in a\) gives \(p(a,a)=1\). So \(p(a,a)=1=p(b,b)\).

B1: if \(c=\varnothing\) both sides are \(1\). Otherwise
\(ab\subseteq a\), so \(t(c)\in ab\) implies \(t(c)\in a\), that is
\(p(ab,c)\leqslant p(a,c)\).

B2: if \(c=\varnothing\) then \(bc=\varnothing\) and both sides equal
\(1=1\cdot1\). Let \(c\neq\varnothing\) and \(t=t(c)\). If \(t\in b\), then
\(t\in bc\subseteq c\), so \(bc\) is nonempty and its greatest point is
\(t\); hence \(p(a,bc)=[t\in a]\), \(p(b,c)=1\), and the right side is
\([t\in a]=[t\in a\cap b]=p(ab,c)\), using \(t\in b\). If \(t\notin b\),
then \(p(b,c)=0\), so the right side is \(0\), and \(t\notin ab\) gives
\(p(ab,c)=0\). Here \([\,\cdot\,]\) is \(1\) for a true and \(0\) for a false
condition.

C: if \(b=\varnothing\) then \(p(c,b)=1=p(b,b)\) for every \(c\), the
exception applies. If \(b\neq\varnothing\), exactly one of \(a,\bar a\)
contains \(t(b)\), so \(p(a,b)+p(\bar a,b)=1=p(b,b)\).

Item~2. For \(\Omega=\{\omega\}\), \(S=\{\varnothing,\Omega\}\); write
\(0=\varnothing\), \(1=\Omega\). Intersection and complement become
arithmetical product and \(1-a\). By the definition,
\(p(a,0)=1\), \(p(1,1)=1\) and \(p(0,1)=0\), which is Popper's table.

Item~3. Under \(0=\varnothing\), \(1=\{u\}\), \(2=\{v\}\), \(3=\Omega\),
intersection is the numerically smaller of the two elements except
\(\{u\}\cap\{v\}=\varnothing\), that is \(1.2=2.1=0\); complement is
\(3-a\). Since \(u<v\), \(t(\{u\})=u\) and \(t(\{v\})=t(\Omega)=v\). So
column \(0\) is all \(1\); column \(1\) is \([u\in a]\), equal to \(1\) for
\(a=1,3\); columns \(2\) and \(3\) are \([v\in a]\), equal to \(1\) for
\(a=2,3\). This is the displayed table. By item~1 the model satisfies
A1--C.

Item~4. \(p(2,3)=1\) from the table, and \(bc=3.1=1\) with \(p(2,1)=0\).

Item~5. In the trivial model, \(\bar0=1\) and \(\bar1=0\);
\(p(0,1)=0\neq1=p(1,0)\) and \(p(1,0)=1\neq0=p(0,1)\), so (0) holds. In the
four-element model, \(\bar1=2\) and \(p(1,2)=0=p(2,1)\), so (0) fails.
Both models satisfy the postulates and A1--C (items~1--3). A formula
derivable from the postulates and axioms holds in every model of them;
(0) fails in one model and its negation fails in another, so neither is
derivable.
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}

\begin{annexitem}{annex:p15-w02b-p15-027-independence}{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-independence}{Independence of the six operational axioms}
\begin{proof}
In each model \(S\) is finite and closed under the given product and
complement. A2 is called vacuous when distinct elements \(a\neq b\) never
satisfy \(p(a,c)=p(b,c)\) for every \(c\), since for \(a=b\) its
conclusion is trivial. C is checked for each \(b\) in turn; it holds
automatically when \(p(c,b)\) is the same for every \(c\).

Item~1 (A1). Every value is \(0\), so A1 fails. A2: the hypothesis may
hold, and the conclusion \(0=0\) holds. A3: \(0=0\). B1: \(0\leqslant0\).
B2: \(0=0\cdot0\). C: \(p(c,b)=0=p(b,b)\) for every \(c\), the exception
applies.

Item~2 (A2). A1: \(p(0,2)=0\neq1=p(0,0)\). A2 fails: \(p(1,c)=1=p(2,c)\)
for every \(c\), yet \(p(0,1)=1\neq0=p(0,2)\). A3: \(p(0,0)=p(1,1)=p(2,2)=1\).
B1 can fail only where \(p(a,c)=0\), that is \(a=0\), \(c=2\); then
\(ab=0.b=0\) and \(p(0,2)=0\). B2: the products are \(0.b=b.0=0\),
\(1.1=1\), \(1.2=2\), \(2.1=1\), \(2.2=2\). For \(c=0\) or \(c=1\), every
\(p(\cdot,c)\) and \(p(\cdot,bc)\) equals \(1\) because \(bc\in\{0,1\}\),
so both sides are \(1\). For \(c=2\): if \(b=0\), then \(p(b,2)=0\) and
\(ab=0\), so both sides are \(0\); if \(b\in\{1,2\}\), then \(bc=2\) and
\(p(b,2)=1\), so the right side is \(p(a,2)\), which is \(0\) exactly when
\(a=0\), and \(ab=0\) exactly when \(a=0\), so the left side
\(p(ab,2)\) agrees. C: for \(b=0\) and \(b=1\) all \(p(c,b)\) equal \(1\);
for \(b=2\), \(p(0,2)+p(\bar0,2)=0+p(1,2)=1\) and, for \(a=1,2\),
\(p(a,2)+p(0,2)=1+0=1\), each equal to \(p(2,2)=1\).

Item~3 (A3). A1: \(p(1,1)=1\neq0=p(0,0)\), and this pair also shows A3
fails. A2 is vacuous, since \(p(0,1)=0\neq1=p(1,1)\). B1: the left side
is \(1\) only if \(ab=1\) and \(c=1\), so \(a=1\) and the right side is
\(1\). B2: the right side is \([a=1\text{ and }bc=1]\cdot[b=1\text{ and
}c=1]=[a=b=c=1]\), which is the left side \([ab=1\text{ and }c=1]\). C:
for \(b=0\), every \(p(c,0)=0=p(0,0)\); for \(b=1\), exactly one of
\(a,\bar a\) is \(1\), so the sum is \(1=p(1,1)\).

Item~4 (B1). Here \(p(a,\pm1)=0\) and \(p(a,0)=a\). A1:
\(p(1,0)=1\neq0=p(1,1)\). A2 is vacuous, since \(p(a,0)=a\) separates
distinct elements. A3: \(p(a,a)=a(1-|a|)=0\) for each of \(-1,0,+1\). B1
fails: with \(a=-1\), \(b\in\{-1,0\}\), \(c=0\), the left side is
\(ab=-b\), equal to \(1\) or \(0\), and the right side is \(-1\). B2: for
\(c=\pm1\), \(p(b,c)=0\) and \(p(ab,c)=0\); for \(c=0\), \(bc=0\) and both
sides are \(ab\). C: for \(b=\pm1\), every \(p(c,b)=0=p(b,b)\); for
\(b=0\), \(p(a,0)+p(-a,0)=0=p(0,0)\).

Item~5 (B2). A1: \(p(0,1)=0\neq2\). A2 is vacuous, since
\(p(0,1)=0\neq2=p(1,1)\). A3: \(p(0,0)=p(1,1)=2\). B1: every value is at
most \(2\); where \(p(a,c)=0\), \(a=0\), \(c=1\), and \(ab=0\) gives
\(p(0,1)=0\). B2 fails as stated. C: for \(b=0\), every \(p(c,0)=2\); for
\(b=1\), \(p(0,1)+p(1,1)=2=p(1,1)\).

Item~6 (C). The product and \(p\) are those of the trivial model, which
is a ranked point model, so A1, A3, B1 and B2 hold by
\cref{thm:p15-w02b-p15-027-consistency}, items~1--2; those axioms do not
mention complement. A2 is vacuous since \(p(0,1)=0\neq1=p(1,1)\). C fails
for \(a=0\), \(b=1\): \(p(0,1)+p(\bar0,1)=0+0\neq1=p(1,1)\), and the
exception does not apply because \(p(0,1)\neq p(1,1)\).
\end{proof}
\end{annexitem}
