## Controlling mathematical reconstruction

The following account is controlled by the complete printed article, not by
the later publisher summary. It preserves the paper's own formalism
\(SC\), its finite-word congruences, its formula classes, and its theorem
numbering. A modern nondeterministic Büchi tuple is not silently inserted
into the 1962 text. Where the source's infinitely-often clause has the same
mathematical effect as a Büchi recurrence condition, that relationship is
identified as a handbook comparison rather than quoted as the source's
terminology.

### The interpreted formalism \(SC\)

On printed p. 1 Büchi fixes an interpreted language with:

- individual variables \(t,x,y,z,\ldots\) ranging over the natural numbers;
- monadic predicate variables \(q,r,s,i,\ldots\) ranging over arbitrary sets
  of natural numbers;
- the individual constant \(0\);
- the unary successor symbol \( '\);
- propositional connectives; and
- quantifiers of both sorts.

The symbol \(SC\) names this interpreted formalism. Its standing is a
language together with the intended natural-number and full monadic-set
domains, not an automaton or a class of words. The paper calls it a fragment
of restricted second-order arithmetic and notes an interpretation through
binary expansions of nonnegative reals. Its stated purpose is both a
description of definability in \(SC\) and an effective method for deciding
truth of \(SC\)-sentences.

For a tuple \(\mathbf i=(i_1,\ldots,i_k)\) of predicate variables,
\(\mathbf i(n)\in\{0,1\}^k\) is the tuple of truth values at \(n\). Thus an
assignment to \(\mathbf i\) is viewed as the infinite state sequence
\[
 \mathbf i(0)\mathbf i(1)\mathbf i(2)\cdots
\]
over the \(2^k\) possible truth-value states. This is the paper's exact
set-assignment-to-infinite-word bridge. It does not yet add a run relation:
the infinite word is the predicate assignment itself.

The present handbook may relate \(SC\) to the modern name S1S, but the
historical source consistently says \(SC\). Likewise, “sequential calculus”
is the paper's suggested description of \(SC\) as a condition language for
sequential circuits; it is not introduced here as a second formal system.

### Formula classes and the infinitely-often clause

Printed pp. 2–3 introduce a hierarchy \(\Sigma_n,\Pi_n\). The first
existential level has the schematic form
\[
\begin{split}
(\exists\mathbf r)\;&
 A[\mathbf r(0)]
 \land
 (\forall t)\,
 B[\mathbf i(t),\mathbf r(t),\mathbf r(t')]
 \land
 (\exists t)\,C[\mathbf r(t)],
\end{split}
\]
with propositional matrices in the displayed constituents; the dual first
universal level reverses the quantifier and Boolean pattern. Higher levels
alternate monadic predicate blocks in the ordinary way described by the
paper.

The source defines
\[
 (\exists^\omega t)\,D(t)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 (\forall x)(\exists t)\,[x<t\land D(t)].
\]
This says that \(D(t)\) holds at arbitrarily large positions, equivalently
infinitely often. It then defines the class \(\Sigma_1^\omega\) by formulas
of the exact shape
\[
(\exists\mathbf r)\;
 A[\mathbf r(0)]
 \land
 (\forall t)\,
 B[\mathbf i(t),\mathbf r(t),\mathbf r(t')]
 \land
 (\exists^\omega t)\,C[\mathbf r(t)].
\]
The standing of \(\Sigma_1^\omega\) is a class of formulas, and—under the
paper's explicitly announced overloading—also the class of relations on
infinite predicate sequences defined by those formulas. The auxiliary tuple
\(\mathbf r\) supplies finitely many truth-value states; the middle conjunct
is a local transition constraint; the last conjunct requires a
\(C\)-state infinitely often.

There is a precise modern comparison but an equally precise source boundary.
Suppose \(\mathbf i\) has \(k\) coordinates and \(\mathbf r\) has \(\ell\).
To eliminate the comparison into exact finite data, put
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \Gamma&=\{0,1\}^k
   &&\text{(input letters)},\\
 Q&=\{0,1\}^{\ell}
   &&\text{(auxiliary states)},\\
 Q_0&=\{q\in Q:A[q]\}
   &&\text{(initial states)},\\
 \Delta(q,a)&=\{q'\in Q:B[a,q,q']\}
   &&\text{(successor relation)},\\
 F&=\{q\in Q:C[q]\}
   &&\text{(designated states)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
These symbols name only the displayed handbook translation of one
\(\Sigma_1^\omega\)-formula; they are not additional primitives of \(SC\).
The existentially quantified predicate tuple \(\mathbf r\) supplies a state
sequence \(q_0q_1\ldots\). The formula holds of the input word exactly when
some such sequence begins in \(Q_0\), obeys
\(q_{n+1}\in\Delta(q_n,\mathbf i(n))\), and visits \(F\) infinitely often.
Thus the formula is extensionally the start-set presentation of a
nondeterministic Büchi word automaton. Nondeterminism is material:
\(B[a,q,q']\) need not determine a unique \(q'\).

The 1962 article does not package these data as a five-component automaton
tuple, define “run” in this notation, or use “Büchi acceptance” as the name
of the infinitely-often clause. The tuple and terminology above are an
eliminable handbook comparison whose equivalence follows directly from the
printed quantifiers.

The paper also defines a bounded interval notation and the class
\(\Sigma^0\) of “regular formulas.” A regular formula
\(R(\mathbf i,x,y)\) has an auxiliary finite-state recurrence constrained
only on \(x\le t<y\), with an initial condition at \(x\) and an output
condition at \(y\). Its truth depends only on the finite word
\[
 \mathbf i(x)\mathbf i(x+1)\cdots\mathbf i(y-1).
\]
Accordingly, the source also uses \(\Sigma^0\) for the class of finite-word
sets determined by such formulas. This overload is source-declared; it does
not identify a formula with its denotation without context.

### Finite-rank congruences and multiperiodic word sets

Let words be finite sequences of the \(2^k\) states of \(\mathbf i\), and let
juxtaposition denote concatenation. The paper defines a congruence
\(\sim\) on words as an equivalence relation satisfying, for all words
\(u,v,w\),
\[
 u\sim v
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 uw\sim vw
 \ \text{ and }\
 wu\sim wv.
\]
Its rank is the number of equivalence classes; finite rank means that number
is finite. A set of words is called multiperiodic exactly when it is a union
of classes of one finite-rank congruence. Thus “multiperiodic” names a
finite-word language class, not a property of a single infinite word.

These definitions are necessary for the source's two bridges. First,
\(\Sigma^0\)-definable finite-word sets are exactly the behaviors of finite
automata with output. Second, the infinite-word normal form is ultimately
described by repeated blocks drawn from multiperiodic word sets.

### Lemma 1: the Sequential Lemma

Suppose
\(\mathcal E_0,\ldots,\mathcal E_n\) is a finite partition of the finite
words over the states of an infinite sequence \(\mathbf i\). Lemma 1 states
that there are cut points
\[
 x_1<x_2<x_3<\cdots
\]
and one cell \(\mathcal E_c\) such that every word
\[
 \mathbf i(x_p)\mathbf i(x_p+1)\cdots\mathbf i(x_q-1),
 \qquad p<q,
\]
belongs to \(\mathcal E_c\). The assertion concerns every concatenation of
one or more consecutive blocks between the chosen cut points, not merely
adjacent blocks.

The proof colors each two-element set \(\{y_1,y_2\}\) by the partition cell
containing the segment from \(y_1\) through \(y_2-1\). Ramsey's theorem gives
an infinite homogeneous set of cut points, which is exactly the conclusion.
The source prints this proof on p. 3. The lemma is later used in the
complementation argument for \(\Sigma_1^\omega\); it is not an independently
named automaton construction.

### Lemmas 2–6: the finite-word automata bridge

Lemma 2 gives equivalent conditions on a finite-word set \(\mathcal R\):

1. \(\mathcal R\) is determined by a \(\Sigma^0\)-formula; and
2. there is a finite-state recursion
   \[
    \mathbf r(0)=I,
    \qquad
    \mathbf r(t')=J[\mathbf i(t),\mathbf r(t)]
   \]
   and an output condition \(U[\mathbf r(x)]\) such that the word
   \(\mathbf i(0)\cdots\mathbf i(x-1)\) belongs to \(\mathcal R\) exactly
   when the state reached at \(x\) satisfies \(U\).

The source sometimes calls \(\mathcal R\) the behavior of \([I,J,U]\).
This is a deterministic finite-word input/output device. It differs from a
nondeterministic infinite-word acceptor in both its run quantification and its
endpoint output test.

The paper attributes the difficult direction of Lemma 2 to Myhill's subset
construction, with details in Büchi's 1960 paper; the converse is rendered
directly as a regular formula. It then obtains:

- Lemma 3: \(\Sigma^0\)-definable word sets are closed under conjunction,
  disjunction, and complement;
- Lemma 4: bounded existential and universal individual quantification
  preserve \(\Sigma^0\)-definability;
- Lemma 5: endpoint existential and universal claims over a
  \(\Sigma^0\)-word predicate can be put into the first normal-form levels;
  and
- Lemma 6: a finite-word set is the behavior of a finite automaton with output
  exactly when it is multiperiodic.

Lemma 6 is stated but its proof is referred to Rabin and Scott. Consequently,
the paper has not proved every finite-automata fact it uses inside these
eleven pages. What it does establish from Lemmas 2 and 6 is the exact
identification of \(\Sigma^0\)-word sets with multiperiodic word sets.

### Lemmas 7–10: Boolean closure and the infinite-word normal form

Section 4 proves that \(\Sigma_1^\omega\) is closed under Boolean operations.
Lemma 7 supplies disjunction. Lemma 8 converts a conjunction of two
infinitely-often obligations into one \(\Sigma_1^\omega\) obligation by adding
a finite-state predicate that alternates which obligation is awaited.

Lemma 9 is the substantial complement argument. Begin with
\[
 A(\mathbf i)\equiv
 (\exists\mathbf r)\,
 K[\mathbf r(0)]
 \land
 (\forall t)H[\mathbf i(t),\mathbf r(t),\mathbf r(t')]
 \land
 (\exists^\omega t)L[\mathbf r(t)].
\]
For auxiliary states \(V,W\) and an input word \(u\), the source records:

- whether an \(H\)-path labeled by \(u\) runs from \(V\) to \(W\); and
- whether such a path exists which passes through an \(L\)-state.

Two input words are equivalent when both answers agree for every ordered pair
\((V,W)\). If the auxiliary recurrence has \(m\) states, this is the
intersection of \(2m^2\) binary distinctions, so the congruence has rank at
most \(2^{2m^2}\). Lemmas 2 and 6 give \(\Sigma^0\)-formulas for its classes.

The Sequential Lemma then gives a homogeneous infinite block decomposition.
The source forms finitely many properties \(F_{c,d}\), where \(c\) records
the congruence class of the finite initial block and \(d\) records the common
class of all later block concatenations. Two infinite inputs satisfying the
same \(F_{c,d}\) agree on \(A\). The finitely many pairs can therefore be
classified according to whether \(A\) holds on their class, and the complement
of \(A\) is a finite union of the opposite \(F_{c,d}\)'s. Lemmas 7 and 8 put
that union back into \(\Sigma_1^\omega\).

This proof also yields Lemma 10: every \(\Sigma_1^\omega\)-definable set of
infinite state sequences is a finite union of sets of the displayed form
\[
 \mathcal C\,\mathcal D\,\mathcal D\,\mathcal D\cdots,
\]
where \(\mathcal C\) and \(\mathcal D\) are multiperiodic finite-word sets.
The notation expresses an existential factorization into one finite stem
block from \(\mathcal C\) followed by endlessly many finite blocks from
\(\mathcal D\). This is the source's finite-congruence description of the
accepted infinite sequences. It should not be silently replaced by a later
deterministic Muller or Rabin construction.

### Lemma 11 and Theorem 1: collapse to \(\Sigma_1^\omega\)

Lemma 11 says that every \(SC\)-formula \(A(\mathbf i)\) can be transformed
into an equivalent formula in some \(\Sigma_n\). The paper does not print
that induction; it refers to methods similar to its 1960 Lemma 1. A
\(\Sigma_1\)-formula can be transformed into \(\Sigma_1^\omega\), and repeated
use of the complement construction handles the alternating monadic blocks.
Theorem 1 therefore states:

> For every \(SC\)-formula \(A(\mathbf i)\), there is an equivalent
> \(\Sigma_1^\omega\)-formula \(B(\mathbf i)\).

The source further says that \(\Sigma_1^\omega\) and \(\Sigma_2\) define the
same relations, so its \(\Sigma_n,\Pi_n\) hierarchy collapses at level two.
It extends the normal form to formulas with free individual variables and
draws consequences for the finite-set restriction \(SC_{\mathrm{fin}}\).
The algorithmic strengthening of Theorem 1 is deferred until the
effectivity discussion in Section 6.

### Lemma 12 and Theorem 2: the decision method

Lemma 12 decides a \(\Sigma_1^\omega\)-sentence. After the monadic
existential tuple is exposed, its matrix has the form
\[
 C(\mathbf r)=
 K[\mathbf r(0)]
 \land
 (\forall t)H[\mathbf r(t),\mathbf r(t')]
 \land
 (\exists^\omega t)L[\mathbf r(t)].
\]
If \(\mathbf r\) has \(k\) predicate coordinates, it has \(2^k\) possible
truth-value states. Any satisfying infinite sequence contains infinitely many
\(L\)-states, so some such state \(U\) repeats. The sequence therefore
supplies:

- a finite \(H\)-path from a \(K\)-state to \(U\); and
- a nonempty finite \(H\)-cycle from \(U\) back to \(U\).

Conversely, such a stem and cycle produce the infinite sequence obtained by
repeating the cycle, and \(U\) recurs infinitely often. Reachability between
two fixed states needs no path longer than \(2^k\): any longer path repeats a
state, and deleting the intervening loop preserves its endpoints and local
\(H\)-constraints. There are therefore only finitely many stems, cycles, and
states to inspect. This is the paper's exact finite stem-cycle decision
argument.

The article then checks effectivity of the earlier reductions. The
finite-rank congruence in Lemma 9 has an explicit bound, so representatives
and their defining finite automata can be found. Whether a pair
\((c,d)\) belongs to the finite classification used in the complement proof
is itself reduced to a \(\Sigma_1^\omega\)-sentence and decided by Lemma 12.
Thus the normal-form conversion is effective. Theorem 2 states:

> There is an effective method for deciding truth of sentences in \(SC\).

The paper proves decidability, not a modern complexity upper bound. Its
finite enumeration and congruence bound must not be restated as a claimed
elementary, PSPACE, or optimal algorithm. The circuit discussion on pp. 1–2
explains an application—deciding whether a proposed finite-state
input/output transformation meets an \(SC\) condition—but the article gives
no worked circuit instance.

Immediately after Theorem 2 the paper records five consequences and
comparisons:

1. It says decidability of what it prints as \(\Sigma_2^0\)-sentences contains
   Joyce Friedman's result and entails finite-automata algorithms associated
   with Church and Wang. The superscripted formula-class notation is not
   defined in that paragraph and must not be conflated with the earlier
   regular-formula class \(\Sigma^0\).
2. It notes that \(SC\) defines equality, strict order, and congruence modulo
   every positive integer and says this improves a result of Putnam.
3. It notes that “\(\mathbf i\) is finite” is definable. Hence
   \(SC_{\mathrm{fin}}\)—the same interpreted formalism with monadic variables
   restricted to finite subsets of \(\mathbb N\)—is decidable. The article
   cites Büchi 1960 and reports Robinson's attribution to Ehrenfeucht.
4. With \(\mathrm{Nn}\) denoting the natural numbers and \(\mathrm{Pw}\) the
   integral powers of two, it obtains decidability of the first-order theory
   \([\mathrm{Nn},+,\mathrm{Pw}]\), improving Presburger's result.
5. It relates Theorem 2 to the Skolem--Behmann decision theorem for the monadic
   predicate calculus of second order and says that a modified Lemma 11 gives
   a simple solution.

These are source-reported consequences and historical comparisons; the paper
does not reprove the cited external results.

### Theorems 3–4 and the printed open problems

The concluding section first says that a careful analysis of the decision
method would yield a complete axiom system for \(SC\). It proposes as a
candidate schema the homogeneous-sequence fragment of the Sequential Lemma
used in Lemma 9, for each regular formula \(E\in\Sigma^0\). No soundness or
completeness theorem for that proposed axiom system is stated or proved.

The concluding section introduces \(SC_{\mathrm{per}}\), obtained by
restricting monadic variables to ultimately periodic sets of natural numbers.
For explicit standing in the handbook, this means a set \(X\subseteq\mathbb N\)
for which there are \(N,p\in\mathbb N\), with \(p\ge 1\), such that
\(n\in X\) exactly when \(n+p\in X\) for every \(n\ge N\).  This quantifier
expansion is the handbook's eliminable definition of the source phrase, not a
new primitive of \(SC\).
It says the needed homogeneous-block principle follows there from idempotent
powers in a finite semigroup rather than from Ramsey's theorem. Theorem 3 is
stated as:
\[
 \text{an \(SC\)-sentence is true in \(SC_{\mathrm{per}}\)}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \text{it is true in \(SC\)}.
\]
The surrounding remarks outline rather than fully detail this proof.

Using predicate assignments as binary expansions, Theorem 4 says that the
first-order theories of
\[
 [\mathrm{Re},+,\mathrm{Pw},\mathrm{Nn}]
 \quad\text{and}\quad
 [\mathrm{Ra},+,\mathrm{Pw},\mathrm{Nn}]
\]
are arithmetically equivalent and decidable. There is a source-level
terminology discrepancy to preserve: p. 1 describes \(\mathrm{Nn}\) as the
natural numbers, whereas p. 10 says that
\(\mathrm{Re},\mathrm{Ra},\mathrm{Nn},\mathrm{Pw}\) stand respectively for
nonnegative reals, rationals, integers, and integral powers of two. The
handbook should not silently decide whether “integers” on p. 10 was intended
to mean nonnegative integers.

The article also notes that adding the function \(2x\) makes \(SC\)
undecidable, attributed to Robinson. It reports, with attribution to Gödel and
without proof, that replacing monadic predicate quantification by monadic
function quantification makes all recursive relations definable. It then
prints three open problems:

1. whether \(SC^2\), defined there by taking \(2x+1\) and \(2x+2\) as
   primitives instead of successor, is decidable;
2. whether \(SC(\omega^2)\) is decidable, where \(SC(\alpha)\) is defined as
   the variant with ordinal individual domain \(\alpha\) and its well-order as
   a primitive; and
3. whether there is an algorithm deciding, for an arbitrary condition
   \(C(\mathbf i,\mathbf u)\), whether some finite-state recursion realizes
   that condition.

The third is a synthesis problem. Theorem 2 decides whether a given
finite-state system satisfies a condition; it does not solve whether a
finite-state implementation exists.
