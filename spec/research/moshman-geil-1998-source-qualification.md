### Recruitment, allocation and group constitution

At 233, **Method / Subjects**, the sample is 143 students from undergraduate
educational psychology courses, approximately equally divided between women
and men. Participation was one means of fulfilling a class requirement. The
authors' affiliation is UNL, but this paragraph does not separately identify
the sampling institution, number of courses, recruitment dates, ages,
racial/cultural composition, selection/response rate, or details of the
alternative ways to fulfil the requirement. It does not describe informed
consent, exclusions, attrition, or a power analysis. These are reporting gaps,
not evidence that such arrangements did not exist.

At 233--234, **Design**, students were randomly assigned to three conditions:

| Condition | Students | Collaborative units | Sequence |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Individual control | 32 | None | Individual solution and written justification |
| Interactive | 54 | 10 groups of 5 or 6 | Joint discussion, then individually recorded solution/explanation |
| Individual/interactive | 57 | 10 groups of 5 or 6 | Individual solution/explanation, joint discussion, then individually recorded solution/explanation |

The article reports random assignment to condition, not the randomization

### Task, instructions, procedure and timing

At 233, **Materials**, the original abstract selection task is printed on
paper with E, K, 4 and 7 visible on four pictured cards, each specified to have
a letter on one side and a number on the reverse. Its conditional says that
a vowel on one side requires an even number on the other. Students must
select the cards needed to determine conclusively whether the hypothesis
holds for this four-card set, then justify the selection in writing. These
are the actual study cards; the introduction's other letter variant is not
the original experiment's stimulus.

At 234, **Procedure**, all sessions were run by the second author. General
instructions framed the research as problem-solving, required written
justification, and encouraged participants to spend sufficient time to be
satisfied with their solution. Task instructions emphasized the letter/number
two-sided format. The experimenter left the room, observed through a one-way
mirror, videotaped all sessions, and recorded each individual's or group's
total completion time.

For a group without consensus after 25 minutes, the experimenter returned
and gave it another ten minutes to reach consensus. One group still lacked
consensus at 35 minutes; the experimenter then instructed its members to
justify their individual answers for the remaining ten minutes. That group
reached consensus during this additional interval. Do not describe every
group as strictly limited to 35 minutes or as receiving no intervention.

Individual controls were told that they could take as much time as needed,
that 50 minutes' participation was needed for class credit, and that they
were not expected to advance beyond the task. After recording their own
selection and justification, they were given the correct solution and asked
to justify it in writing. This later prompted explanation is not the
individual group's initial correct-choice outcome in Table 1.

Both collaborative conditions aimed at consensus. After an apparently
unanimous judgment, participants recorded and explained the result
individually, with further discussion permitted if wanted. Consequently,
these are immediate responses within the same task/session, not a blinded,
anonymous, delayed or novel-task transfer test. The individual/interactive
condition supplies individual pre-discussion answers; the interactive
condition does not supply an independent pretreatment answer from every
member.

At 242, **Nonrational Influences on Group Behavior**, the authors report that
groups averaged approximately 2.5 times the individual time to a selection.
The article does not supply the individual/group numerical time means,
dispersion, distributions, group-level times or a time-adjusted analysis.
The 20--40 minute classroom conversations at 233 are earlier motivating
observations, not the study's measured duration distribution.

### Scoring and discussion analysis

At 235, **Results**, a correct selection is exactly E and 7, represented as

Adding the 4 or selecting any other combination is not counted as correct.
Table 1 scores the individual or group selection pattern; Table 2 records
individual initial/final patterns in the individual/interactive condition.
At 236, a positive change is incorrect to correct and a negative change
correct to incorrect. Change here is operationalized by selection correctness,
not by a separately reported validated scale of reasoning quality.

Written justifications are required by the procedure, and the two examples

The article provides no formal justification-coding rubric, independent

Discussion evidence consists of selected video-based summaries/transcript

## Results, denominators and reported uncertainty

At 235, **Individuals vs. Groups / Table 1**, the exact correct counts are:

| Outcome unit | Correct | Total | Rate |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Individual control solvers | 3 | 32 | 9.375% (article: 9.4%; abstract: 9%) |
| Interactive group solutions | 7 | 10 | 70% |
| Individual/interactive group solutions | 8 | 10 | 80% |
| Both collaborative conditions pooled | 15 | 20 | 75% |

The arithmetic checks are author qualification, not a new statistical
reanalysis: 32 + 54 + 57 = 143 participants, while the headline comparison
has 32 individual outputs and 20 group outputs, not 143 independent
individual outcomes. Group members are not multiplied into independent
copies of the group-level result. The two group procedures are distinct
despite their pooled abstract summary.

The source reports `chi-square(1, N=52)=23.42, P<.001` for individual versus
pooled group correctness and `chi-square(1, N=20)=0.27, P>.05` for the two
group conditions (235). The latter nonsignificant test does not establish
equivalence of the two procedures. There are only ten groups per procedure.
No confidence intervals, prospective equivalence margin, adjusted model,
preregistered analysis, or individual/group time covariate is reported.
These are the authors' tests, not independently admitted local estimates.

At 236, **Individual Responses / Table 2**, 12 of 57 individual/interactive
participants initially selected the exact correct pattern. Ten of those
remained correct and two became incorrect. Of the initial 45 incorrect
responses, 35 became correct and ten remained incorrect. Thus 45/57 were
correct after discussion, 37 changed correctness (35 positively and two
negatively), and 20 had no correctness change. The paper reports a binomial
`P<.001` among the 37 changes. The 57 students are nested within ten groups;
the article does not report a cluster-adjusted change analysis. The counts
are directly inspectable, while any inference treating all changes as
independent would need that dependence addressed.

At 236--237, **Individual/Interactive Group Patterns**, three groups began
with a majority incorrect `p,q` response; two ended correct. Another three
had modal but nonmajority `p,q`; two ended correct. A seventh group's modal
response was all four cards; it ended correct. The final three had no modal
pattern and all ended correct. Seven groups initially contained at least
one exact-correct member; five ended correct and two did not. The three
groups without any exact-correct member all ended correct. In no group was
the exact-correct initial selection the modal response. These finite results
make an initially correct member neither necessary nor sufficient for
success in this sample; they are not a universal theorem about groups.

At 242--243, the authors report that 49% of controls asked to justify the
given correct answer either rejected it or did not know why those cards
should be turned; most of the remaining 51% gave ambiguous justifications.
The article supplies neither a coding rule nor explicit counts for that
49/51 split. Do not invent an integer count or recode this prompted stage
as an independent posttest success rate.

## Mechanism evidence and limits of the causal interpretation

At 237--241, the two detailed groups show distributed partial insights,
challenge, revision and integration, not simple initial selection of one
member's already complete correct answer. Group 1 first reconsidered 7,
developed the irrelevance of K and then of 4, and ultimately selected E and
7. Group 2 integrated one member's argument for 7 with another's argument
against 4. At 244--245, the authors trace the contributions and describe
at least seven of the ten illustrated members as actively involved.

At 242--245, the authors interpret the outcome and videos as collaborative
co-construction of a justified argument structure, rather than imitation or
peer pressure. The actual discriminating evidence has bounded force:

- The three all-initially-incorrect groups directly challenge an explanation
  restricted to copying an initially complete correct selection. They do not
  rule out distributed partial competence, insight emerging in one member
  during discussion, or later interpersonal influence.
- Correct answers were never initially majority/modal choices in the ten
  pretested groups. This challenges simple conformity to the initial modal
  answer; it does not measure or eliminate conformity to a later apparent
  consensus, confidence, dominance or differential social influence.
- The videos and immediate written explanations support the authors'
  account in the illustrated cases. There is no randomized argumentation
  versus pressure manipulation or systematic process-coding reliability.
- Groups spent more time than individuals. Generous solo instructions and
  difficulty explaining the supplied answer are the authors' counterargument
  (242--243), but no time-yoked control separates discussion from time,
  persistence, motivation or attention. The study supports a benefit of the
  implemented collaborative procedure; it does not isolate every ingredient.
- Random assignment to condition is reported. Initial selections and
  justifications were measured in the individual/interactive condition,
  but social influence/status and a separate standardized measure of prior
  competence were not reported. The authors'
  inference at 243 that there was no reason for particular members to be
  influential independently of argument quality is not an empirical
  demonstration that such differences were absent.

These qualifications do not erase the observed group advantage. They keep
the stronger mechanism-exclusion language in the abstract/discussion
attributed to the authors rather than promoting it to an independently
established unique causal explanation.

## Generalization and integration boundary

At 245--246, **Individual and Collective Rationality**, the authors themselves
avoid downplaying individual reasoning or insisting that collaborative
processes are categorically different from internal argumentation. They
allow group success to depend on constituent individual competence and
offer a prediction about younger children, not new child data. At 246 they
explicitly decline to claim that this brief interaction produced a
generalizable, durable increase in individual hypothesis-testing competence.
Repeated collaborative learning and developmental benefit remain plausible
extensions in their discussion, not tested transfer outcomes here.

This paper therefore supplies one bounded original experiment: course-based
undergraduates, a single abstract conditional task, groups of five/six,
consensus-oriented discussion with written explanations, and immediate
selection outcomes. It does not establish that groups normally outperform
individuals across domains, group sizes, incentives or cultures; guarantee
resistance to groupthink; demonstrate durable learning; or independently
validate all Piagetian/Habermasian theoretical claims or cited studies.
The small group counts, unspecified within-condition group construction,
missing formal process coding and unresolved time/process separation remain
visible qualifications, not reasons to return to an abstract-only record.
