## Source coverage and exact empirical distinctions

The chapter uses independently worded descriptions of the source stimuli
and preserves its stated populations, procedures, outcomes and numerical
summaries. Historical forecasts and clinical/crime scenarios remain
elicitation materials. Their content is not asserted as current fact.

### Word availability and representativeness, pp. 295–296

The 60-second seven-letter word-production contrast compares the suffix
ing with n in position six: means 6.4 and 2.9, t(44)=4.70, p<.01. Separate
group sizes are not printed and are not reconstructed from the degrees of
freedom. Production is distinguished from a census of the word classes.

The four-page, approximately 2000-word frequency question has the actual
seven response categories. Reported medians are 13.4 (N=52) versus 4.7
(N=53), with the reported median-test p<.01. The parallel ly/l comparison
reports 8.8 versus 4.4 without its own Ns or test; neither is invented.

The Stanford representative/frequent contrasts retain the 105/102 group
sizes and the reported 68%/78% weight judgments and 65%/83% actress
judgments. These event pairs are not nested. They demonstrate a reported
typicality/frequency dissociation, not a conjunction-error percentage.

### Bill and Linda, pp. 297–298

Both full profile fact sets, the eight candidate descriptions, the
five common fillers and the representative/atypical constituent roles
are supplied. The separate representativeness task has N=88 and the
reported 87%/85% orderings. Those percentages are not probability errors.

The direct and indirect conditions are explained before Table 1. One
participant could contribute a direct answer on one problem and an
indirect answer on the other. Indirect Ns are totals split approximately
equally between constituent and conjunction groups, not Ns for each
group. The complete table preserves these source cells:

### Transparent Linda comparisons, pp. 299–300

The chapter retains N=142 and 85% for the direct choice; N=119, ratings
3.5/5.6 and 82% for the nine-point task; N=58 and 65% for choosing the
representativeness argument; and N=75, ratings 3.8/5.1, 57% above and 16%
below for explicitly inclusive teller wording. The reported mean
difference has p<.01. The separate-group means 3.3, 3.9 and 4.5 are
identified as such; missing Ns and exact tests are not supplied.
The hypothetical ten-dollar bet has N=60 and 56% choosing the narrower
event. The separate statistically trained graduate group has N=64,
means 3.8/3.5 and 36% reversals. Rating scores are not rescaled and
called numerical probabilities. Choosing an argument is a distinct
dependent measure from judging a probability.

### Clinical and sports tasks, pp. 301–303

The clinical cohorts are the 37 Boston internists on the Harvard course
and 66 New England Medical Center internists. Five constructed problems
were counterbalanced across initial indirect and subsequent direct
questions. Only the source's printed vignette is reconstructed in full:
the 55-year-old woman's postoperative pulmonary embolism, angiography,
dyspnea and hemiparesis alternatives. Unprinted stimuli are not invented.
The independent representativeness sample has 32 Stanford physicians.
Aggregate correlations exceed .95; direct mean ranks are 2.7/4.6,
indirect 2.8/4.3; reversals range from 73% to 100%, averaging 91%.
The 60-of-62 inclusive-wording responses are not treated as another
independent experiment. The further discussion with 24 physicians is
qualitative and has no manufactured coding frequencies.

The tennis forecast recruited 93 Oregon respondents in October 1980 and
conditioned the 1981 question on Borg reaching the final. All four mean
ranks (1.7, 2.7, 2.2, 3.5) and the 72% critical reversal are retained.
The separate 56-person Stanford sequence-classification check does not
establish the interpretations of those original forecasters.

### Twenty-roll dice task, pp. 303–304

The fair die has four green and two red faces. The prospect is a string
occurring in 20 rolls, for the same 25-dollar prize. The exact strings
are RGRRR, GRGRRR and GRRRRR; the modified comparison uses RGRRRG.
The representativeness ordering is reported for N=50, with 88% choosing
the second as first and the third as last. N=260 spans the UBC/Stanford
choice tasks, including 125 actual-payment participants and the remaining
hypothetical condition: 65%/62% prefer the second, 2% the third in both.
The modified task has N=59 and 63%; the separate argument choice has
N=88 and 76% preferring the valid inclusion argument.

and a complete prefix-state counting derivation. Overlapping occurrences
are counted correctly. The exact probabilities below are independent

### Causal scenarios, pp. 305–308

Figure 1's signed associative relations are reconstructed as those
relations, not as identified structural causal models. The same-person
health choice has N=115 and 58%; the different-person-age comparison has
N=90 and 29%. The running task has N=96, with 76% ranking the conjunction
above at least one constituent and 48% above both; the separate 4:06/sub-
four-minute comparison is also retained.

The crime-profile comparison has N=171 split 86/85, means 3.17 versus
2.90, the reported p<.05 t-test, and the separate 23%/50% relation to the
addiction filler. These are separate-group ratings. The qualitative
violent-police/drug/assault example is retained without invented Ns.

The April 1982 UBC forecast has N=245, the 1983 flood question and the
more-than-1000-drownings criterion. Geometric means are 2.2% for the
broader flood and 3.1% for earthquake-plus-flood, with p<.01 by the
reported Mann–Whitney test. The intended shared geographic/outcome
condition is explicit. The July 1982 Istanbul conference sample has
N=115: diplomatic .14% versus invasion-plus-diplomatic .47% (p<.01),
and oil-consumption .22% versus price-plus-consumption .36% (reported
significant, without a fabricated exact test). The nine probability
categories are retained. Per-item Ns are not printed and not assumed.
These are historical judgments, not empirical forecast-accuracy results.

### Frequency elicitation and interpretation, pp. 308–315

All health-frequency conditions and samples are distinguished:

- Naive percentage task: N=147, 65% reversals, mean broad probability
  18% and mean joint probability 30%.
- Advanced Stanford task: N=62, 28%, as reported in the footnote.
- Percentage task with the over-55 question first: N=159, 31%.
- Counts out of a hypothetical 100: N=117, 25%.
- Counts with the over-55 count first: N=360, 11%.
- Separate explicit conditional judgment: N=55, mean 59%; the original
  reversal subgroup's mean joint estimate is 45%, not a new cohort.

The hypothetical denominator 100 is not a participant count. Rounded
percentages are not inverted into fictitiously exact raw counts.
Qualitative profile-omission, bored-Bill/jazz and mathematics/physics
examples remain qualitative where the source gives no Ns.
The paper's project-level statement of more than 3000 participants
does not define one pooled representative experiment. Its selected
demonstrations do not estimate the prevalence of error in everyday life.

The concluding treatment preserves the source's conceptual-fallacy and
miscommunication criteria and distinguishes recognition of inclusion,
pragmatic interpretation, representative fit, conditional probability,
evidence likelihood, coherence and empirical calibration. It does not
infer a unique mental process from an observed response pattern.
