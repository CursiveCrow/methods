### C01: components as carriers of transfer, actual limitations

The candidate develops two linked hypotheses: transfer is carried by reusable
knowledge components and external symbolic languages help learners acquire
such components. It refuses both a simple yes/no answer about general transfer
and the idea that instruction can create expertise in every domain without
domain-specific content. A component is a proposed latent representation or
function, not simply an observable stimulus-response link or a list of words
the student can repeat. Its scope includes skills, schemas, representations,
metacognition, learning strategies, motivation/disposition and self-beliefs.
Giving these a common name does not establish any particular causal mechanism.
The author's computational description is not identified with the mental
function itself (especially closing section, galley713).

Scope is the range of contexts where the component is appropriately usable,
not its verbal generality. The programming ladder moves from similar tasks,
to tasks within one language, to other programming languages, to nonprogramming
debugging. Note2 contrasts memorizing particular words with acquiring
letter/sound mappings that can serve new words. Note4 preserves decoding
versus comprehension: decoding skill does not guarantee comprehension.

The combining-like-terms examples distinguish over-specific and over-general
representations: learning only to combine visible positive numbers before
x fails on an implicit coefficient in x+5x and on signed coefficients in
-3x+4x. Ignoring grouping can turn3(x+2)+4x into7x+2 incorrectly. The correct
results are6x, x, and7x+6, respectively; at x=0 the last incorrect expression
gives2 instead of6. These are exact algebra checks, not observed new training
outcomes. Any future body theorem claiming them should receive its real proof
at the assigned annex rather than an empirical-transfer proof status.

The three cautions about "big ideas" are substantive: vague general heuristics
do not specify applicability; a useful general strategy may still depend on
domain fluency; some general strategies may already be acquired without the
proposed formal instruction. The source contrasts conditioned induction
heuristics with a bare instruction to draw diagrams. The function-induction
example divides y-values1,3,6,10 by x-values2,3,4,5 to obtain.5,1,1.5,2;
recognizing its linear pattern distinguishes success after a common working-
backward strategy. The four data pairs alone do not uniquely determine a
function; any future worked construction must distinguish a task's intended
rule from a logically forced interpolation.

The LOGO third-grade/twelve-week report and self-explanation findings remain
reports from this related review, not acquired original experiments. The
source's "small-big" idea means broad productive reuse, not intrinsic
complexity: phoneme mappings and distributive knowledge exemplify it.

### C02: task analysis and instructional redesign

ACT/ACT-R modularity and context sensitivity motivate isolating components
without stripping away the cues needed for valid application. Procedural
production rules have applicability conditions and actions/subgoals/retrieval;
declarative retrieval depends on active context. This is a cognitive modeling
framework, not a new deductive calculus. The reported seven-study task-analysis
review's mean effect1.7 is review-level: the candidate does not supply a shared
participant denominator, heterogeneity or interval and cannot establish a
universal instructional gain.

The algebra story/equation example is62% versus43% on matched representations,
against some experts' expectation that a story requires prior equation solving.
Its worked equation6x+66=81.90 gives x=2.65. The task demands need not be a
simple story→equation→solution pipeline: a learner can use another strategy.
The candidate's expert-blind-spot interpretation is a competing psychological
account, not proved by the two percentages alone.

Three methods are developed: think-aloud analysis of novices/experts,
including experts outside their familiar domain; Difficulty Factors Assessment
(DFA), a factorial arrangement of matched item factors and forms; and
educational-data mining of step-level longitudinal logs. DFA examples vary
story/words/equation, unknown in start/result position, and whole/rational
numbers. Tutor logs supply first-try errors, assisted subsequent steps and
longitudinal practice, but are not automatically as controlled/balanced as
designed quizzes. Neither a cross-sectional difficulty gap nor a fitted
component label alone establishes transfer or a unique latent mechanism.

The overlap model has taskA needing a recursive-expression-production component
and taskB needing it plus English-to-operation interpretation; the nonoverlap
model assigns separate components. Sequence/intervention evidence can
distinguish their predictions only under actual order/assignment and practice
conditions. Initial43% versus27% success is not that evidence by itself.

### C03: symbolic languages, LM/NLM and mechanisms not settled

The candidate defines language broadly as a culturally transmitted external
symbol system: speech/writing, algebra/calculus/probability notation, graphs,
scientific visualizations and programming. Visible representations can make
abstract patterns available to perceptual processing and external memory;
internalized expertise can later simulate such operations without paper.
Merely being handed a notation does not confer fluency in it.

Non-language-mediated (NLM) learning is not defined as wholly unconscious.
Notes6 and10 explicitly distinguish it from a strong implicit-learning claim:
learners can know that they are learning algebra or recognize a result without
verbally mediating every detail. No verbal instruction is evidence about
instructional presentation, not proof that no covert self-explanation occurred.
Computational feasibility of nonverbal learning is not identification of the
human mechanism. Language-mediated (LM) learning, classroom dialogue,
explanations and naming components remain useful. The chapter's open questions
ask about their separate mechanisms, continued NLM use after language learning,
and independent versus subordinate operation. Understanding may precede some
practice and follow other practice; no universal ordering is established.

The closing82% versus44% equation/story relationship in note11 is conditional
performance associated with equation success, not randomized proof that algebra
competence caused the difference. The candidates concerning preschool relational
language, symbolic invention and classroom self-explanation are review-level
unless their actual originals are acquired for stronger claims.

difficulty rankings. The displayed grammar is schematic and should not be
misrepresented as an unambiguous parsing/precedence specification.

The paired plumber/electrician examples produce60x+30 and50h+35. Changing
numbers/cover stories, changing measured quantities, changing slope sign,

### C05: practice versus transfer, content-dependent strategies

The candidate contrasts testing/practice with worked-example study and proposes
a content-by-method interaction: simpler facts might favor earlier recall
practice, while complex schema induction might benefit from more example
study. It explicitly says that interaction had not been tested to the authors'
knowledge. It is not an established universal scheduling rule.

Observed transfer from substitution to new story symbolization is a defined
algebra transfer, not an increase in general intelligence, proof skill or
unmeasured everyday rationality. Similar external answer structure, productive
feedback and constrained symbolic representation are proposed mechanisms;
their separate causal effects are not isolated merely by the package comparison.
