# P19.6 — Analogy and problem transfer

## Completed manuscript scope

The chapter supplies all five numbered experiments in Gick and
Holyoak (1980), their auxiliary source-prompt rating and practical
choice tasks, and the distinct Gentner (1983) theoretical presentation.
There are three complete formal benchmark statement/proof pairs.
The empirical results are evidence records, not proved theorems.

The experimental coverage comprises seven task records: five
numbered experiments, one source-prompt rating task with an
additional Parade condition and overlapping Attack comparison,
and one separate twenty-person practical-choice task.
The two presentations of the source-rating task are not counted
as independent replications. No overall unique-person count
across all experiments is inferred.

## Experimental evidence inventory

### Experiment I — source story and think-aloud production

Printed pp. 316–331. Forty University of Michigan
introductory-psychology undergraduates, course credit,
ten per Attack-Dispersion, Open Supply Route, Tunnel
and no-story control. Individual tape-recorded sessions,
candle-problem practice, source read aloud and summarized,
initial instruction to use the story, rereading permitted.
Further clarification and analogy prompts were possible.
Two independent transcript scorers; blinding is not stated.

Table 3's nonexclusive percentages are preserved in full:

| Condition | Dispersion | Open passage | Operation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Attack | 100 | 10 | 30 |
| Open route | 10 | 70 | 50 |
| Tunnel | 20 | 30 | 80 |
| Control | 0 | 20 | 50 |

Suggestions later rejected by their authors still count.
One open-route response was abstract rather than a concrete
passage. Seven Attack participants solved without a further
prompt, three after one; all had the initial hint.
Four of seven Open Route solvers and four of eight Tunnel
operation proposers received further prompts.

The full reported G-squared comparisons, other-proposal
means, tissue-protection frequencies, within-person
practicality/creativity ratings and their tests are in
the actual chapter. The nonanalogous-proposal count
removes different categories for each story group and
none for controls. The selected transcript illustrations
are paraphrased; their causal ambiguity and the
experimenter's unequal prompting are retained.
They are not represented as an exhaustive process code.

### Auxiliary source-prompt rating task

Printed pp. 321–323 and 333. Table 4 reports 51
participants, seventeen for each of Attack, Open Route
and Tunnel. Each person rates six proposed target
solutions on a nine-point likelihood scale, with 9
maximum; proposal order is randomized. This is different
from the seven-point self-ratings with 1 maximum.

All eighteen Table 4 means and all reported
Newman–Keuls comparisons are preserved in the chapter.
The six categories, in order, are dispersion, existing
esophageal route, inserted tube, exposing incision,
repeated low intensity and intermediate intensity.
The reported interaction is F(10,240)=8.84, p<.001.

The Parade condition adds seventeen people to this
task. The Attack–Parade analysis uses the Attack
group already represented in Table 4; it is not a
new independent group of 34. The source reports
F<1 for the main effect and F(5,160)=1.32, p>.25
for the interaction. The six Parade means are not
printed, and no such means are fabricated.
Nonsignificance is not claimed to establish equivalence.

### Auxiliary practical-choice task

Printed pp. 324–325. An independent group of twenty
undergraduates unfamiliar with the target chose
between dispersion and an esophageal route.
Fifteen preferred dispersion, reported sign-test p<.05.
Eleven of those fifteen cited possible tissue injury.
The task measures stated practical judgment, not
solution generation or actual clinical outcomes.

### Experiment II — matched endings, different problem structure

Printed pp. 332–337. Initially 143 students in five
introductory classes: Attack 47, Parade 46, control 50.
One familiar Parade participant was excluded, leaving
47, 45 and 50. Written booklets, source summary,
initial analogy hint and rereading permission; no
interactive clarification. Two independent scorers
were blind to condition, resolving disagreements
through discussion.

Complete dispersion requires multiple directions,
low intensity and simultaneity. Partial dispersion
requires directions but may omit the other features
or permit some tissue damage.

The complete Table 10 and all eighteen Table 11
helpfulness percentages are preserved. The Table 10
Attack display 57+19=76 adds rounded component
percentages. It is not silently treated as an exact
76/100 proportion for the forty-seven participants.
The Table 11 Parade partial row totals 101 because
of rounding. Its denominators are response subgroups,
not all recruited participants.

Every reported central comparison, proposal-rank
result, other-proposal mean, tissue-protection frequency
and retrospective-helpfulness qualification is supplied.
The source's degrees-of-freedom inconsistency is
corrected as described below.

### Experiment III — self-generated source solutions

Printed pp. 337–340. Forty-six students in two
introductory classes, one familiar participant
excluded, forty-five analyzed. Participants solve
the source problem without its supplied ending,
then the target with a hint to use their own prior
solutions; rereading remains permitted.
Two independent scorers resolve disagreements by
discussion; blinding is not stated.

The two rows of the source-dispersion association
are self-selected outcomes, not randomized conditions:
22 source solvers produce 9 target solutions
(5 complete, 4 partial); the other 23 produce
3 (2 complete, 1 partial).
All stated G-squared comparisons with the other
row and the earlier control/supplied-source groups
are retained with their different design meanings.

The mean of 3.04 source proposals, ten imperfect
source dispersion descriptions and four transfers
among them are retained. The successive-group
association, 10/24 versus 3/21 accumulated-radiation
proposals, is also included. Source and target
proposal categories can overlap; they are not
summed as disjoint participant categories.

The source's description of helpfulness among the
nine dual solvers combines an all-but-one phrase
with another nonresponse. It does not give an
unambiguous complete allocation; no exact count
is invented. The 58% not-helpful report for the
remaining participants and the two positive/one
missing responses among the three other target
solvers are retained.

### Experiment IV — hint after recall among distractors

Printed pp. 340–343. Twenty-seven paid undergraduates
from the Human Performance Center pool, explicitly
randomly assigned: Hint 12, No Hint 15.
Fixed story order: Wine Merchants, The General,
Identical Twins. Each story is studied for three
minutes, collected, and recalled for up to fifteen
minutes. The relevant source is in the middle.
After a three-to-five-minute break, target solving
uses either the hint sentence or its omission.

Two independent scorers fully agree. Hint gives
11/12 complete solutions. No Hint gives 3/15
solutions, one complete and two partial.
The reported G-squared(1)=15.5, p<.001, is retained.

Three No Hint participants report using the source;
only one also produces dispersion. The two partial
solvers deny using the stories. The overlap of
the production and self-report groups is therefore
one, not three. Twelve report no thought of using
the stories. The source's inference that perhaps
only one noticed the full useful analogy is
kept distinct from directly measured mental access.

The 43-unit gist scoring, means 32.08 and 33.53,
t<1, and universal recall of the three stated
setting/road units are preserved. Recall and
relevance detection remain separate measures;
no equivalence test for memory is claimed.

### Experiment V — story placement and first-success stages

Printed pp. 343–346. Forty-seven paid undergraduates:
Story First 17, Story Second 20, Incubation Control 10.
Assignment is reported, but randomization is not
explicitly stated. Story First has a single source
recall task before the first target attempt.
Story Second has an initial ten-minute target attempt,
then source recall, then an unhinted attempt and
a later hinted attempt. The control substitutes
the irrelevant twins story. The Story First
follow-up hint is conditional on the no-use response,
as described in the actual methods.

All first-success stages in Table 12 are preserved:

| Group | Before story | After story, no hint | Later with hint | Never | N |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Story First | not applicable | 41% | 35% | 24% | 17 |
| Story Second | 10% | 35% | 30% | 25% | 20 |
| Incubation Control | 10% | 0% | 0% | 90% | 10 |

The uniquely compatible integer stage counts are
(7,6,4), (2,7,6,5) and (1,0,0,9), respectively.
The text independently identifies the seven early
Story First solvers and two prestory Story Second
solvers. One Story First solution is partial;
the source does not assign its stage in that
sentence, and no stage is invented.

The pooled 16/37 before-hint count includes two
participants who solved before seeing the source.
Only fourteen first solve after the source and
before the hint. The eventual count is 28/37.
The source's 43% and 76% are therefore not
interchangeable measures of spontaneous analogy use.
The self-report interpretation, 35% in both groups,
and the 47%/40% relevance reports are retained.

The comparison with Experiment IV changes both
story count and serial position and has reported
G-squared(1)=2.64, p=.10. The eventual comparison
with the incubation control has G-squared(1)=15.0,
p<.001. The control's only solver succeeded
before the break, but this small condition is
not treated as a general no-incubation theorem.

## The statistical-label correction

On printed p. 334 the Attack–Parade any-dispersion
comparison is printed as G-squared(2)=7.70, p<.01.
For the stated two-condition binary response
comparison, the contingency table has one degree
of freedom, not two.

Under ordinary rounding, the component percentages
and reported Ns uniquely reconstruct Attack
complete/partial counts 27 and 9 and Parade counts
14 and 8. The corresponding binary table is:

| Condition | Any dispersion | None |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Attack | 36 | 11 |
| Parade | 22 | 23 |

A single authoring calculation of
2 sum O_ij log(O_ij/E_ij), with expected counts
E_ij=row_i column_j/92, gives
7.697356090576365, which rounds to the printed
7.70 and uses one degree of freedom.
This is a conditional reconstruction from the
rounded printed table, not participant-level
raw-data reanalysis.

The actual chapter states the correct binary
comparison and one degree of freedom. It does
not repeat the inconsistent source label or
claim an unreported three-category analysis.
No test program or manuscript scanner was created.

## Distinct theory accounts and exact qualifications

Gick and Holyoak's Figure 1/Table 1 are approximate
analyst representations, with some propositions
inferred rather than literally stated. Their
three stages are representation, mapping and
target-solution generation. Relations may be
semantically transformed. They discuss abstraction
levels, solution focusing and the source/target
role mismatch involving army and rays.
The military radial roads have no explicitly
given counterpart in the target description.
The chapter does not assert a perfect isomorphism.

Gentner's account distinguishes object attributes,
object relations and higher-order relations.
It transfers candidate predicates under object
correspondences, favors relational systems and
separates candidate generation from plausibility
or truth. Its arity distinctions are relative
to a psychologically chosen representation;
equivalent recodings are acknowledged.
It assumes predicate identity at a suitable
component level, a distinct convention from
Gick and Holyoak's semantic-transform discussion.

The literal-similarity, analogy, appearance,
anomaly and abstraction comparisons are all
included. The source treats them as continua.
Its exceptions to one-to-one mapping, the
qualification that object assignments may be
resolved during relational matching, and the
mixed nature of metaphors are retained.

The solar-system/atom discussion is presented
as the source's idealized representation, not
a current physical theory of atomic structure.
The water/heat example illustrates systematic
connections; no general guarantee of causal
or physical truth is inferred from syntax.

The analogical-shift sequence is a conjecture,
illustrated by one informal learner observation.
The empirical-support section refers to
Gentner 1980b and other studies without giving
their full designs or numeric results. Those
summaries are not counted as new complete
primary experiments in this chapter.

## Formal benchmark inventory

Each suffix has body label thm:p19-SUFFIX and
annex label annex:p19.SUFFIX.

- analogy-structural-map: existence and uniqueness
  of a free typed expression substitution,
  preservation of positions/sorts/nesting and
  repeated object identity, and injectivity when
  the object correspondence is injective.
- analogy-threshold-transfer: exact condition
  kh>=q, minimum route count, positive scaling,
  a numerical feasible allocation and failures
  after changing route count or aggregation.
- analogy-candidate-truth: explicit target
  structures with the same known relations
  and object map but opposite truth of a
  further binary relation; a higher-order
  expression's syntax does not fix its
  substantive interpretation.

These are declared handbook constructions.
They are not claimed participant representations,
the unique Gentner algorithm, or proofs of
the empirical response rates.
