## Exact source language, model, and temporal convention

### Informal clauses

The introductory time sequence is

\[
\sigma=s_0,s_1,\ldots .
\]

A classical state formula is evaluated in the state \(s_i\). The four temporal
operators are introduced with these truth conditions:

\[
\begin{aligned}
M,i\models Fw
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists j\,(j>i\land M,j\models w),\\
M,i\models Gw
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall j\,(j>i\rightarrow M,j\models w),\\
M,i\models Xw
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
M,i+1\models w,\\
M,i\models w_1Uw_2
&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists j\bigl(j>i\land M,j\models w_2\land
\forall k(i<k<j\rightarrow M,k\models w_1)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus `F` and `G` exclude the current point. The witness for \(w_1Uw_2\) is
strictly later; \(w_1\) is required only at the open interval between the
evaluation point and the witness, not at either endpoint. The source explicitly
draws \(w_1Uw_2\supset Fw_2\). This is a strict, witness-requiring `U`, not the
later inclusive strong-until convention. [Printed p. 163; PDF p. 1.]

### Formal syntax and models

The formal language \(L(F,X,U)\) has atoms
\(q_0,q_1,q_2,\ldots\), classical connectives
\(\neg,\land,\lor,\supset\), and temporal connectives \(F,X,U\). At this point
`G` is derived by

\[
Gw:=\neg F\neg w.
\]

A model is

\[
M=(M,<,Q_0,Q_1,Q_2,\ldots),
\]

where \((M,<)\) is linearly ordered and \(Q_i\subseteq M\). The atomic and
Boolean clauses are the expected ones:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\lVert q_i\rVert_m&=\mathsf{true}\iff m\in Q_i,\\
\lVert\neg w\rVert_m&=\mathsf{true}\iff
  \lVert w\rVert_m=\mathsf{false},\\
\lVert w_1\supset w_2\rVert_m&=\mathsf{true}\iff
  \lVert w_1\rVert_m=\mathsf{false}\ \text{or}\
  \lVert w_2\rVert_m=\mathsf{true}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Clause (3) says that \(\lVert Xw\rVert_m=\mathsf{true}\) when \(m\) has an
immediate successor \(m'\in M\) and \(\lVert w\rVert_{m'}=\mathsf{true}\).
It does not separately print an `iff` or state the no-successor case. The
natural two-valued completion makes `Xw` false when no such successor exists,
but that completion must be disclosed when arbitrary orders or maximal points
matter. [Printed p. 164, clause (3); PDF p. 2.]

Clause (5) formally agrees with the informal strict `U` clause:

\[
\lVert w_1Uw_2\rVert_m=\mathsf{true}
\]

if some \(m_2>m\) satisfies \(w_2\) and every \(m_1\) with
\(m<m_1<m_2\) satisfies \(w_1\). [Printed p. 164, clause (5); PDF p. 2.]

Clause (4), however, literally prints

\[
\lVert Fw\rVert_m=\mathsf{true}
\quad\text{iff}\quad
\exists m_1<m\;\lVert w\rVert_{m_1}=\mathsf{true}.
\]

That inequality points into the past. It conflicts with the informal
\(j>i\) definition on p. 163, clause (5),
\(Fw\equiv(\mathsf{true}\ U\ w)\) on
p. 164, all later uses of `F`, and the p. 172 witness construction. The intended
formal clause is therefore plainly \(m<m_1\), but the source record must retain
that this is an editorial repair of a printed direction error. [Printed p. 164,
clause (4); PDF p. 2; compare printed pp. 163, 164, and 172.]

An omega-model has \((M,<)=(\omega,<)\) with the usual order. A formula is
omega-valid when it is true at every \(n\in\omega\) in every omega-model.
[Printed p. 164; PDF p. 2.]

### `L(U)` and exact eliminations

On p. 164 the authors define \(L(U)\) syntactically as the sublanguage obtained
from \(L(X,F,U)\) by omitting `X` and `F`, then give

\[
Fw\equiv(\mathsf{true}\ U\ w),
\qquad
Xw\equiv(\mathsf{false}\ U\ w).
\]

The second identity works because `U` is strict: the open interval can contain
no point satisfying `false`, so on a discrete order the witness must be the
immediate successor. Later the paper writes \(L(U)=L(F,X,U)\). That is an
expressive-equivalence convention, not literal equality of the grammars just
defined. [Printed pp. 164--165 and 168--169; PDF pp. 2--3 and 6--7.]

These identities are semantically valid on every linear order under the
intended strict clauses when `X` is read as existence of a satisfying immediate
successor: in a dense interval \(\mathsf{false}\ U\ w\) is false, as is `Xw`;
at a maximal point both are false under the disclosed two-valued completion.

## Program and execution-sequence frame

Programs are treated as generators of execution sequences. In the
nondeterministic case an input generates a set of possible sequences. A
sequence \(\sigma=s_0,s_1,\ldots\) begins with an input-bearing initial state;
\(s_{i+1}\) is obtained by executing the appropriate instruction at \(s_i\).
The program location is included in the state so that the applicable
instruction or set of instructions is identifiable. Claims about a program
are claims holding over all of its generated execution sequences. [Printed p.
164; PDF p. 2.]

For program labels \(\ell_0,\ldots,\ell_e\), the program-state proposition
\(at_{\ell_i}\) says that the instruction at \(\ell_i\) is about to execute.
Let \(\bar y\) be the tuple of current program-variable values and let
\(\bar\xi\) be a same-arity tuple of logical variables retaining the input
values; \(\varphi(\bar\xi)\) is the precondition and
\(\psi(\bar\xi,\bar y)\) the postcondition. These are atemporal program-state
predicates, value tuples, and formula metavariables inside the temporal
formula, not additional temporal connectives. The partial-correctness formula
is

\[
(at_{\ell_0}\land\bar y=\bar\xi\land\varphi(\bar\xi))
\supset
G(at_{\ell_e}\supset\psi(\bar\xi,\bar y)).
\]

It guarantees the postcondition only if the exit is reached; the source
explicitly says it does not guarantee termination. [Printed pp. 164--165; PDF
pp. 2--3.]

The total-correctness formula is

\[
(at_{\ell_0}\land\bar y=\bar\xi\land\varphi(\bar\xi))
\supset
F(at_{\ell_e}\land\psi(\bar\xi,\bar y)).
\]

It asserts eventual correct termination from the stated initial condition.
[Printed p. 165; PDF p. 3.]

The paper's first classification is:

| Form | Source class and complete printed inventory |
|---|---|
| \(p\supset Gq\) | **Invariance**: Safety, Partial Correctness, Mutual Exclusion, Clean Performance, Deadlock Freedom |
| \(p\supset Fq\) | **Eventualities**: Liveness, Total Correctness, Accessibility, Livelock Freedom |

[Printed p. 165; PDF p. 3.]

## Complete inventory of source examples, pp. 166--168

### Response to Insistence

The weakest response condition is

\[
Gp\supset Fq,
\]

or, imposed throughout the future,

\[
G(Gp\supset Fq).
\]

The first formula is given the equivalent form

\[
\neg G(p\land\neg q).
\]

Because `G` is strict here, local \(Gp\) requires \(p\) at every point strictly
later than the current reference point; it is not the eventual-permanence
condition \(FGp\). The outer `G` in the globally imposed version repeats the
local obligation at every strictly later reference point.

This formulation avoids the interpretive problem that the response `q` may
cause the request `p` to cease holding. [Printed p. 166; PDF p. 4.]

For the busy-wait fragment

```text
m: if x=0 then go to m
```

the source gives

\[
\neg G(at_m\land x>0).
\]

It says the process cannot remain at `m` while `x` stays positive. The paper
calls this the weakest acceptable semaphore behaviour and calls the
corresponding scheduling assumption the minimal fairness requirement: every
process is eventually scheduled. For semaphores, this condition still permits
infinite overtaking. [Printed p. 166; PDF p. 4.]

### Response to Persistence

The stronger condition is

\[
GFp\supset Fq,
\]

or globally

\[
G(GFp\supset Fq).
\]

The first is given the equivalent form

\[
\neg G(Fp\land\neg q).
\]

Here `p` need not hold continuously; recurring infinitely often suffices.
[Printed pp. 166--167; PDF pp. 4--5.]

For the semaphore instruction

```text
m: P(x)
```

the source gives

\[
\neg G(F(x>0)\land at_m).
\]

It explicitly notes that scheduling the process infinitely often is not by
itself sufficient: those scheduling instants could coincide exactly with
instants where \(x\le 0\). [Printed p. 167; PDF p. 5.]

For the conditional critical section

```text
m: with r when B do S.
```

the stronger requirement is

\[
\neg G(F(r>0\land B)\land at_m).
\]

The authors say it can be implemented using semaphores and a queue, or an
unbounded counter, but not using semaphores alone. Their weaker alternative is

\[
\neg G(F(r>0)\land at_m\land B).
\]

That version guarantees admission only when `B` is permanently true from some
point while the resource is free infinitely often; the paper says its
semaphore implementation is easy. [Printed p. 167; PDF p. 5.]

### Response to an Impulse

The strongest response form is

\[
p\supset Fq,
\qquad\text{or globally}\qquad
G(p\supset Fq).
\]

The paper says this is natural for total correctness, “sometimes” reasoning,
and causalities among internal events, but generally too strong for an
external signal that might occur only once. [Printed p. 167; PDF p. 5.]

### Absence of Unsolicited Responses

The source complements \(p\supset Fq\) by

\[
Fq\supset(\neg q\ U\ p).
\]

Under the source's strict `U`, if `q` occurs in the strict future then a
strict-future `p` witness occurs no later than the first such `q`: `q` is absent
at every strict intermediate point, while `p` and `q` may coincide at the
witness because \(\neg q\) is not required there. The source explicitly says
it is ignoring boundary effects. [Printed pp. 167--168; PDF pp. 5--6.]

### Strict fairness and FIFO

The paper defines

\[
\operatorname{Pr}(p_1,p_2)
:=
Fp_2\supset(\neg p_2\ U\ p_1).
\]

Its FIFO request/response condition is

\[
\operatorname{Pr}(p_1,p_2)
\supset
\operatorname{Pr}(q_1,q_2).
\]

For competing semaphore instructions `m: P(x)` and `n: P(x)`, with successor
locations \(m'\) and \(n'\), the source writes

\[
\operatorname{Pr}(at_m,at_n)
\supset
\operatorname{Pr}(at_{m'},at_{n'}).
\]

It offers this as a possible basic axiom for a particular FIFO semaphore
implementation, not as a consequence of the temporal logic alone. [Printed p.
168; PDF p. 6.]

The paper then warns that this is only a first approximation: a single
\(\operatorname{Pr}(p,q)\) constrains the first `q` only. The printed refinement
requiring a new `p` between successive `q` occurrences is

\[
\operatorname{Pr}(p,q)\land
G(q\supset\operatorname{Pr}(p,q)).
\]

Its reference points are the initial state and every strict-future state where
`q` holds. [Printed p. 168; PDF p. 6.]

No other worked temporal formula or model example occurs in the published
body. The later normal-form proof has one informal auxiliary example, recorded
below.

## Four questions and historical placement

The paper asks, in order:

1. whether \(L(F,X,U)\) is strictly more expressive than \(L(F,X)\), or `U`
   can be expressed from `F,X`;
2. whether \(L(U)=L(F,X,U)\) is expressively complete;
3. for an appropriate deductive system for \(L(F,X)\);
4. for an appropriate deductive system for \(L(F,X,U)\).

[Printed p. 168; PDF p. 6.]

The authors attribute the negative answer to Question 1 to Kamp 1968,
Chapter IV, Theorem 3; attribute the introduction of `U` and its past dual `S`
and the mixed expressive-completeness result to Kamp; and present as their own
contribution a simpler proof, isolation of the future fragment, its dual past
result, the separation of mixed \(L(S,U)\) into past and future components, and
the extension to arbitrary linear orders with gap operators. [Printed p. 169;
PDF p. 7.]

This citation requires a qualification. Kamp's literal Theorem IV.3 says that
`ISOLP` and `ISOLF` are not expressible over the integers from his specified
Boolean/one-place tense basis; it is not itself worded as “`U` is not
expressible from `F,X`.” GPSS compresses the additional reduction connecting
that isolation result to nondefinability of `U`; it does not print that
reduction here. [Kamp 1968, printed pp. 105--108; GPSS printed p. 169.]

The paper further says, with the source keys mapped above, that [P2] proposed
the system \(DX\) for \(L(X,F)\) and [MY] proposed \(DUX\) for
\(L(X,F,U)\), respectively, and that completeness was claimed there without
proofs. Section 3 promises an outline applying to both systems. These are
GPSS's reported external proposal and completeness claims; the external bodies
are not imported or independently verified here. This article must not be
described as the first statement of those systems or as containing a fully
printed proof inherited from the earlier works. [Printed p. 169; PDF p. 7.]

## First-order comparison language and order classes

The comparison language \(L_1\) is first-order logic with equality, binary
\(<\), and unary predicates \(q_0,q_1,q_2,\ldots\), interpreted over the same
structures

\[
M=\langle M,<,Q_0,Q_1,Q_2,\ldots\rangle.
\]

The paper defines:

- **complete**: every nonempty bounded subset of \(M\) has a least upper bound
  and a greatest lower bound;
- **discrete**: every nonmaximal point has an immediate successor and every
  nonminimal point has an immediate predecessor;
- **omega-model**: the order is the natural numbers with their usual order.

It gives the natural-number and integer orders as discrete and complete; the
real order as complete but not discrete; and the rational order as incomplete.
Endpoints are not excluded by either definition. [Printed p. 169; PDF p. 7.]

There is a natural temporal-to-first-order translation
\(\theta\mapsto\theta^*(x)\), truth-preserving in every linear model at every
point. “Expressive completeness” is the existence of a translation in the
other direction. The paper does not display the recursive clauses for the easy
temporal-to-first-order map. [Printed pp. 169--170; PDF pp. 7--8.]

## `Proof of 2.2 (outline)`: complete printed architecture

The source labels the passage exactly `Proof of 2.2 (outline)` and says it
follows [SS], identified on p. 173 as the forthcoming Shelah--Stavi manuscript

For each variable \(z\), let

\[

\]

where the sign denotes either \(q_i(z)\) or \(\neg q_i(z)\). For distinct
variables \(x,y\):

\[

\[
\begin{aligned}

\mid m\in\omega,\\

\end{aligned}

\[

\qquad
Aft(x)=Bet(x,\infty),
\qquad
Bef(x)=Bet(-\infty,x).

[Printed p. 170; PDF p. 8.]

\[
\pi\land\psi_1\land\cdots\land\psi_m,

where \(\pi\) fixes their relative order and every \(\psi_i\) belongs to

\[
Bef(x_1)\cup At(x_1)\cup Bet(x_1,x_2)\cup\cdots\cup

\end{aligned}

## Boundaries that must survive integration

### Kamp 1968: strict semantics, reversed operand order

Kamp's prefix operator uses its first operand at the strict-future witness and
its second operand throughout the open interval:

\[
\mathrm{UNTIL}(P_0,P_1;t_0)
\equiv
\exists t_1\bigl(t_0<t_1\land P_0(t_1)\land
\forall t_2((t_0<t_2\land t_2<t_1)\rightarrow P_1(t_2))\bigr).
\]

Consequently GPSS infix (A\,U\,B) is Kamp prefix
\(\mathrm{UNTIL}(B,A)\), not \(\mathrm{UNTIL}(A,B)\). Both are strict in time;
their argument order is opposite. [Kamp 1968, printed pp. 25--26; GPSS printed
pp. 163--164.]

Kamp's thesis concerns a two-sided language and order-class results that must
not be folded into the GPSS future-only theorem. GPSS Theorem 2.2 has only
future first-order inputs and discrete complete orders; its Theorem 2.3 is the
separation claim for mixed `S,U`; its Theorem 2.5 adds undefined gap operators
for arbitrary linear orders. [GPSS printed pp. 169--171.]

### Pnueli 1977 and the paper's own historical comparison

The paper itself supplies only the p. 173 equations above as its exact
comparison with [P1]. A source-faithful presentation may say GPSS attributes
the earlier inclusive operators to Pnueli's line of work. It may not attribute
GPSS's strict `U`, Theorems 2.1--2.5, or DX/DUX completeness backward to Pnueli
1977. [Printed p. 173; PDF p. 11.]

### Later inclusive strong-until LTL

The term `LTL` does not occur in the article. Its `U` is not the now-common
inclusive strong-until operation \(A\,U_{\mathrm{inc}}\,B\), under which `B`
may already hold at the current point and `A` holds from the current point up
to, but not necessarily at, the witness. In particular, the source identity

\[
Xw\equiv(\mathsf{false}\ U\ w)
\]

does not survive that substitution: with inclusive strong until,
\(\mathsf{false}\ U_{\mathrm{inc}} w\) reduces to `w` now, not `w` at the next
point. [GPSS printed pp. 163--164.]

Nor may a presentation silently claim that later inclusive `U,S` alone
recover `X` on the natural numbers. Duplicating one state in a temporal word
preserves every formula built from atoms, Boolean connectives, and inclusive
`U,S` at corresponding positions, by induction on formulas; it can change
`Xp`. Therefore no such formula defines `Xp` on all natural-number models.
Any shared expressive-completeness statement formulated with the later
inclusive operators needs explicit `X` (and `Y` for the past direction), or it
must retain the GPSS/Kamp strict operators and disclose the convention.

### Automata and complexity

No automaton, acceptance condition, temporal-to-automata translation,
omega-regular-language theorem, or model-checking result appears. The word
“Automata” occurs only inside the title of a venue in reference [MP]. The
finite quotient and ultimately periodic path on p. 172 are proof objects in a
canonical-model outline; recasting them as an automata construction would be a
later reconstruction requiring its own source and proof. [Printed pp. 172--173;
PDF pp. 10--11.]

Likewise, the decidability corollary is not a complexity theorem. The paper
does not state PSPACE, EXPTIME, an elementary bound, or any other named class.
No later LTL or automata complexity result may be cited as though it were
Theorem 3.2 or its corollary. [Printed p. 172; PDF p. 10.]
